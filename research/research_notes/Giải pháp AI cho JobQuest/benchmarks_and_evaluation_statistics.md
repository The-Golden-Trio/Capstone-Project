## 1. LLM-as-judge benchmarks (AI-5, and the offline judge in AI-3)

### Takeaway
Most judge benchmarks test a *pairwise or binary correctness* decision on *model-written English* outputs; only BiGGen Bench, the Prometheus Feedback Collection / Feedback Bench and (partly) JUDGE-BENCH and FLAMe's task pool test ordinal rubric scoring against human raters, and none uses human-written short answers, Vietnamese, or occupational tasks. They can justify design choices (reference answers, rubric with per-level descriptions, panel) but cannot stand in for the project's own agreement study.

### Cited Findings

**JudgeBench** — Tan, Zhuang, Montgomery, Tang, Cuadron, Wang, Popa, Stoica, "JudgeBench: A Benchmark for Evaluating LLM-based Judges", ICLR 2025 (peer-reviewed), arXiv:2410.12784.
- Judged: pairwise, objective correctness (one correct and one incorrect response to the same question); each pair is judged twice with the order swapped and counts as correct only if the verdicts are consistent (a tie in one ordering is tolerated). Domains: knowledge (MMLU-Pro), reasoning and math (LiveBench), coding (LiveCodeBench). [P] — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.12784)
- Size: the paper reports 350 pairs (154 knowledge, 98 reasoning, 56 math, 42 coding) generated by GPT-4o [P]; the repository states "350 unique response pairs generated by GPT-4o and 270 unique response pairs generated by Claude-3.5-Sonnet" [A]. Language and licence are not stated on either page. — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.12784); [GitHub](https://github.com/ScalerLab/JudgeBench)
- Results in the paper [P]: fine-tuned judges are at or below chance-level usefulness (Prometheus2-8x7b 40.29, JudgeLM-33B 35.71, PandaLM 13.14); GPT-4o vanilla prompt 50.86, GPT-4o with the Arena-Hard prompt 56.57; Claude-3.5-Sonnet 64.29; o1-mini 65.71; o1-preview 75.43; o3-mini (medium) 76.57; o3-mini (high) 80.86; best reward model Skywork-Reward-Gemma-2-27B 64.29. — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.12784)
- Conflict with the earlier notes: `00_earlier_findings.md` records GPT-4o as 50.65; the value read here is 50.86 (vanilla) and 56.57 (Arena-Hard prompt). Re-check against the PDF before citing. — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.12784)
- Current leaderboard: hosted as a Hugging Face Space whose table is rendered dynamically; the tool could not read it, so the 2026 leaderboard state is **not verified**. — [HF Space](https://huggingface.co/spaces/ScalerLab/JudgeBench)
- Fit: no (pairwise, binary correctness, model-written, English). Use: evidence for the advisor's question 1 (judging accuracy tracks the judge's own reasoning ability when no reference is given) and that fine-tuned small judges fail on hard correctness.

**RewardBench** — Lambert, Pyatkin, Morrison, Miranda, Lin, Chandu, Dziri, Kumar, Zick, Choi, Smith, Hajishirzi, "RewardBench: Evaluating Reward Models for Language Modeling", arXiv:2403.13787 (the arXiv page lists no venue; treat as preprint).
- Judged: prompt–chosen–rejected trios (pairwise preference) across chat, reasoning and safety. Size not in the abstract. [A] — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.13787)
- A published result on it: FLAMe-RM-24B 87.8% vs GPT-4-0125 85.9% and GPT-4o 84.7% (from the FLAMe abstract). [A] — [arXiv:2407.10817](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.10817)
- Fit: no (pairwise, model outputs, English). Use: none beyond background; already in the report as [64].

**RewardBench 2** (exists) — Malik, Pyatkin, Land, Morrison, Smith, Hajishirzi, Lambert, "RewardBench 2: Advancing Reward Model Evaluation", ICLR 2026 per the arXiv journal reference (peer-reviewed), arXiv:2506.01937.
- Judged: best-of-four (one correct among four completions; random baseline 25%) on 1,865 newly sourced human prompts in six subsets: Factuality 475, Precise instruction following 160, Math 183, Safety 450, Focus 495, Ties 102 (Ties uses a weighted score for several equally valid answers). Code Apache-2.0; data stated as ODC-By. Language not stated. [P] — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.01937)
- Models score "about 20 points on average lower" than on RewardBench. [A] — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.01937)
- Top results in the paper's Table 3 [P]: Skywork-Reward-V2-Llama-3.1-8B 84.1; LMUnit-qwen2.5-72b 82.1; LMUnit-llama3.1-70b 80.5; PGRM 80.0; Gemini 2.5 Pro as judge 79.5; Gemini 2.5 Flash 77.7; Claude Opus 4 (20250514) 76.5; Claude 3.7 Sonnet 75.4. — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.01937)
- Fit: no (selection among model completions). Use: the "Ties" subset is a published precedent for checking that a judge does not invent distinctions between equally good answers, relevant to the project's bias probes.

**LLMBar** — Zeng, Yu, Gao, Meng, Goyal, Chen, "Evaluating Large Language Models at Evaluating Instruction Following", ICLR 2024 (peer-reviewed), arXiv:2310.07641.
- Judged: pairwise; which of two outputs follows the instruction. 419 instances: Natural 100; Adversarial 319 (Neighbor 134, GPTInst 92, GPTOut 47, Manual 46). English. CC BY 4.0 as shown on the page. Expert agreement 94% on a sample of 80 (90% Natural, 95% Adversarial). [P] — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.07641)
- Results [P]: best GPT-4 evaluator (Rules+Metrics+Reference) 96.0% on Natural and 82.8% on Adversarial; on Adversarial, ChatGPT vanilla 32.0% and LLaMA-2-70B-Chat 32.6% (below the 50% chance level), PaLM2 60.7%. — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.07641)
- Fit: no (pairwise, model outputs). Use: the adversarial set is the closest published model for the project's Stage A "variants built to mislead the judge" (outputs that sound better but do not satisfy the instruction), and it shows prompt strategy (rules, self-generated metrics, reference) matters by 10+ points.

**MT-Bench human judgments** — Zheng, Chiang, Sheng, Zhuang, Wu, Zhuang, Lin, Li, Li, Xing, Zhang, Gonzalez, Stoica, "Judging LLM-as-a-Judge with MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena", NeurIPS 2023 Datasets and Benchmarks (peer-reviewed), arXiv:2306.05685.
- Judged: pairwise (winner / tie) on 80 questions and 6 models; about 3.3K expert votes (graduate students); English; CC-BY-4.0; the card and abstract report over 80% agreement between GPT-4 and humans, "the same level" as between humans. [A] — [dataset card](https://huggingface.co/datasets/lmsys/mt_bench_human_judgments); [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.05685)
- Fit: no. Use: already in the report as [57]; historical baseline only.

**BiGGen Bench** — Kim et al. (32 authors, first author Seungone Kim), "The BiGGen Bench: A Principled Benchmark for Fine-grained Evaluation of Language Models with Language Models", NAACL 2025 main (peer-reviewed), arXiv:2406.05761.
- Judged: direct assessment on a 1–5 Likert scale with an *instance-specific rubric that describes every score level*; 765 instances, 77 tasks, 9 capabilities; 103 response models; 5 evaluator models. English plus a multilingual capability (annotators for Korean, Kazakh, Bengali, Spanish, Indonesian, German, French, Arabic, Russian, Thai; Vietnamese is not in the list read). Dataset licence stated as CC-BY-SA. [P] — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.05761)
- Human ratings: 3,236 human ratings over the 765 prompts (responses of 4 models, 6 for multilingual). Pearson correlation of evaluator with humans, averaged over capabilities [P]: GPT-4-Turbo-2024-04-09 0.623; majority vote of all five evaluators 0.627; GPT-4-1106 0.597; Claude-3-Opus 0.578; Prometheus-2-BGB 8x7B 0.577 (0.607 with self-consistency); Prometheus-2 8x7B 0.471. Weakest capabilities for GPT-4-Turbo: theory of mind 0.478, tool usage 0.551. — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.05761)
- Fit: partial. It is the closest published design to AI-5 (ordinal scale, rubric with per-level anchors, human raters, several judges) but the answers are model-written, the scale is 1–5, and agreement is reported as Pearson, not weighted kappa. Use: (i) precedent for per-item rubrics with a description per level; (ii) a published data point that a five-judge majority vote (0.627) was essentially equal to its best member (0.623), which bears directly on the project's panel-median decision.

**Feedback Collection / Feedback Bench (Prometheus)** — Kim et al., "Prometheus: Inducing Fine-grained Evaluation Capability in Language Models", ICLR 2024 (peer-reviewed), arXiv:2310.08491; and Kim, Suk, Longpre, Lin, Shin, Welleck, Neubig, Lee, Lee, Seo, "Prometheus 2: An Open Source Language Model Specialized in Evaluating Other Language Models", EMNLP 2024 main (peer-reviewed), arXiv:2405.01535.
- Feedback Collection: 1K score rubrics, 20K instructions, 100K responses with feedback (20K per score 1–5); all *generated by GPT-4*. Feedback Bench: seen-rubric subset (1K instructions) and unseen-rubric subset (1K instructions, 50 new rubrics; the page is internally inconsistent on whether these are hand-crafted). [P] — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.08491)
- Human check is small: 45 instances with customised rubrics; Pearson with human evaluators Prometheus 0.897, GPT-4 0.882, GPT-3.5-Turbo 0.392. A dataset licence is not stated on the page read. [P] — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.08491)
- Prometheus 2 is evaluated on four direct-assessment and four pairwise benchmarks and reports the highest correlation with humans among open evaluator models. [A] — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.01535)
- Fit: partial in form (1–5 rubric with reference answer), no in substance: labels are GPT-4's, not humans', so agreement on Feedback Bench measures agreement with GPT-4. Use: the input format (instruction, response, reference answer, rubric with level descriptions → feedback then score) is the published template closest to AI-5's "quote, rationale, level" output.

**JUDGE-BENCH** — Bavaresco et al. (20 authors), "LLMs instead of Human Judges? A Large Scale Empirical Study across 20 NLP Evaluation Tasks", ACL 2025 main (peer-reviewed), arXiv:2406.18403.
- 20 datasets with human annotations, 11 LLMs; conclusion: models are reliable on some tasks but vary widely with the property judged, the expertise of the human judges, and whether the text is human- or model-written; validate against humans before use. [A] — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.18403)
- Fit: partial (mixed categorical and graded human annotations; not rubric scoring of short answers). Use: a peer-reviewed citation for "validate the judge on your own task before trusting it".

**FLAMe** — Vu, Krishna, Alzubi, Tar, Faruqui, Sung, "Foundational Autoraters: Taming Large Language Models for Better Automatic Evaluation", arXiv:2407.10817 (no venue on the arXiv page; preprint).
- Trained on 100+ quality-assessment tasks with 5M+ human judgments; outperforms the proprietary judges considered on 8 of 12 autorater benchmarks (53 tasks). [A] — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.10817)
- Fit: no (weights not a hosted API; English). Use: evidence that training on human judgments, not model size, drives judge quality.

**Newer judge benchmarks (2026)**
- RubricEval — Pan, Lin, Yang, He, Chen, Qi, Xu, Feng, Xu, Xiao, "RubricEval: A Rubric-Level Meta-Evaluation Benchmark for LLM Judges in Instruction Following", arXiv:2603.25133 (preprint, 26 Mar 2026): 3,486 quality-controlled instances with Easy and Hard subsets; "even GPT-4o achieves only 55.97% on the Hard subset"; rubric-level beats checklist-level evaluation; explicit reasoning improves accuracy. [A] — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.25133)
- RuVerBench — Peng, Qi, Xia, He, Shi, Xuan, Lu, Liu, Hu, Liu, Peng, "Can LLM-as-a-Judge Reliably Verify Rubrics in Agentic Scenarios?", EMNLP 2026 (peer-reviewed), arXiv:2606.29920: 2,458 instances with human labels of whether an output satisfies one rubric (binary), deep research and agentic coding; frontier models strong "but still show substantial noise"; batched verification trades accuracy for efficiency; majority voting gives diminishing returns. No numbers in the abstract. [A] — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.29920)
- Fit of both: partial (rubric-item level, but binary satisfied / not satisfied, model outputs, English). Use: RuVerBench's finding on batched verification is the only source found that speaks to the project's "one call scores all competencies" decision; it reports a cost in accuracy.

### Inferences
- Benchmarks that test ordinal rubric scoring against human raters: BiGGen Bench (yes, 1–5, 3,236 human ratings), Prometheus human study (yes but n=45), JUDGE-BENCH and FLAMe (some of their constituent tasks). JudgeBench, RewardBench, RewardBench 2, LLMBar, MT-Bench, RubricEval and RuVerBench do not.
- No judge benchmark found uses a four-level scale with a negative level, Vietnamese text, or human-written occupational answers. The report should say that public judge benchmarks inform *design*, and that NFR-14 can only be checked on the project's own data.
- Two published results cut against untested assumptions in the current design and should be named as risks to measure: panel vote ≈ best single judge (BiGGen), and batched rubric verification loses accuracy (RuVerBench, abstract only).

### Gaps
- Current (Oct 2026) leaderboard states for JudgeBench and RewardBench / RewardBench 2 could not be read (dynamic Hugging Face Spaces).
- Licences of JudgeBench, the Feedback Collection and RewardBench data were not found on the pages opened.
- RAVEL (arXiv:2603.00686) and the meta-judging survey (arXiv:2601.17312) appeared in search results only [U].

## 2. Automated scoring of human-written answers (AI-5)

### Takeaway
On the standard English datasets, fine-tuned models approach or exceed human–human agreement (ASAP-AES about 0.79–0.82 QWK vs 0.75 human–human) while prompted LLMs sit far lower (about 0.3–0.5 average on ASAP-AES, 0.26–0.68 on ASAP-SAS), with single-prompt highs near 0.6–0.8. No public dataset of human-scored occupational free-text answers on an ordinal rubric was found; the nearest (Casper SJT) is private and shows LLM–human kappa mostly below human–human.

### Cited Findings

**ASAP-AES** (Hewlett Foundation, Kaggle 2012)
- 12,978 essays, 8 prompts; human–human QWK average .752 (per prompt .721, .812, .769, .850, .753, .775, .720, .620). [P, as reported by] Ormerod and Kwako, "Automated Text Scoring in the Age of Generative AI for the GPU-poor", arXiv:2407.01873 (preprint). — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.01873)
- Score ranges differ per prompt; the two sources read disagree for prompts 1, 7 and 8 (Ormerod and Kwako: 2–12, 1–6, 0–3, 0–3, 0–4, 0–4, 2–24, 10–60; Mansour et al.: 2–12, 1–6, 0–3, 0–3, 0–4, 0–4, 0–30, 0–60). — [arXiv:2407.01873](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.01873); contradicted by [arXiv:2403.06149](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.06149)
- Fine-tuned: NPCR (Xie et al. 2022) average QWK .817 cited as state of the art; fine-tuned Llama-3-8B .789, Phi-3-mini .786; fine-tuned GPT-3.5 .738. [P] — [arXiv:2407.01873](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.01873); also R2BERT 0.7940, fine-tuned GPT-3.5 0.7384 in [arXiv:2401.06431](https://arxiv.org/html/2401.06431)
- Prompted LLMs: GPT-4 zero-shot without rubric 0.3312, zero-shot with rubric 0.3437, few-shot with rubric 0.4743 average (set-level range 0.04–0.78) — Xiao, Ma, Song, Xu, Zhang, Wang, Fu, "Human-AI Collaborative Essay Scoring: A Dual-Process Framework with LLMs", arXiv:2401.06431 (preprint) [P]. ChatGPT (gpt-3.5-turbo-0301) average 0.313 and Llama-2-13b 0.297 with the best prompt per task — Mansour, Albatarni, Eltanbouly, Elsayed, "Can Large Language Models Automatically Score Proficiency of Written Essays?", arXiv:2403.06149 (earlier notes give LREC-COLING 2024) [P]. Zero-shot Llama-3-8B .452, Phi-3-mini .402 [P]. — [arXiv:2401.06431](https://arxiv.org/html/2401.06431); [arXiv:2403.06149](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.06149); [arXiv:2407.01873](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.01873)
- The Kaggle page could not be read; licence **not verified**. — [Kaggle](https://www.kaggle.com/c/asap-aes)
- Fit: partial (ordinal, human-written, but long essays, English, school pupils). Use: the reference point for "QWK ≥ 0.70" and for the gap between prompted and fine-tuned scoring.

**ASAP-SAS** (Kaggle 2012)
- 17,043 responses, 10 items, grades 8 and 10; scales 0–3 (items 1, 2, 5, 6) and 0–2 (items 3, 4, 7–10); human–human QWK average .874. [P, as reported by Ormerod and Kwako] — [arXiv:2407.01873](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.01873)
- Fine-tuned / supervised: ensemble of pretrained LMs (Ormerod) .796; AutoSAS (Kumar et al.) .791; BERT-base .749; fine-tuned Llama-3-8B .753, Phi-3-mini .750. [P] — [arXiv:2407.01873](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.01873)
- Prompted: GPT-4 .677 (Jiang and Bosch, as cited by Ormerod and Kwako [S]); zero-shot Llama-3-8B .425, Phi-3-mini .332 [P]. — [arXiv:2407.01873](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.01873)
- Chamieh, Zesch, Giebermann, "LLMs in Short Answer Scoring: Limitations and Promise of Zero-Shot and Few-Shot Approaches", BEA 2024, pp. 309–315 (peer-reviewed workshop), read from the PDF [P]: on ASAP (5 of 10 tasks usable zero-shot), supervised BERT .74, SVM .46, fine-tuned GPT-3.5 .61; zero-shot GPT-4 .26, GPT-3.5 .19, LLaMA-70b .14; 3-shot GPT-4 .30, GPT-3.5 .22, LLaMA-70b .24. On Powergrading (0–1 scale, US citizenship questions) GPT-4 reaches .86 zero-shot vs BERT .94. GPT-3.5 got *worse* as shots increased. Answers in ASAP-SAS average about 50 words. — [ACL Anthology PDF](https://aclanthology.org/2024.bea-1.25.pdf)
- McGrane and Torres Irribarra, "How Much Training Data Is Enough? Fine-Tuning LLMs for Short-Answer Scoring", AIME-Con 2026 Works in Progress, pp. 306–314: open-weight models met operational criteria on seven of ten items; median 50 responses per score point among passing items. The dataset is not named on the page. [A] — [ACL Anthology](https://aclanthology.org/2026.aimecon-wip.39/)
- Fit: best available public match in *form* (short answers of about 50 words, 3–4 ordinal levels, analytic rubric, two human raters) but English, school science/reading. Use: the nearest external sanity check for the AI-5 prompt; human–human .874 shows how high expert agreement can be on short constrained answers.

**ASAP 2.0**
- Crossley, "ASAP 2.0: A large-scale corpus for assessing source-based writing quality", Zenodo preprint, 31 Jan 2025, doi:10.5281/zenodo.14781349: 24,278 source-based persuasive essays, 7 prompts, US grades 6, 8, 9, 10, with demographic fields (ELL status, economic background, disability, race/ethnicity, gender); CC BY 4.0. Score scale and inter-rater reliability are not on the page. [A] — [Zenodo](https://zenodo.org/records/14781349); [GitHub](https://github.com/scrosseye/ASAP_2.0)
- Other papers describe the Kaggle release as 17,307 essays scored 1–6 [U, search snippet]; the two figures refer to different releases and were not reconciled.
- Prompted LLMs: "strong open-weight models" reach QWK "about 0.6" on holistic scoring; large stable negative bias on grammar/conventions traits; concise prompts beat long rubric-style prompts for analytic traits; bias can be detected and corrected with small human-labelled sets — Kucia, Chakraborty, Wróblewska, "LLM Essay Scoring Under Holistic and Analytic Rubrics: Prompt Effects and Bias", ICCS 2026, LNCS 16789, pp. 531–546 (peer-reviewed), arXiv:2604.00259. [A] — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.00259)
- Fit: partial (ordinal 1–6, human-written; essays, English). Use: the only large public set with demographic fields, hence the model for the project's fairness check (SMD ≤ 0.15 by subgroup).

**SemEval-2013 Task 7 (Beetle, SciEntsBank)** — Dzikovska, Nielsen, Brew, Leacock, Giampiccolo, Bentivogli, Clark, Dagan, Dang, "SemEval-2013 Task 7: The Joint Student Response Analysis and 8th Recognizing Textual Entailment Challenge", SemEval 2013, pp. 263–274 (peer-reviewed); read from the PDF [P].
- Labels are *categorical, not ordinal*: 5-way (correct, partially_correct_incomplete, contradictory, irrelevant, non_domain), collapsed to 3-way and 2-way. Beetle: 56 questions, about 3,000 answers (basic electricity). SciEntsBank: about 10,000 answers to 197 questions in 15 science domains. Answers are 1–2 sentences, English, with a reference answer per question. Test sets: unseen answers, unseen questions, unseen domains. Nine teams. — [ACL Anthology PDF](https://aclanthology.org/S13-2045.pdf)
- Best 5-way macro-F1: Beetle 0.619 (unseen answers) and 0.552 (unseen questions); SciEntsBank 4-way 0.581 / 0.384 / 0.375 (unseen answers / questions / domains). Performance drops sharply on unseen questions and domains. Human–human kappa is not reported in the pages read. — [ACL Anthology PDF](https://aclanthology.org/S13-2045.pdf)
- Fit: partial (short answers with reference answers; but nominal labels, English, science). Use: the "unseen question / unseen domain" split is the published model for testing whether a scorer generalises to a new scenario or occupation (NFR-10); no QWK applies.

**Mohler dataset** — Mohler, Bunescu, Mihalcea, "Learning to Grade Short Answer Questions using Semantic Similarity Measures and Dependency Graph Alignments", ACL 2011, pp. 752–762 (peer-reviewed); read from the PDF [P].
- 80 questions, 2,273 answers from 31 undergraduates in a Data Structures course (University of North Texas); integer scale 0–5; two graders (the course TA and an author), gold = their average; graders gave the same grade 57.7% of the time and differed by one point 22.9% of the time; no explicit grading instructions were given; mean grade 4.43 vs 3.94 (one grader systematically more generous); "the dataset is biased towards correct answers". — [ACL Anthology PDF](https://aclanthology.org/P11-1076.pdf)
- Fit: the closest public dataset in *domain* (computer-science short answers, 0–5 ordinal, two human raters), but English, student knowledge questions, and no rubric. Use: a small, IT-domain external check; it also documents exactly the skew and rater-severity problems Section 7 deals with. Best QWK for fine-tuned and prompted LLMs was **not found** (the literature on it mostly reports Pearson r and RMSE).

**TOEFL11** — Blanchard, Tetreault, Higgins, Cahill, Chodorow, "TOEFL11: A Corpus of Non-Native English", ETS Research Report Series 2013(2), doi:10.1002/j.2333-8504.2013.tb02331.x (technical report).
- Only the metadata record was opened: a corpus of non-native English writing intended for native-language identification, error detection and essay scoring. Size, score levels and licence were not in the record. [A] — [Crossref record](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1002/j.2333-8504.2013.tb02331.x)
- Fit: partial (three coarse proficiency levels per the literature, long essays, learners of English). Use: the dataset behind Yoshida 2025 (already in the earlier notes: reasoning models were worse than GPT-4o mini).

**Professional, workplace and situational-judgment answers**
- Walsh, Ivan, Iqbal, Robb (Acuity Insights), "Using LLMs to identify features of personal and professional skills in an open-response situational judgment test", arXiv:2507.13881 (preprint): 162 typed Casper responses (6 scenarios × 27); human holistic scores on a 1–9 norm-referenced scale; two humans coded seven features with quadratic-weighted kappa 0.356–0.788 (INT 0.700, JUST 0.788, PERSP 0.722, VAGUE 0.356); zero-shot LLM–human kappa (average with the two humans) ranged from about 0.05 to 0.66 across five models; adding level descriptions raised o4-mini by about 0.08–0.10 on most features (0.206 on one). Data availability not stated. [P] — [arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.13881v1)
- Walsh and Ivan, "Measuring What Matters -- or What's Convenient?: Robustness of LLM-Based Scoring Systems to Construct-Irrelevant Factors", arXiv:2603.25674; short version accepted at AIED 2026: an LLM scorer for short SJT essays was robust to padding, spelling errors and writing sophistication; duplicated passages lowered scores; off-topic responses were heavily penalised. [A] — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.25674)
- Maity, Deroy, Sarkar, "Towards Smarter Hiring: Are Zero-Shot and Few-Shot Pre-trained LLMs Ready for HR Spoken Interview Transcript Analysis?", arXiv:2504.05683 (preprint): HURIT, 3,890 HR mock-interview transcripts; GPT-4 Turbo and GPT-3.5 Turbo gave scores "comparable to expert human evaluators" but often failed to identify errors and give actionable advice; authors recommend human-in-the-loop. [A] — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.05683)
- Fit: Casper is the closest in construct (written responses to work-like dilemmas, human raters, weighted kappa) but is private, English, and scored on features, not a per-competency rubric. Use: the best available citation that (i) even trained humans reach only 0.36–0.79 weighted kappa on such features and (ii) level descriptions in the prompt help.

### Inferences
- Best published QWK, summarised: ASAP-AES fine-tuned ≈ .79–.82 vs prompted ≈ .31–.47 average (human–human .752); ASAP-SAS supervised ≈ .75–.80 vs prompted GPT-4 ≈ .26–.68 depending on the study and task subset (human–human .874). The prompted figures are for 2023–2024 models; no all-prompt result for 2025–2026 frontier models was verified.
- NFR-14 (QWK ≥ 0.70) is above every verified *prompted* average on these datasets. It is defensible only because AI-5 differs from those baselines (expert reference answer, behavioural anchors per level, scored examples, panel), and that difference is itself an untested claim. The report should state this and treat ASAP-SAS and Mohler as cheap external pre-checks of the AI-5 prompt before any expert time is spent.
- The human ceiling varies a lot by task: .874 (ASAP-SAS), .752 (ASAP-AES), 57.7% exact agreement (Mohler, no rubric), 0.36–0.79 (Casper features). "At most 0.10 below expert–expert" therefore needs the project's own expert–expert figure; it cannot be borrowed.

## 6. Elicitation and structuring (AI-1, AI-2)

### Takeaway
There is one evaluation environment for LLM interviewers (ReqElicitGym, software requirements, simulated users; LLMs elicit under half of implicit requirements) and one benchmark for schema-constrained extraction (ExtractBench, PDFs; frontier models fail outright on very wide schemas). Nothing covers expert job-analysis interviews, interview transcripts as the extraction source, or Vietnamese.

### Cited Findings
- **ReqElicitGym** — Jin, Jin, Fang, Li, Yang, He, Chen, "ReqElicitGym: An Evaluation Environment for Interview Competence in Conversational Requirements Elicitation", arXiv:2602.18306 (preprint, 20 Feb 2026): 101 website-requirements scenarios in 10 application types, an "oracle user" simulator and a task evaluator (both reported to agree highly with real users and experts); seven LLMs elicit "less than half of the users' implicit requirements"; effective questions tend to come in later turns; style requirements are consistently missed. [A] — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.18306)
- **Follow-up questions in elicitation interviews** — Shen, Singhal, Breaux, "Requirements Elicitation Follow-Up Question Generation", IEEE RE 2025 (peer-reviewed), arXiv:2507.02858: in two controlled experiments GPT-4o follow-ups were "no worse than" human-authored ones on clarity, relevancy and informativeness, and better when generation was guided by a list of common interviewer mistake types. [A] — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.02858)
- **ExtractBench** — Ferguson, Pennington, Beghian, Mohan, Kiela, Agrawal, Nguyen, "ExtractBench: A Benchmark and Evaluation Methodology for Complex Structured Extraction", arXiv:2602.12247 (preprint, Feb 2026): 35 PDFs paired with JSON Schemas and human gold labels, 12,867 evaluable fields; each field declares its own scoring rule (exact match for identifiers, tolerance for quantities, semantic equivalence for names), array items are aligned, and omissions are separated from hallucinated values; frontier models are unreliable and degrade with schema breadth, with "0% valid output on a 369-field financial reporting schema across all tested models". [A] — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.12247)
- LLMREI (33 simulated interviews) and the Chopra–Haaland and Wuttke et al. interviewer studies are already in the earlier notes and were not re-opened.
- Fit: partial for both. ReqElicitGym: interview competence, but the "expert" is a simulated lay user with hidden requirements, not a senior practitioner recalling incidents. ExtractBench: schema-constrained extraction, but from PDFs, not transcripts. Uses: ReqElicitGym's design (hidden ground-truth list + simulated respondent + coverage score) is the template for an AI-1 test harness; ExtractBench's per-field scoring rules and omission-vs-invention split are the template for AI-2's seed-extraction metric; its breadth result argues for filling the seed section by section instead of in one call.

### Inferences
- AI-1 can be tested in Stage A with a ReqElicitGym-style harness: write a hidden "incident sheet" per role, have a simulated expert answer only what is asked, and report the share of sheet items elicited and the interviewer-mistake rate. This shows the machinery works; it does not show real experts will respond the same way.
- AI-2 can be tested with a small hand-built gold set: transcripts (team-written) paired with a gold seed; report per-field precision / recall with field-type-specific matching, and count invented fields separately, since inventions in keys or anchors are the costly error.

### Gaps
- No benchmark for LLM-led expert elicitation, critical-incident or job-analysis interviews was found.
- No benchmark for schema-constrained extraction from interview transcripts outside clinical dialogue was verified; MEDIQA-SYNUR (nurse–patient transcripts, arXiv:2605.15467), a second "ExtractBench" (370 enterprise documents), AOE (arXiv:2507.16271) and C-LEIA appeared in search only [U].

## 7. Statistics for the project's own benchmark

### Takeaway
Plan the number of items from a confidence-interval width, not from a significance test: with the Bonett approximation, estimating an agreement of about 0.70–0.80 to within ±0.10 between two raters needs on the order of 50–100 items, and ±0.15 about 25–50. Report QWK with a resampling interval together with the full confusion matrix, exact and adjacent agreement, a bias (mean-difference) index and the score distribution, because QWK moves with prevalence and scale even when raw agreement does not.

### Cited Findings

**Sample size for kappa and ICC**
- Flack, Afifi, Lachenbruch, Schouten, "Sample Size Determinations for the Two Rater Kappa Statistic", Psychometrika 53(3):321–325, 1988, doi:10.1007/BF02294215 (peer-reviewed): a method to choose the sample size that achieves a prespecified bound on the confidence-interval width of kappa for two raters; also usable for power. [A, Crossref record] — [Crossref](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1007/BF02294215)
- Rotondi and Donner, "A confidence interval approach to sample size estimation for interobserver agreement studies with multiple raters and outcomes", Journal of Clinical Epidemiology 65(7):778–784, 2012, doi:10.1016/j.jclinepi.2011.10.019 (peer-reviewed): sample size for prespecified lower and upper confidence limits of kappa, any number of raters and outcome categories; R package `kappaSize`. [A, Europe PMC record] — [Europe PMC](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=TITLE%3A%22confidence%20interval%20approach%20to%20sample%20size%20estimation%20for%20interobserver%20agreement%22&format=json&resultType=core)
- `kappaSize` v1.2 provides `CI4Cats(kappa0, kappaL, kappaU, props, raters = 2..6, alpha)` for four outcome categories; it needs the anticipated prevalence of each category and warns when an expected cell count is below 5. The documentation does not say the kappa is weighted. [S] — [CRAN docs](https://search.r-project.org/CRAN/refmans/kappaSize/html/CI4Cats.html)
- Bonett, "Sample size requirements for estimating intraclass correlations with desired precision", Statistics in Medicine 21(9):1331–1335, 2002, doi:10.1002/sim.1108 (peer-reviewed): approximate number of subjects for a confidence interval of desired width for ICCs in one-way and two-way ANOVA models; "shown to be very accurate". [A, Europe PMC record] — [Europe PMC](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=TITLE%3A%22Sample%20size%20requirements%20for%20estimating%20intraclass%20correlations%20with%20desired%20precision%22&format=json&resultType=core)
- The formula as restated by a secondary source: n = 1 + 8·z²·(1−ρ)²·[1+(k−1)ρ]² / [k(k−1)·w²]. The R package `presize::prec_icc` implements "formula 3 in Bonett (2002)", reproduces Bonett's example (4 raters, ICC 0.85, confidence *width* 0.2 → 19.2), and for k = 2 with ICC ≥ 0.7 adds 5ρ to n. The secondary source calls w a half-width, but the formula reproduces 19.2 only when w is the *full* width, which matches `presize`. The paper itself was not opened. [S] — [presize docs](https://search.r-project.org/CRAN/refmans/presize/html/prec_icc.html); [MetricGate](https://metricgate.com/docs/sample-size-icc-multiple-raters/)
- Fleiss and Cohen, "The Equivalence of Weighted Kappa and the Intraclass Correlation Coefficient as Measures of Reliability", Educational and Psychological Measurement 33(3):613–619, 1973, doi:10.1177/001316447303300309 (peer-reviewed): title and metadata only; this is the standard citation for treating quadratic weighted kappa as an ICC. [A, Crossref record; content not read] — [Crossref](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1177/001316447303300309)
- Sim and Wright, "The kappa statistic in reliability studies: use, interpretation, and sample size requirements", Physical Therapy 85(3):257–268, 2005, doi:10.1093/ptj/85.3.257 (peer-reviewed): tutorial covering weighted and unweighted kappa, the effect of prevalence and bias, confidence intervals and sample sizes. [A, Europe PMC record] — [Europe PMC](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=TITLE%3A%22kappa%20statistic%20in%20reliability%20studies%22&format=json&resultType=core)

**The kappa paradox and what to report alongside**
- Feinstein and Cicchetti, "High agreement but low kappa: I. The problems of two paradoxes", J Clin Epidemiol 43(6):543–549, 1990, doi:10.1016/0895-4356(90)90158-L: with imbalanced marginal totals a high observed agreement yields a low kappa; kappa can also be higher under asymmetric than symmetric imbalance. Part II (Cicchetti and Feinstein, 43(6):551–558, doi:10.1016/0895-4356(90)90159-M): report kappa together with separate indexes of agreement per category (positive and negative agreement). [A, Europe PMC records] — [Europe PMC](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=TITLE%3A%22High%20agreement%20but%20low%20kappa%22&format=json&resultType=core)
- Byrt, Bishop, Carlin, "Bias, prevalence and kappa", J Clin Epidemiol 46(5):423–429, 1993, doi:10.1016/0895-4356(93)90018-V: kappa depends on observer bias and on prevalence as well as agreement; defines a bias index and a prevalence index and recommends reporting them with kappa. **Krippendorff's alpha (ordinal, few raters) and interval estimation**
- Zapf, Castell, Morawietz, Karch, "Measuring inter-rater reliability for nominal data – which coefficients and confidence intervals are appropriate?", BMC Medical Research Methodology 16:93, 2016, doi:10.1186/s12874-016-0200-9 (peer-reviewed, open access): point estimates of Fleiss' K and Krippendorff's alpha did not differ in any simulated scenario; the asymptotic interval for Fleiss' K "should not be used" (low coverage); bootstrap intervals had coverage close to nominal; with data missing completely at random, alpha stays stable while complete-case Fleiss' K is biased; alpha is recommended for missing data or for scales above nominal. ### Inferences
- **Worked sample sizes (my arithmetic with the Bonett approximation as restated above, 95% level, w = full interval width; QWK treated as an ICC; to be re-run with `presize::prec_icc` before quoting).** Two raters (judge vs expert consensus): ρ = 0.70 → about 101 items for width 0.20 (±0.10) and about 46 for width 0.30 (±0.15); ρ = 0.80 → about 51 and 24; ρ = 0.60 → about 159 for width 0.20. `presize` adds 5ρ (3–4 items) for two raters when ρ ≥ 0.7. Three raters (expert–expert–expert): ρ = 0.70 → about 68 for width 0.20 and 31 for width 0.30; ρ = 0.80 → about 36 for width 0.20. These are per competency and per language if each is to be reported separately.
- The approximation assumes roughly normal scores and balanced use of the scale. A four-level scale with most answers at 0 and +1 violates this, and Doewes et al. show QWK then drops at fixed raw agreement. Two consequences for design: (i) Stage A items should be *stratified* so every level of every competency has enough cases (the perturbation design allows this by construction); (ii) the final interval should come from resampling items, not from the planning formula. For the Stage B natural distribution, `kappaSize::CI4Cats` with the pilot's level prevalences is the matching planning tool.
- **Reporting set to adopt** (each element traceable to a source above): QWK with a 95% interval from resampling items; the full 4×4 confusion matrix; exact and adjacent (within one level) agreement; linear weighted kappa; per-level agreement; mean signed difference / SMD as the bias index; the distribution of expert and judge scores (prevalence); for the three experts and the three judges, Krippendorff's alpha with the ordinal metric, with a jackknife or bootstrap interval and a note that the two interval methods are disputed.
- With only three raters, pairwise QWK (three pairs) and one alpha are both reportable; alpha additionally tolerates an expert skipping items, which volunteer experts will do.
- Stage A construction, following the validated recipes: start from the expert reference answer; apply one targeted edit per variant aimed at one criterion and one target level (SummEdits / FBI style); have a human (advisor's one-fifth sample) confirm that the variant sits at the intended level *and that no other criterion moved* (Hu et al. show judges confuse criteria, so cross-criterion leakage is a metric, not just a nuisance); remove or relabel borderline variants; add LLMBar-style "sounds better but is worse" variants and CALM / Walsh-style construct-irrelevant probes (length, fluency, spelling, duplication, off-topic) whose expected score change is zero.

### Gaps
- No published perturbation benchmark was found in which a criterion is degraded to a *known ordinal level* (as opposed to "degraded or not"); the project's Stage A design appears to be an extension, and the report should say so.
- The Bonett formula and the QWK–ICC equivalence were not read from the primary papers; Krippendorff's own bootstrap algorithm and his 0.667 / 0.800 conventions (reported in a guide, González-Prieto et al., arXiv:2008.00977 / JSS 2023, whose abstract page only was opened) were not verified against his texts.
- No sample-size method specific to *quadratic weighted* kappa on a four-level scale was verified (Flack et al. and `kappaSize` address unweighted kappa). A small simulation with the project's expected level distribution would close this.
- No source was found on the minimum number of items for a stable Krippendorff's alpha with three raters; a "30 units" rule appeared in a search snippet with no primary source [U].
- Albatarni and Elsayed, "Quadratic Weighted Kappa Is Not Enough for Evaluating Automated Essay Scoring Models" (LREC 2026), which reportedly recommends Gwet's AC2 variants, returned 404 and is unverified [U].
