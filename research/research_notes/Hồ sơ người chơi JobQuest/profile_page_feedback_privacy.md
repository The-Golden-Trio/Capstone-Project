## 1. Barnum / Forer effect: original finding, replications, writing rules, a practical test

### Takeaway
People rate generic personality text as highly accurate, and more so when it is labelled "for you", is flattering, and comes from a trusted source; but when asked separately whether the text describes them *as a unique person*, they can tell generic from specific. That separation (accuracy versus uniqueness) is the basis for a usable test of AI-written summaries.

### Cited Findings
- Forer (1949) gave 39 introductory-psychology students an identical personality description built largely from a newsstand astrology book, one week after a questionnaire. Rated on a 0 (poor) to 5 (perfect) scale, mean accuracy was 4.3; nobody rated below 2 and only 5 rated below 4. Forer cautioned that such "personal validation" cannot distinguish one person from another and so says nothing about the validity of the instrument. **[secondary: as described in Dickson & Kelly 1985, pp. 367–368, which was read in full text]** — [Dickson & Kelly 1985](https://cortecs.org/wp-content/uploads/2014/12/the-barnum-effect-in-personality-assessment-a-review-of-the-literature.pdf). Original: Forer, B. R. (1949). The fallacy of personal validation: A classroom demonstration of gullibility. *Journal of Abnormal and Social Psychology, 44*, 118–123, [doi:10.1037/h0059240](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0059240) (not opened; popular sources give 4.26 rather than 4.3).
- Four kinds of Barnum statement (after Sundberg 1955), with the review's own examples: **vague** ("you enjoy a certain amount of change and variety in life"); **double-headed** ("you are generally cheerful and optimistic but get depressed at times"); **modal characteristics of the subject's group** ("you find that study is not always easy"); **favourable** ("you are forceful and well-liked by others"). **[full text, p. 367]** — [Dickson, D. H., & Kelly, I. W. (1985). The "Barnum effect" in personality assessment: A review of the literature. *Psychological Reports, 57*, 367–382](https://cortecs.org/wp-content/uploads/2014/12/the-barnum-effect-in-personality-assessment-a-review-of-the-literature.pdf).
- The review's summary conclusion: acceptance of Barnum profiles "depends on the relevance and favorability of the profile and to some extent on the type of assessment utilized". **[full text, summary p. 367]** — [Dickson & Kelly 1985](https://cortecs.org/wp-content/uploads/2014/12/the-barnum-effect-in-personality-assessment-a-review-of-the-literature.pdf).
- **"For you" label.** Telling subjects that a general description was derived "specifically for them" raised accuracy ratings compared with telling them it was "generally true of people" (Snyder & Larson 1972, replicated several times; one non-significant result in the same direction). **[full text, p. 369]** — [Dickson & Kelly 1985](https://cortecs.org/wp-content/uploads/2014/12/the-barnum-effect-in-personality-assessment-a-review-of-the-literature.pdf).
- **Favourability.** Favourable interpretations are accepted more readily than unfavourable ones in most studies; in Sundberg's data the most-accepted interpretations had five times as many favourable as unfavourable statements. **[full text, pp. 371–372]** — [Dickson & Kelly 1985](https://cortecs.org/wp-content/uploads/2014/12/the-barnum-effect-in-personality-assessment-a-review-of-the-literature.pdf).
- **Why it works is not pure gullibility.** The review argues people accept Barnum statements "because they do fit" (high base rate) and they have nothing to compare them with; people "often have no trouble distinguishing between true and false feedback of a specific nature" (Dana & Fouke 1979). **[full text, p. 369]** — [Dickson & Kelly 1985](https://cortecs.org/wp-content/uploads/2014/12/the-barnum-effect-in-personality-assessment-a-review-of-the-literature.pdf).
- **Accuracy versus uniqueness.** When asked "Does this interpretation describe you as a unique person?", subjects rated the generic text as accurate but *not* as a description of their unique personality, and recognised it could apply equally to classmates (Greene 1977). Harris & Greene (1984) found Barnum feedback was rated *more accurate but less individual* than genuine inventory-based feedback or deliberately inverted feedback. Subjects do not make this distinction when it is only implied by the "for you" label; they make it when asked directly. **[full text, pp. 370–371]** — [Dickson & Kelly 1985](https://cortecs.org/wp-content/uploads/2014/12/the-barnum-effect-in-personality-assessment-a-review-of-the-literature.pdf).
- **Suggestion.** Marks & Kammann (1980) noted students would even accept specific, inaccurate feedback as descriptive, "albeit not very highly", and concluded the effect relies heavily on suggestion. **[full text, p. 372]** — [Dickson & Kelly 1985](https://cortecs.org/wp-content/uploads/2014/12/the-barnum-effect-in-personality-assessment-a-review-of-the-literature.pdf).
- **Source of the description matters little.** Identical profiles attributed to a projective test, an interview, an objective test, graphology or a horoscope received broadly similar ratings (e.g. Snyder, Larsen & Bloom 1976: projective M = 4.54, graphology M = 3.52, horoscope M = 3.44, differences not significant); the review calls this evidence inconsistent. **[full text, pp. 372–373]** — [Dickson & Kelly 1985](https://cortecs.org/wp-content/uploads/2014/12/the-barnum-effect-in-personality-assessment-a-review-of-the-literature.pdf).
- **Feedback changes the reader.** After receiving feedback, subjects' faith in both the instrument and the interpreter increases; and subjects given an astrological profile describing them as high on dominance and change afterwards rated themselves higher on those qualities (Delaney & Woodyard 1974), which the review reads as a self-fulfilling prophecy "on an intellectual level". Interpreter status has little effect "except when dealing with negative feedback". **[full text, pp. 379–380]** — [Dickson & Kelly 1985](https://cortecs.org/wp-content/uploads/2014/12/the-barnum-effect-in-personality-assessment-a-review-of-the-literature.pdf).
- **Limits of the classic literature.** Most studies used university students; the review asks for work with the general public and other cultural groups. **[full text, p. 379]** — [Dickson & Kelly 1985](https://cortecs.org/wp-content/uploads/2014/12/the-barnum-effect-in-personality-assessment-a-review-of-the-literature.pdf).
- **Modern extension to AI.** In an experiment with 238 participants shown *fictitious* predictions about themselves attributed to AI, astrology or personality psychology, belief in the AI predictions correlated positively with belief in the astrology and personality predictions; paranormal beliefs and positive attitudes to AI raised perceived validity, reliability, usefulness and personalisation; cognitive style had no detectable effect. **[abstract] (preprint, arXiv v3 Dec 2024)** — [Lee, Pataranutaporn, Amores & Maes, "Super-intelligence or Superstition?", arXiv:2408.06602](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.06602v3).

### Inferences
- Writing rules that follow directly from the four statement types and the moderators: (1) no vague quantity words ("a certain amount", "sometimes", "at times"); (2) no double-headed sentences that assert a trait and its opposite; (3) no statement that is true of most people in the reader's group (for JobQuest: "you find studying is not always easy", "you want a job you like"); (4) no free-floating praise; (5) every sentence must be traceable to something this player said or did, because specific content is what readers can actually check.
- The label "written for you by AI" is itself an acceptance booster ("for you" + trusted source). The team's planned disclaimer "written by AI, may be wrong" works against that, but the Lee et al. result suggests belief is driven by intuition rather than careful reading, so the disclaimer alone should not be relied on.
- A practical **swap test** for generated summaries, built on Greene (1977) and Harris & Greene (1984):
  1. *Human version (small pilot, 15–30 players):* show each player their own summary plus two summaries written for other players (or one written from their inverted profile), unlabelled, in random order. Ask two separate questions per text: "How well does this describe you?" and "Could this have been written about most of your classmates?" Then ask which one is theirs. Pass if players pick their own well above chance (1 in 3) *and* rate it higher on "only me" than the decoys. High "describes me" with low "only me" is the Barnum signature.
  2. *Automated version (every release of the prompt):* for a batch of N players, give a judge model one summary and the inputs of several players, and ask which input it was written from. If matching accuracy is near chance, the summaries are generic.
  3. *Sentence-level check:* for each sentence, the generator must output the answer or play event it relies on; sentences with no evidence are dropped (the same rule Nam's spec already uses for trait values).
- The inverted-profile decoy also catches the opposite failure: if players accept the inverted summary as readily as the real one, the text carries no information.

### Gaps
- Forer's original paper was not opened; the figures above come from the Dickson & Kelly review. Check n, scale and mean against the DOI before quoting in the report.
- Pages 374–378 of Dickson & Kelly (subject and administrator variables, the list of propositions) were not read.
- A real-versus-inverted feedback study on a NEO summary (176 students; real feedback rated as more accurate, moderate effect) appeared in search results only; the journal page returned 404, so it is **not verified**. No peer-reviewed 2023–2026 study was found that measures the Barnum effect specifically for LLM-written personality summaries.
- No replication with secondary-school students or with Vietnamese participants was found.

## 2. How people receive personality, interest or strengths feedback

### Takeaway
Feedback helps on average but often backfires, and it backfires more when it pulls attention to the self ("what kind of person you are") rather than to the task ("what you did"). Adolescents react more strongly than adults to both positive and negative evaluative feedback. Evidence that self-reflection tools by themselves improve career decisions is thinner than the team may assume: the strongest career-intervention effects are tied to counsellor support, which JobQuest does not have.

### Cited Findings
- Meta-analysis of feedback interventions: 607 effect sizes, 23,663 observations; average improvement d = .41, but **over one third of feedback interventions decreased performance**, not explained by sampling error or by feedback sign. The authors' feedback intervention theory holds that effectiveness falls as attention moves up the hierarchy "closer to the self and away from the task". **[abstract]** — [Kluger, A. N., & DeNisi, A. (1996). *Psychological Bulletin, 119*(2), 254–284](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.119.2.254) (read via the [Hebrew University record](https://cris.huji.ac.il/en/publications/the-effects-of-feedback-interventions-on-performance-a-historical/)).
- Adolescents (13–18, M = 15.6, n = 120) showed larger state self-esteem gains after positive feedback and sharper drops after negative feedback than adults (25–40, n = 120) in a simulated social-media setting (Age × Valence interaction, p = 0.002); perceived authenticity of one's self-presentation buffered the effect of negative feedback. Caveats stated by the page: cross-sectional, simulated feedback, urban Chinese sample, single author. **[abstract]** — [Chen, Y.-H. (2025). *Frontiers in Psychology, 16*](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2025.1625771).
- Favourable descriptions are accepted more than unfavourable ones, and interpreter status matters mainly for negative feedback. **[full text, pp. 371–372, 379]** — [Dickson & Kelly 1985](https://cortecs.org/wp-content/uploads/2014/12/the-barnum-effect-in-personality-assessment-a-review-of-the-literature.pdf).
- Self-knowledge is uneven by trait: the self is predicted to be more accurate than others for low-observability traits (e.g. neuroticism), and others more accurate than the self for highly evaluative traits (e.g. intellect); tested with 165 participants rated by self, friends and strangers. **[secondary: abstract as shown in search results and a university press summary]** — [Vazire, S. (2010). Who knows what about a person? The self–other knowledge asymmetry (SOKA) model. *JPSP, 98*(2), 281–300](https://findanexpert.unimelb.edu.au/scholarlywork/1452513-who-knows-what-about-a-person).
- Career interventions: Whiston, Li, Mitts & Wright (2017, *Journal of Vocational Behavior, 100*, 175–184) is described as a replication of Brown & Ryan Krane (2000) covering 57 studies, with the largest effects on career decision-making self-efficacy, and with counsellor support, values clarification and psycho-educational interventions as the important ingredients. Brown & Ryan Krane's five original "critical ingredients" are reported as written exercises, individualised interpretations and feedback, world-of-work information, modelling, and support from social networks. **[secondary: practitioner and review summaries; the 2017 abstract could not be opened and the summaries are not fully consistent with each other]** — [CDAA evidence outline](https://cdaa.imiscloud.com/Web/Blog/Posts/What-is-best-practice-in-careers-education--An-outline-of-the-evidence.aspx); [Frontiers in Psychology 2024 systematic review](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2024.1461503/full); original at [doi:10.1016/j.jvb.2017.03.010](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jvb.2017.03.010).
- Narrative career work with young adolescents: a group intervention based on the *My Career Story* workbook (N = 108, groups assigned by convenience, not randomised) raised career adaptability and future orientation in the intervention group only; no change in hope, optimism or resilience. **[secondary: repository record seen in search results]** — [Santilli, Nota & Hartung (2018), *Journal of Vocational Behavior*](https://neomed.omeka.net/items/show/6391).

### Inferences
- "Describe what you did and prefer in work situations" is safer than "describe who you are". This fits Kluger & DeNisi's task-versus-self finding and matches the existing draft rule (how the person seems to like working). It argues against block B items such as "world view" or "psychology" appearing in the visible summary.
- The single "thing that may be hard" should be framed as a situation plus something to try, about a task, never about ability, intelligence or mental state. Adolescents' stronger reaction to negative evaluative feedback (Chen 2025) and the acceptance asymmetry (Dickson & Kelly) both point this way. This is strengths-led framing with one honest friction point, not a deficit list.
- Vazire's asymmetry suggests the profile should not ask players to rate themselves on evaluative qualities (smart, creative, good at X), and that behaviour during play is the natural complement to self-report for exactly those qualities.
- "Individualised interpretation and feedback" is one of the classic ingredients, which supports having a personal summary at all; but the 2017 emphasis on counsellor support means the report should not claim that an automated self-summary by itself improves career decisions.

### Gaps
- Whiston et al. (2017) was not read (Elsevier blocked; aggregator blocked). The figures "57 studies" and the three ingredients are secondary. Open the abstract before citing.
- No meta-analysis was found on self-concept clarity and career decision-making in adolescents. Search results surfaced a two-wave study of 1,342 emerging adults (Kvitkovičová, Umemura & Macek 2017, *JVB*) in which self-concept clarity mediated links to career decision-making, and a quasi-experimental study (Cardoso 2017) reporting no effect of a narrative intervention on career adaptability; neither was opened.
- No randomised trial was found comparing strengths-based with deficit-framed feedback from a personality or interest assessment in adolescents.
- No study was found on how adolescents react to *AI-written* descriptions of themselves in a career context.

## 3. Type label versus spectrum; showing uncertainty and "not enough data yet"

### Takeaway
The taxometric evidence says individual differences in personality are continuous, so type labels cut a continuum at an arbitrary point. Score-reporting guidance asks for precision to be shown in plain terms; a range (band) per indicator and an explicit "not enough data yet" state are the standard ways to do that.

### Cited Findings
- Meta-analysis of taxometric research: 317 findings from 183 articles using the comparative-fit index; dimensional findings outnumbered taxonic (category) findings five to one; no content domain showed aggregate support for the categorical model; normal personality variation was no more categorical than psychopathology. Conclusion: most psychological differences between people are latently continuous. **[abstract]** — [Haslam, N., McGrath, M. J., Viechtbauer, W., & Kuppens, P. (2020). Dimensions over categories. *Psychological Medicine, 50*(9), 1418–1432](https://doi.org/10.1017/S003329172000183X) (read via the [Maastricht University record](https://cris.maastrichtuniversity.nl/en/publications/dimensions-over-categories-a-meta-analysis-of-taxometric-research/)).
- Common weaknesses of score reports listed in the score-reporting literature (Goodman & Hambleton 2004): too much or too little information, **no information about score precision**, jargon, undefined terms, cluttered layout; and precision should be reported "in terms appropriate to the audience". **[secondary: search-result summary of a 2019 Frontiers in Education article; neither that article nor Goodman & Hambleton was opened]** — [Frontiers in Education 2019, 4:20](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2019.00020/pdf).
- Guidance on reading confidence bands: the more two bands overlap, the less meaningful the difference between the scores. **[secondary: search-result snippet of an AAMC score-interpretation document]** — [AAMC](https://aamc.org/media/35641/download).
- A description treated as authoritative shifts self-ratings toward it (Delaney & Woodyard 1974, in the review). **[full text, p. 380]** — [Dickson & Kelly 1985](https://cortecs.org/wp-content/uploads/2014/12/the-barnum-effect-in-personality-assessment-a-review-of-the-literature.pdf).

### Inferences
- The report's existing criticism of MBTI-style labels (Pittenger, already cited there) is strengthened by Haslam et al.: there is no evidence of natural "types" to label. Each indicator should be shown as a position between two named poles, with neither pole presented as the good one.
- Show a band, not a dot. With few answers or few play events the band is wide; it narrows as evidence accumulates. Two indicators whose bands overlap should not be described as different ("you prefer A over B") in the summary text.
- Below a minimum amount of evidence an axis should be shown as "chưa đủ dữ liệu" (greyed, with what would fill it: "play 2 more events of this kind"), instead of a midpoint value. A midpoint looks like a measurement ("you are average") when it is actually an absence of data.
- Because labels can become self-fulfilling (Delaney & Woodyard), avoiding nouns for people ("a planner", "the analyst") is a harm-reduction measure as well as a validity one.

### Gaps
- The AERA/APA/NCME *Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing* and Hambleton & Zenisky's score-report guidelines were not opened; no publisher guidance specific to personality feedback reports (e.g. from Hogrefe, PAR, The Myers-Briggs Company) was located and read.
- No empirical study was found comparing users' understanding of a labelled type against a spectrum display of the same result.
- No evidence was found for a specific minimum number of observations per axis; any threshold the team sets is a design decision to be stated as such.

## 4. Visualisation: radar charts, number of axes, alternatives

### Takeaway
In the two controlled comparisons found, the radar chart was the worst-performing and least-liked option. Bars (and, for a two-pole trait, a slider between poles) are the better default. No evidence was found for an optimal number of axes.

### Cited Findings
- Controlled experiment on composite-indicator displays comparing radar, "flower" (as in the OECD Better Life Index) and circle charts: "the Radar chart was the least effective and least liked"; the other two were task-dependent and participants strongly preferred the flower chart. The paper notes radar charts are widely used despite no prior evidence of effectiveness. **[abstract]** — [Albo, Y., Lanir, J., Bak, P., & Rafaeli, S. (2016). Off the Radar. *IEEE Transactions on Visualization and Computer Graphics, 22*(1), 569–578](https://doi.org/10.1109/TVCG.2015.2467322) (read via the [University of Haifa record](https://cris.haifa.ac.il/en/publications/off-the-radar-comparative-evaluation-of-radial-visualization-solu/)).
- Eye-tracking study (14 participants, two sessions, 16 questions per graph): bar and line graphs outperformed circular and radial graphs on effectiveness, efficiency and perceived ease of use; "the radar graph had the worst performance". Small sample. **[abstract]** — [Abeynayake, Goonetilleke, Wijeweera & Reischl (2023). *Applied Ergonomics, 109*, 103996](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.apergo.2023.103996).
- Crowd-sourced study with 92 non-expert users comparing radial and linear layouts for daily patterns: the plain linear bar chart was the most accurate, most efficient and preferred. (Radial charts, not radar charts specifically.) **[secondary: search-result summary]** — [Waldner et al. (2019), IEEE TVCG](https://www.cg.tuwien.ac.at/research/publications/2019/waldner-2019-rld/).
- The order of axes changes the shape of a star glyph and how people group and read it. **[secondary: search-result summary]** — [Miller et al. (2019), "Evaluating Ordering Strategies of Star Glyph Axes", IEEE VIS](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1908.00576).

### Inferences
- A radar polygon invites reading the *area* and *shape*, both of which depend on arbitrary axis order and on which axes sit next to each other; it also implies "bigger is better", which contradicts two-pole indicators where neither end is better. This is a reasoned criticism consistent with the axis-order finding, not a directly measured result.
- For two-pole work-style indicators the matching form is a horizontal slider per indicator: pole name on each side, a marker with a band, stacked vertically. This reads well on a phone (full-width rows, no rotation of labels) and makes "not enough data" easy to show per row.
- If the team keeps a "pentagon" for game feel, treat it as a decorative emblem with the sliders as the readable display: fixed axis order, no filled-area comparison between players or over time, at most five or six axes, and never the only representation.
- The current eight dimensions are too many for a readable radar on a phone; this is a design judgement (label crowding, small angular differences), not a cited threshold.

### Gaps
- No study was found that tests how many radar axes lay users can read, or that isolates area misjudgement in radar charts; the "3 to 6 axes" advice common in practitioner writing was not traced to an empirical source.
- No games-UX study on stat polygons (e.g. Persona 5's five social stats, mentioned in the plan) was found; that example remains unverified.
- No evaluation was found of slider-between-poles displays against bars for personality feedback specifically.

## 5. LLM-written personal summaries: risks and safeguards

### Takeaway
Three documented risks apply directly: language models lean toward telling users what they want to hear; people trust and prefer that; and inference of traits from free text is only weakly accurate at the level of one person. Safeguards have to be structural (evidence-bound sentences, banned content categories, a visible uncertainty label, a swap test), because users do not scrutinise AI statements about themselves.

### Cited Findings
- Sycophancy is described as "a general behavior of state-of-the-art AI assistants": five assistants showed it across four free-form text tasks; responses that match a user's views are more likely to be preferred by humans, and optimising for human preference sometimes sacrifices truthfulness. **[abstract] (preprint as listed on arXiv, v4 May 2025)** — [Sharma et al., "Towards Understanding Sycophancy in Language Models", arXiv:2310.13548](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.13548).
- Across 11 models, AI affirmed users' actions 50% more than humans did, including when the user described manipulation or deception; in two preregistered experiments (N = 1,604) interacting with sycophantic AI reduced willingness to repair a conflict and increased conviction of being right, while participants rated sycophantic responses as higher quality, trusted them more and wanted to use them again. **[abstract] (preprint; search results say a later version was published in *Science* in 2026 with slightly different figures, not verified here)** — [Cheng et al., arXiv:2510.01395](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.01395).
- Zero-shot inference of Big Five traits from text: for 1,000 Facebook users (last 200 status updates each), GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 scores correlated with 100-item self-report at an average r = 0.29 (range 0.22 to 0.33; GPT-4 average 0.31). Inference was less accurate for men, and for older adults with GPT-3.5. The authors warn about profiling without consent and call for regulation. **[full text via PMC]** — [Peters, H., & Matz, S. C. (2024). *PNAS Nexus, 3*(6), pgae231](https://doi.org/10.1093/pnasnexus/pgae231).
- People believe fictitious AI predictions about themselves in the same pattern as astrology; positive attitude to AI raises perceived validity and personalisation; analytic thinking style does not protect. **[abstract] (preprint)** — [Lee et al., arXiv:2408.06602](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.06602v3).
- UNICEF's *Guidance on AI and children*, version 3.0 (December 2025), lists ten requirements for child-centred AI, including "Ensure safety for children", "Protect children's data and privacy", "Provide transparency, explainability and accountability for children" and "Support children's best interests, development and well-being"; the new version adds material on AI companions used by children. **[landing page read; the full PDF was not opened]** — [UNICEF Innocenti](https://www.unicef.org/innocenti/reports/policy-guidance-ai-children).
- Vietnam's Law 91/2025/QH15, Article 30: personal data in AI systems must be processed for the right purpose and "giới hạn trong phạm vi cần thiết" (30.1), and AI processing of personal data must be risk-classified so that suitable protection is applied (30.4). **[full text, signed PDF p. 18]** — [chinhphu.vn](https://datafiles.chinhphu.vn/cpp/files/vbpq/2025/7/91qh.signed.pdf).

### Inferences
- An r of about 0.3 between model-inferred and self-reported traits means the model's reading of one player's free text is a weak signal. This supports the existing rules: every inferred value must carry a verbatim quote, a single narrative answer must not dominate, and the summary must say what it is based on. It also cautions against the 12 October direction of making free text the *main* input for indicator values without a cross-check from choices in play.
- Safeguards that follow from the cited risks:
  - *Against flattery:* the prompt forbids evaluative adjectives and requires one friction point; the swap test in section 1 detects praise-only output, since praise is the most transferable content.
  - *Against invented traits:* sentence-level evidence binding (quote or play event); drop unsupported sentences in code, not by asking the model to be careful.
  - *Against over-trust:* label "AI viết, có thể sai", show "dựa trên N câu trả lời và M lượt chơi", and give a one-tap "chưa đúng với mình" that removes or regenerates the text. The player's correction outranks the model.
  - *Against harm to minors:* a closed list of forbidden topics for the summary regardless of what the player typed (mental or physical health, intelligence or ability level, family circumstances, appearance, predictions about success or failure, "fit / not fit" for a job), enforced by an output check before display, with a fixed fallback text when the check fails or the model is unavailable.
  - *Against group bias:* do not send gender or exact age to the model for the summary (accuracy differed by gender and age in Peters & Matz); send only the answers and play signals.
- The summary should be regenerated sparingly and old versions kept, so a player can see that the text is a draft that changes with evidence instead of a verdict.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study was found that measures hallucinated traits or harmful statements in LLM-written self-descriptions for minors; the safeguards above are derived from adjacent evidence.
- The peer-reviewed status of Sharma et al. and the *Science* version of Cheng et al. were not verified on the publisher pages.
- No industry guideline specific to "AI-generated personality feedback" was located. The UNICEF PDF itself (recommendations on AI companions) was not read.

## 6. Which profile fields matter

### Takeaway
Ordinary profile content already reveals real personality to strangers, and behavioural traces allow sensitive attributes to be inferred that the person never stated. So each field should exist only if a feature uses it, and "decorative" fields are not free: they add disclosure without adding function.

### Cited Findings
- Observers who viewed 236 social-network profiles of college-age users (Facebook in the US, StudiVZ in Germany) formed impressions that matched the owners' *actual* personality and not their idealised self; accuracy was strongest for extraversion and lowest for neuroticism. **[secondary: university press release and search-result summaries; the article page showed only title and authors]** — Back, Stopfer, Vazire, Gaddis, Schmukle, Egloff & Gosling (2010). *Psychological Science, 21*(3), 372–374, [doi:10.1177/0956797609360756](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797609360756); summary at [Universität Mainz](https://press.uni-mainz.de/?p=13247).
- Strangers who looked only at a person's office or bedroom agreed with one another and were partly accurate about the occupant, best for openness and conscientiousness; sex and race stereotypes partly drove the judgments. **[secondary: APA Monitor coverage and search-result summary of the abstract]** — Gosling, Ko, Mannarelli & Morris (2002). *JPSP, 82*(3), 379–398, [doi:10.1037/0022-3514.82.3.379](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.82.3.379); coverage at [APA Monitor](https://www.apa.org/monitor/mar02/officebetray).
- From Facebook Likes alone (58,466 US volunteers, about 170 Likes each on average), models distinguished sexual orientation in men with 88% accuracy (75% in women), African American from Caucasian American users 95%, Democrat from Republican 85%, Christian from Muslim 82%, gender 93%; Openness was predicted at r = 0.43, intelligence r = 0.39, age r = 0.75. The authors warn such inference can be done without consent or awareness and recommend transparency and user control over data. **[full text via PMC]** — [Kosinski, M., Stillwell, D., & Graepel, T. (2013). *PNAS, 110*(15), 5802–5805](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3625324/).
- UK Children's Code, data minimisation: collect only the minimum needed for the elements of the service the child is "actively and knowingly" using; do not bundle collection for "improve", "enhance" or "personalise" purposes with the core service; give a separate choice for each additional purpose. **[regulator page read]** — [ICO, standard 8](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/childrens-information/childrens-code-guidance-and-resources/age-appropriate-design-a-code-of-practice-for-online-services/8-data-minimisation/).

### Inferences
- A field is *functional* if a named feature reads it; otherwise it is decorative. Applying that to the lead's list:
  - **Display name (nickname):** functional (greeting, AI addresses the player). A real full name is not needed.
  - **Birth date:** only the *year* (or an age band) is needed for age-appropriate handling and the guardian-consent flow that already exists; day and month are needed only if the zodiac feature ships and the player opts in. Age should not be shown as a profile decoration.
  - **Life stage** (lower-secondary, upper-secondary, university, working, changing career): functional for tone, examples and which occupations to suggest first; less identifying than age or school.
  - **Interests:** functional only if scenarios or suggestions use them. Choosing from a fixed set of tags gives usable data and avoids identifying free text; free-text interests are mostly decorative and harder to protect.
  - **Photo, school, class, location, social links, gender:** no feature in the plan uses them; photo and school are the fields that make a minor identifiable. Gender in particular should not be collected "for suggestions", given the plan's own warning about gender differences on the People–Things axis.
- Kosinski et al. is the reason play logs deserve the same care as typed fields: choices across 97 events are a behavioural trace from which more can be inferred than the eight intended dimensions.
- Back et al. implies that if any part of the profile were ever made visible to others, the interest list and self-description would reveal real personality to strangers; that is an argument for keeping the profile private, in line with BR-06.

### Gaps
- Back et al. (2010) and Gosling et al. (2002) were not read in the original; trait-level correlations are not quoted for that reason.
- No study was found that measures which onboarding fields improve personalisation quality in a career-exploration product; the functional/decorative split above is reasoning from the plan's features.

## 7. Privacy and ethics for minors

### Takeaway
All three reference regimes converge on the same design rules: collect only what an actively used feature needs, keep profiles private by default, keep profiling-based options off by default for children, and make access, correction and deletion easy. In Vietnam's decree, a psychological profile is not named as a sensitive category, but "data tracking behaviour in the use of ... online services" and "private life" are, and both can plausibly cover play logs and free-text self-description.

### Inferences
- **Is a psychological profile "sensitive"?** Under the decree as read, not by name. But (i) the eight indicators are *derived from* tracking choices in an online service, which is point (l); (ii) free-text self-description can easily contain point (c) private life or point (d) health. The cautious reading is to treat the play log, the free text and the derived profile as sensitive data: restricted access (Art. 4.2 of the decree), and note that the start-up exemption in Art. 38.2 of the law does not apply to those who directly process sensitive data. This is an interpretation for the university's legal office to confirm, not a legal conclusion.
- **Public versus private profile.** A public profile for players under 16 that shows self-description or indicators could amount to "disclosing information about the private life" of a child, which under Art. 24.2 needs consent from both the child and the legal representative. Together with the Children's Code default (not visible to other users unless the child changes the setting) and the project's own BR-06, the simplest compliant design is: no public profile, no profile sharing feature, no leaderboard built from profile data. If a social layer is wanted later, Art. 29 obligations (clear notice, no-tracking option, privacy settings) would also come into play.
- **Profiling default.** In JobQuest the profile is close to the core of the service, so the "essential to core service" reasoning may apply to building the profile from play. It does not obviously apply to *optional* uses such as reordering suggested occupations or the zodiac feature; those should be separate, off-by-default choices. Get to Know Me being optional (D-29) already fits this.
- **Minimisation in practice** matches section 6: each field tied to one feature; collection for "personalisation" not bundled with account creation; no nudging (no profile-completeness bar or reward for filling optional fields, which would be a nudge to disclose under standard 13).
- **Deletion** must cover three things separately: typed fields, free-text answers, and play-derived data, each deletable by the player (and by the guardian for children), with the derived summary deleted when its inputs are.
- **De-identification before the LLM call** has a clear legal payoff: under Art. 2.1 de-identified data is no longer personal data, which bears on the cross-border transfer issue already recorded in the hub. Free text needs a filter for names, schools, phone numbers and similar before it leaves the server.
- COPPA and the Children's Code do not apply to a Vietnamese university project as law; they are reference points showing the same principles, and useful to cite as international practice.

### Gaps
- Decree 356 was not read in the official gazette version, and the list wording above is a paraphrase from a reproduction. Art. 4.1(l) and (c) should be read in the official text before the report relies on them.
- The law's pages 5–13 and 16 (including Art. 9 on consent, Art. 14 on deletion, Art. 19, Arts. 20–22) were not re-read in this session; the hub's earlier notes on them stand unverified by this note.
- The law does not define "trẻ em" in the pages read; the under-16 threshold comes from the Law on Children 2016 as noted in the hub and was not checked here.
- Whether a university capstone team counts as a controller subject to these duties, and whether play-derived indicators fall under point (l), are open legal questions.
- GDPR Article 9 (special categories) was not opened, so no claim is made here about whether personality data is a special category under EU law.
- The 2025 COPPA amendments were not read; only their existence is confirmed.

## 8. Profiles that evolve with behaviour; statistics that reflect the person, not skill

### Takeaway
No direct evidence was found on showing users a comparison between what they said about themselves and what they did. The supporting evidence is indirect: self-report and observation each see things the other misses, feedback is safer at task level than self level, and self-tracking tools fail when data is collected but never turned into something the person can reflect on.

### Cited Findings
- Self and others (observers) each have blind spots: the self is predicted to be better for low-observability traits, observers for highly evaluative traits. **[secondary]** — [Vazire (2010), *JPSP, 98*(2), 281–300](https://findanexpert.unimelb.edu.au/scholarlywork/1452513-who-knows-what-about-a-person).
- Feedback is less effective, and more often harmful, as it shifts attention from the task toward the self. **[abstract]** — [Kluger & DeNisi (1996)](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.119.2.254).
- The stage-based model of personal informatics describes five stages (preparation, collection, integration, reflection, action); barriers in an early stage cascade into later ones, and the stages are iterative. **[secondary: author's publication page and search-result summary]** — [Li, Dey & Forlizzi (2010), CHI '10, pp. 557–566](https://ianli.owlstown.net/publications/17).
- A description presented as being about you can shift your later self-ratings toward it. **[full text, p. 380]** — [Dickson & Kelly 1985](https://cortecs.org/wp-content/uploads/2014/12/the-barnum-effect-in-personality-assessment-a-review-of-the-literature.pdf).
- Behavioural traces support inferences well beyond what was intended. **[full text]** — [Kosinski et al. (2013)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3625324/).

### Inferences
- Keep two visible layers: "Bạn tự kể" (self-report, editable by the player) and "Khi chơi" (from choices in events). Showing them side by side is the product's own argument made visible (D-03: trying beats self-report). A difference between the layers should be presented as a question for the player ("Bạn nói thích làm một mình; trong 6 tình huống bạn chọn làm cùng người khác 4 lần. Bạn nghĩ sao?"), never as a correction, because neither source is the ground truth and because self-level contradiction is the risky kind of feedback.
- A statistic is *informative and non-judgmental* when it (a) counts something the player did, (b) has no better or worse direction, (c) is compared only with the player's own other numbers or own past, and (d) is something the player can act on by choosing what to play next. Candidates that meet this: breadth of occupations tried, most-chosen kind of task, occupations returned to, self-report versus play comparison, enjoyment marks after a run, consistency of choices across similar situations.
- Candidates that fail the test or need care: speed or time per decision (reads as ability), streaks and daily counts (pressure, and they are behaviour tracking with no self-knowledge value), any percentile or rank against other players (social comparison amplified feedback effects in Chen 2025), and hint use. Hint use is informative as a working style ("tries first" versus "asks early") but is easily read as weakness; if shown, it needs neutral wording and no colour coding, and it must stay out of competence scoring, which is a separate open item in the hub (07 D-95).
- Every extra statistic is extra behavioural tracking under point (l) of the decree. Statistics should be computed from the events already stored for gameplay, with no new collection (no time-of-day, device or session-length statistics).
- Change over time should itself be visible and explained: "this band narrowed because you played 3 more events of this kind". An unexplained change in a description of oneself is the kind of opaque AI behaviour the UNICEF transparency requirement is aimed at.

### Gaps
- No study was found on the effect of showing users a discrepancy between self-reported and behaviour-derived traits (acceptance, usefulness, or harm), in games or elsewhere.
- No evidence was found on which behavioural statistics adolescents find meaningful in a career-exploration product; the candidate list is design reasoning to be tested with users.
- Research on help-seeking and hint use in tutoring systems was not searched in this session.
- Li, Dey & Forlizzi (2010) was not opened; only the publication record was seen.

## Đề xuất cho JobQuest

Viết cho cả nhóm, đọc được với người 15 tuổi. Mỗi mục dựa trên các phần 1–8 ở trên; chỗ nào là suy luận của người viết chứ chưa có nghiên cứu trực tiếp thì ghi rõ.

### 1. Danh sách trường của hồ sơ

| Nhóm | Trường | Dùng để làm gì |
|---|---|---|
| **Bắt buộc** | Tên hiển thị (biệt danh, không cần tên thật) | Game và AI gọi bạn bằng tên này |
| | Năm sinh | Biết bạn dưới hay trên 16 để xin đồng ý của người giám hộ. Không hiện tuổi lên hồ sơ |
| | Bạn đang ở giai đoạn nào (THCS, THPT, đại học, đã đi làm, đang đổi nghề) | Chọn giọng văn, ví dụ và nghề gợi ý trước |
| **Tuỳ chọn** (bỏ qua vẫn chơi được, không có thanh "hồ sơ hoàn thành 70%") | Sở thích: chọn tối đa 5 thẻ có sẵn | Lấy ví dụ trong tình huống cho gần với bạn |
| | Ngày và tháng sinh | Chỉ hỏi khi bạn bật phần hoàng đạo |
| | Câu trả lời Get to know me | Viết đoạn "Bạn tự kể" và gợi ý nhóm nghề thử trước |
| | Huy hiệu đại diện do game vẽ | Thay cho ảnh thật |
| **Không thu** | Ảnh thật, họ tên đầy đủ, trường, lớp, địa chỉ, số điện thoại, giấy tờ, vị trí, giới tính, tài khoản mạng xã hội | Không tính năng nào cần. Ảnh và tên trường là thứ làm lộ danh tính nhanh nhất |
| | Sức khoẻ, tâm lý, hoàn cảnh gia đình, tôn giáo, chính trị | Dữ liệu nhạy cảm. Nếu bạn lỡ gõ vào ô tự do thì hệ thống lọc bỏ trước khi gửi cho AI |

Ba luật đi kèm: hồ sơ **chỉ mình bạn xem**, không có trang công khai, không bảng xếp hạng; mỗi trường gắn với đúng một việc ghi ở cột phải; xoá được riêng từng phần (thông tin đã gõ, câu trả lời, dữ liệu khi chơi), xoá phần nào thì đoạn mô tả dựa trên phần đó cũng mất.

### 2. Bảy luật viết đoạn tự mô tả

1. **Có bằng chứng mới viết.** Mỗi câu phải chỉ ra được bạn đã nói gì hoặc đã chọn gì ("Bạn kể rằng…", "Trong 5 tình huống, bạn 4 lần chọn…"). Không có bằng chứng thì bỏ câu đó.
2. **Cấm câu ai đọc cũng thấy đúng.** Không dùng "đôi khi", "phần nào", "vừa… vừa…", không viết điều đúng với hầu hết học sinh ("bạn muốn có công việc mình thích").
3. **Tả việc bạn làm, không dán nhãn con người.** Viết "bạn hay chọn làm xong từng phần nhỏ rồi mới đi tiếp", không viết "bạn là người cẩn thận", không nhãn kiểu MBTI, không "hợp" hay "không hợp" nghề nào.
4. **Đúng một điều có thể khó, kèm một việc để thử.** Nói về tình huống ("khi đề bài chưa rõ, bạn thường dừng lại lâu; lần tới thử hỏi lại một câu"), không nói về trí thông minh, năng lực, sức khoẻ hay tương lai.
5. **Chỉ so bạn với chính bạn.** "Bạn chọn việc với dữ liệu nhiều hơn việc với người", không so với người chơi khác, không xếp hạng.
6. **Nói rõ mức chắc chắn.** Luôn có dòng "AI viết, có thể sai · dựa trên 6 câu trả lời và 3 lượt chơi". Ít dữ liệu thì viết "chưa đủ để nói", không đoán. Có nút "chưa đúng với mình" để bỏ hoặc viết lại.
7. **Ngắn và không khen suông.** 2–3 câu, tối đa 80 chữ, từ ngữ học sinh lớp 9 hiểu được, không tính từ khen ("tuyệt vời", "xuất sắc").

**Phép thử tráo hồ sơ** (làm trước khi dùng thật, với 15–30 bạn chơi thử): đưa mỗi bạn ba đoạn không ghi tên, gồm đoạn của bạn ấy và hai đoạn của người khác. Hỏi riêng hai câu cho từng đoạn: "Đoạn này đúng với bạn tới đâu?" và "Đoạn này có thể viết cho hầu hết bạn cùng lớp không?", rồi hỏi đoạn nào là của bạn. Đạt khi phần lớn chọn đúng đoạn của mình (đoán mò chỉ trúng 1 trong 3). Nếu ai cũng thấy cả ba đoạn đều đúng thì đoạn mô tả đang chung chung, phải sửa prompt. Bản tự động chạy mỗi lần đổi prompt: cho một model khác ghép đoạn mô tả về đúng bộ câu trả lời gốc; ghép sai nhiều nghĩa là đoạn viết không riêng.

### 3. Dạng biểu đồ và số trục

- **Dạng chính: thanh trượt hai đầu, mỗi chỉ số một hàng.** Hai đầu là hai cách làm việc, không đầu nào tốt hơn (ví dụ "Thích việc rõ ràng ↔ Chịu được việc mơ hồ"). Vị trí của bạn là một **đoạn** chứ không phải một chấm; càng nhiều dữ liệu đoạn càng ngắn.
- **Chưa đủ dữ liệu thì hàng đó màu xám** và ghi "chơi thêm 2 tình huống loại này để thấy", không đặt tạm ở giữa.
- **Mỗi lần hiện 5–6 hàng** có đủ dữ liệu nhất; các hàng còn lại gập lại. Con số 5–6 là lựa chọn thiết kế cho màn hình điện thoại, chưa tìm thấy nghiên cứu nào cho con số tối ưu.
- **Không dùng biểu đồ radar làm cách đọc chính.** Hai thí nghiệm so sánh tìm được đều cho thấy radar khó đọc nhất và ít được thích nhất; hình đa giác còn khiến người xem nghĩ "to hơn là giỏi hơn". Nếu muốn giữ hình ngũ giác cho giống game thì dùng làm huy hiệu trang trí nhỏ, thứ tự trục cố định, và luôn có các thanh trượt bên dưới.
- Mỗi đầu thanh có một ví dụ lấy từ chính lượt chơi của bạn, bấm vào thì thấy tình huống nào đã tạo ra vị trí đó.

### 4. Bảy thống kê ứng viên (không phải điểm kỹ năng)

| Thống kê | Nghĩa trong một dòng |
|---|---|
| Số nghề và số nhóm nghề đã thử | Bạn đã nhìn rộng tới đâu, không phải bạn giỏi tới đâu |
| Loại việc bạn chọn nhiều nhất | Khi được chọn, bạn hay nghiêng về việc với người, với dữ liệu hay với máy móc |
| Nghề bạn quay lại chơi nhiều nhất | Thứ kéo bạn trở lại, thường thật hơn điều bạn nói là mình thích |
| "Bạn tự kể" so với "Khi chơi" | Chỗ giống và chỗ khác giữa điều bạn nghĩ về mình và điều bạn đã chọn; khác không có nghĩa là sai |
| Dấu thích / không thích sau mỗi lượt (một chạm) | Nghề nào chơi xong bạn thấy vui, nghề nào thấy mệt |
| Độ nhất quán của lựa chọn | Gặp tình huống giống nhau bạn có chọn cùng hướng không; đổi hướng cũng là thông tin |
| Cách bạn dùng gợi ý | Bạn thường tự thử trước hay hỏi sớm; đây là thói quen làm việc, không tính vào điểm |

Không làm: tốc độ trả lời, chuỗi ngày chơi liên tục, giờ chơi trong ngày, thứ hạng hay phần trăm so với người khác. Mọi thống kê chỉ tính từ dữ liệu game vốn đã lưu để chạy lượt chơi, không thu thêm. Thống kê "cách dùng gợi ý" dễ bị hiểu thành điểm yếu nên cần thử với người chơi thật trước khi giữ.

### 5. Hồ sơ đổi thế nào khi chơi

1. **Hai lớp luôn tách nhau:** "Bạn tự kể" (từ Get to know me, bạn sửa được) và "Khi chơi" (từ lựa chọn trong tình huống). Lớp sau không bao giờ lặng lẽ ghi đè lớp trước.
2. **Bỏ qua Get to know me vẫn có hồ sơ:** lớp "Khi chơi" tự đầy dần; lớp "Bạn tự kể" để trống kèm lời mời, không ép.
3. **Đoạn thanh trượt ngắn dần** theo số tình huống đã chơi; hàng xám sáng lên khi đủ dữ liệu.
4. **Mỗi thay đổi có lý do đọc được:** "Hàng này dịch sang phải sau lượt chơi Kiểm thử phần mềm hôm 12/10".
5. **Đoạn mô tả chỉ viết lại khi bạn bấm**, hoặc sau một mốc rõ ràng (ví dụ thử xong nghề thứ ba). Bản cũ được giữ để bạn thấy mình đã đổi thế nào.
6. **Chỗ hai lớp lệch nhau thì hỏi, không phán:** "Bạn nói thích làm một mình, nhưng khi chơi bạn hay chọn làm cùng người khác. Bạn nghĩ sao?"
7. **Khi AI lỗi hoặc đoạn viết không qua bộ lọc:** hiện các thanh trượt và thống kê như thường, chỗ đoạn mô tả ghi "Chưa viết được, thử lại sau", không hiện đoạn chưa kiểm.

### 6. Ba việc cần hỏi lại trước khi có người dùng thật

- Đọc Điều 4 Nghị định 356 trong bản công báo để chốt: log lựa chọn khi chơi và hồ sơ suy ra từ đó có thuộc "dữ liệu theo dõi hành vi sử dụng dịch vụ trực tuyến" không. Trong lúc chờ, coi như có.
- Hỏi pháp chế của trường: nếu sau này muốn có phần hồ sơ cho người khác xem, người dưới 16 tuổi cần đồng ý của cả bạn ấy và người giám hộ (Luật 91/2025, Điều 24 khoản 2).
- Mở bản gốc Forer (1949) và Whiston và cộng sự (2017) trước khi đưa số liệu vào báo cáo; trong ghi chú này hai nguồn đó mới ở mức nguồn thứ cấp.
