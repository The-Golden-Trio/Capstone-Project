\subsubsection{State modelling}

The scenario is the unit of content that passes between the domain expert and the content administrator, and its status decides who may act on it and whether explorers can see it. Its lifecycle is modelled as state machine SM-01 below. Only a scenario in the Published state is served to explorers, which is how the model enforces BR-05 and BR-13: no scenario reaches an explorer without expert review and the author's play-test. Every transition is logged with its actor and time, and the content of a published scenario cannot be edited (NFR-16, DR-13); a change to published content therefore takes the form of a new scenario that replaces the old one.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth,height=0.9\textheight,keepaspectratio]{figures/SM-01_ScenarioLifecycle.png}
\caption{Scenario lifecycle (SM-01)}
\end{figure}

A scenario begins in Draft when the content administrator opens it for a task, and only one scenario per task may be active at a time (FR-33). Draft, Submitted, and In Review belong to the domain experts: the author prepares the content, and a second expert judges it against the review criteria. Approved, In Playtest, and Ready belong to presentation: the content administrator turns the approved content into a game, and the author confirms that the game presents it correctly. Published and Retired are the states explorers can encounter, the first as playable content and the second only in the records of explorers who played it. Retired is final: there is no separate Rejected state, since rejecting or deleting a scenario retires it; a retired scenario cannot be reopened and no longer counts as the active scenario of its task. Besides the lifecycle of content, three statuses decide how a play session behaves, and they are modelled as SM-02: the run, the assessment of an answer, and the AI job that produces it. A run is in progress while turns are appended to its event log, and it finishes when its last turn closes or is abandoned when the explorer leaves for good; a pause because no model is available is not a status of the run but an event on its log, so the run stays in progress and its progress is kept (D-92). An assessment is queued when the answer is stored with its job; it is graded when enough judges agree, it stays pending when too few judges answered (D-108) so that it can be regraded later without losing the answer, and it fails when the attempts are used up. An AI job moves from queued to running when a worker takes it, to succeeded when its result is stored and published, back to queued for a retry while attempts remain, and to failed after the last attempt; the system administrator can re-run a failed job (FR-43).

\subsubsection{Frontend}

The frontend serves two populations. Explorers, in twenty of the fifty-three use cases of Section 5.1.2, play on their own phones over weak connections (NFR-13), may use assistive technology (NFR-19), read the interface in Vietnamese (NFR-08), and wait seconds while a model assesses a written answer (NFR-01, NFR-02). Staff, in the other thirty-\allowbreak{}three, work at desktops in forms, review queues, and tables (FR-21 to FR-43).

Application framework. The frontend must render a game interface with many interacting states --- the occupation map, conversations with characters, the four response forms of FR-10, hints, follow-up questions, and results (FR-05, FR-07, FR-10 to FR-14). It must show provisional feedback on closed formats at once, by the same rules the server applies (FR-15, BR-11), stay responsive while a model works (NFR-01, NFR-02), and load quickly on a mid-range phone without installation (NFR-13, DC-05). Every route except the landing page requires sign-in (FR-01), so no screen needs search indexing.

\textbf{Decision: React 19 with Vite 8, as a client-\allowbreak{}rendered single-page application.\allowbreak{}} React's component model fits an interface of many independent, stateful parts, and it is the most used web framework, by 44.7\% of developers\cite{r134}, so libraries for drag-\allowbreak{}and-\allowbreak{}drop ordering, 3D rendering, and accessibility are mature. Running in the browser lets the shared rules package, \textit{game-\allowbreak{}core}, compute provisional scores without a round trip. React 19's optimistic-\allowbreak{}update hook shows ``saved, assessing'' the moment an answer is sent\cite{r135}. Vite's production build splits code into chunks loaded on demand\cite{r136}: the prototype's entry was about 195 KB compressed in the build of 18 September 2026, and a budget of 200 KB for the first interactive screen, about one second under the Lighthouse mobile profile\cite{r137}, is checked in continuous integration. Server-side rendering is not used: with every screen behind sign-in it brings no indexing benefit, and it would add a server that renders personal data, whereas static files hold none and can be cached on a content delivery network without affecting DC-03. Vue 3 rates as well on the technical criteria; the choice rests on feasibility, since moving to it would rewrite every screen and the 3D map.

Occupation map. The map must convey the progression metaphor of Section 4.2.1 (FR-05, FR-07), remain operable with a keyboard and a screen reader (NFR-19), and not exclude low-end devices (NFR-13).

\textbf{Decision: three.js 0.186 through @\allowbreak{}react-\allowbreak{}three/\allowbreak{}fiber 9.7, with an SVG view drawn from the same data.} @\allowbreak{}react-\allowbreak{}three/\allowbreak{}fiber writes the 3D scene as React components that share state with the rest of the interface\cite{r138}. Because a canvas exposes nothing to a screen reader and a 3D scene costs GPU work every frame\cite{r139}, an SVG view, in which each planet is a focusable, named element\cite{r140}, is the accessible equivalent. It can be chosen at any time and is used automatically when WebGL is missing or lost, when reduced motion or data saving is requested, or on low-memory devices. The 3D libraries, about 310 KB compressed, load only when the 3D view opens.

Styling. Styles must add little to the download (NFR-13) and let each occupation carry its own theme without code changes (NFR-10).

\textbf{Decision: Tailwind CSS 4.3.} It generates CSS only for the classes used and has no runtime\cite{r141}; occupation themes are design tokens held as CSS variables and supplied by content data. CSS Modules rate as well technically, and occupation themes as CSS variables work in either; Tailwind is kept because the prototype is already written in it.

Client state. Client state must apply \textit{game-\allowbreak{}core}'s reducer directly (FR-10, FR-15) and keep unsent turns through a lost connection (NFR-03).

\textbf{Decision: zustand 5.0.} A store wraps the reducer, so one dispatch updates both screen and rules state; its persistence middleware keeps unsent turns in browser storage, resent with an idempotency key on reconnection\cite{r142}. It needs no provider tree and adds little to the bundle.

Routing. Routing must keep the 3D map and all staff code out of the explorer's first download (NFR-13), separate explorer and staff areas by role (FR-41), and resume a run on any device (NFR-03).

\textbf{Decision: React Router 7.18.} Routes load on demand, nested layouts separate the areas by role, and route loaders fetch the state of an interrupted run from the server\cite{r143}.

Result delivery. Assessment results must reach the explorer as soon as they are ready (NFR-02, FR-14) over connections that drop (NFR-13). Only the server needs to push; the client submits answers by ordinary requests.

\textbf{Decision: server-sent events.} The browser's built-in \textit{EventSource} receives pushed events over plain HTTP, through any proxy, and reconnects by itself\cite{r144}; a bidirectional WebSocket channel is not needed. Polling is kept only as a fallback where a stream cannot be opened (Section 5.3.1).

Staff application. Staff work in review queues, lists, and statistics (FR-21 to FR-43), in dialogs, comboboxes, and grids, where hand-built interfaces most often fail accessibility (NFR-19).

\textbf{Decision: the same stack, plus TanStack Query and React Aria.} TanStack Query caches and refreshes server data\cite{r145}; React Aria supplies accessible interaction primitives\cite{r146}. The staff application is a separate bundle on the same origin and sign-in screen. For server data, the alternative to TanStack Query is fetching by hand, which repeats loading, caching, and refresh logic on every staff screen.

Localisation. Only Vietnamese is supported in this phase (NFR-08). Interface strings must be complete in every supported language, and adding English later must not require changes to the screens.

\textbf{Decision: a typed message table with the standard \textit{Intl} API.} An unknown key fails compilation and a test fails a missing translation; plurals, dates, and numbers are formatted by \textit{Intl}\cite{r147}; content fields carry a locale, so a second language is added as data. Generated text is inserted as text, which React escapes, so model output cannot inject markup (NFR-17)\cite{r148}.

\subsubsection{Backend}

The backend carries five kinds of work: short requests for navigation, records, and staff edits; durable recording of every turn as it happens (NFR-03); assessment and follow-up generation, which wait seconds on external models and occasionally fail (NFR-01, NFR-02, NFR-04); authoritative scoring (BR-02, BR-11); and a governed content lifecycle with logged transitions (NFR-16).

Application framework. Most backend time is spent waiting --- on models, on the database, on open result streams --- and at Stage 4 about five thousand explorers are connected at once (Section 5.3.1). The framework must also give the modular structure of Section 5.3.1 clear module boundaries, check occupation-\allowbreak{}scoped roles on every request (FR-40, FR-41), integrate a job queue and server-sent events, and score by the same rules as the browser (FR-15, BR-11).

\textbf{Decision: NestJS 11.1 on Node.js, in TypeScript.\allowbreak{}} Node.js serves I/O on an event loop, so a waiting request or open stream costs memory rather than a thread. NestJS modules and dependency injection give each bounded context its own module; guards enforce occupation-\allowbreak{}scoped roles; interceptors write the activity log; and first-party integrations provide BullMQ queues and server-sent events\cite{r149}. Dependency injection also binds the model port to the provider adapter named in configuration (DC-02, NFR-12)\cite{r150}. The server imports the same \textit{game-core} package as the browser and recomputes every score from stored events. Models are called over HTTP and run nowhere in the process, so the request path needs no Python; the statistics for evaluating RP-1 and RP-2 run offline in Python scripts. Long computation, which would block an event loop, runs in worker processes. FastAPI's advantage, the Python AI ecosystem, is not needed on the request path, because models are called over HTTP; it would cost a second copy of the game rules.

Database. Much of the data is relational, with rules that span records: a reviewer is never the author (FR-25), one active scenario per task (FR-33), exactly one content administrator per occupation (FR-40), and the replay cap (BR-11). Publication and its log must be atomic, and published content immutable (NFR-16); scenario content is nested documents (NFR-10, NFR-15); statistics are shown only above a population threshold (NFR-06, FR-19, FR-39); and the store must be hostable in Vietnam (DC-03).

\textbf{Decision: PostgreSQL, accessed through Prisma ORM 7.10.} Foreign keys, check constraints, partial unique indexes, and transactions enforce the cross-\allowbreak{}record rules in the database itself --- ``one active scenario per task'' is a single partial index\cite{r151}. A transition and its log entry share one transaction, and a trigger refuses updates to published releases. Content is stored in \textit{jsonb} columns\cite{r152}, validated by \textit{game-\allowbreak{}core}'s schemas on write and read. Thresholded statistics are SQL grouping, window functions, and materialised views. PostgreSQL is open source, offered by every cloud provider, and the most used database, at 55.6\%\cite{r134}. Prisma generates a typed client from the schema and, from version 7, runs over the standard \textit{pg} driver\cite{r153}; partial indexes, checks, and triggers, which Prisma does not express, live in SQL migrations and are tested against the database.

Job queue and messaging. Model work must survive failures, be retried, and be listed for re-run when it fails (NFR-04, FR-43); it must respect each provider's rate limits and serve live play first --- narration and follow-ups for an explorer in a scenario before background scoring, and both before authoring work (NFR-01, NFR-11); results must reach the API replica holding the explorer's stream (NFR-02); and each session's model cost must be counted atomically (NFR-11).

\textbf{Decision: Redis with BullMQ 6.3.} BullMQ provides persisted jobs, retries with exponential backoff, job priorities, per-queue rate limiting, and a set of failed jobs for re-run\cite{r154}; Redis's publish--subscribe carries results to every API replica, and its atomic counters meter the session budget. Redis holds nothing that cannot be rebuilt from PostgreSQL, and a scheduled reconciliation re-queues any stored answer without a result, so losing Redis loses no answer. pg-boss is the closest alternative and would avoid running Redis during the pilots; BullMQ is chosen to keep queue traffic off the system of record at national scale and for its first-party NestJS integration.

Authentication. The platform must register, sign in, recover, and delete accounts (FR-01, FR-02); hash passwords, protect sessions, and end idle ones (NFR-17); scope roles to occupations (FR-41); and keep identity data in Vietnam (DC-03).

\textbf{Decision: the platform's own module --- argon2id, JWT bearer access and refresh tokens, and optional Google sign-in.} Passwords are hashed with argon2id\cite{r155}; a fifteen-\allowbreak{}minute access token and a rotating refresh token with reuse detection\cite{r156} are sent as bearer tokens, the short-lived access token being held in memory by the application; Google ID tokens are verified with google-\allowbreak{}auth-\allowbreak{}library. Accounts stay in the platform's database in Vietnam, which an external identity service would hold abroad, and occupation-\allowbreak{}scoped authorisation must live in the application whichever service is used. The module is completed against ASVS 5.0 level 2\cite{r157}: single-use, short-lived reset tokens; an inactivity limit; account status checked on every refresh and staff request; rate limiting of sign-in; and a second factor for staff. The remaining risk is the security work that Keycloak would supply ready-made, which is why the module is held to ASVS level 2 and tested against it.

Validation and code organisation. Every input and every model output must be checked against a schema (NFR-17, FR-11), and one copy of the rules and contracts must serve both applications, the API, and the worker (FR-15).

\textbf{Decision: Zod 4.6 schemas in an Nx 23.1 monorepo.} Zod schemas, shared by API, clients, and the rule checks, validate requests, content documents, and model output --- scores within rubric anchors, quotations verbatim. Nx keeps \textit{game-core} and the schemas in one repository, enforces module boundaries so staff code never enters the explorer's bundle (NFR-13), and builds and tests only what a change affects\cite{r158}.

Packaging and hosting. Personal data must be stored in Vietnam (DC-03, NFR-05) --- Decree 53/2022 further requires domestic online-\allowbreak{}service enterprises to store users' data in Vietnam, applicability to be confirmed (Section 4.1.4)\cite{r159,r160} --- and the platform must stay available (NFR-18) while growing from a university pilot to a national service.

\textbf{Decision: Docker containers, hosted in Vietnam.} One image runs as API or worker, with rolling deployment and health checks, and the platform depends only on portable services --- PostgreSQL, Redis, and S3-\allowbreak{}compatible storage --- so moving host is a change of configuration. The system of record runs on university infrastructure for the Stage 1 pilot and on a cloud provider in Vietnam from Stage 2\cite{r161}; an international region such as Singapore would make every account a cross-\allowbreak{}border transfer\cite{r99}. Only de-\allowbreak{}identified text leaves the country, through the model gateway. International providers adding capacity in Vietnam, such as the AWS Hanoi Local Zone, whose control plane remains in Singapore\cite{r162}, are reassessed with the legal office before Stage 2.

\subsubsection{AI algorithms, technologies, and models}

The project does not design, train, or fine-tune a model. It composes existing language models, under rules and expert oversight, so that the problem of Section 4.2.2 is solved: occupational scenarios that stay faithful to expert content (RP-1) and free-text answers scored in agreement with experts (RP-2). The choices that shape that composition are compared here; how the selected parts work together is designed in Section 5.3.6, and how well they work is evaluated in Section 7.3.

Where AI works. AI is used at five steps, three while content is authored and two during play. AI drafts, connects, scores, and filters; domain experts decide occupational content, scoring keys, and publication; deterministic rules, not models, check what models produce while an explorer is waiting.

The five components are described in Appendix 3: what each does, the material it receives, the checks on its output, and where a person decides. In short, AI-1 interviews the domain expert, AI-2 drafts the seed from the answers, AI-3 helps the expert test before publishing, AI-4 narrates during play without changing canon, and AI-5 grades each answer; the scoring model is never the narrating model (DC-01).

Holding generation to expert content. The narrator (AI-4) must not invent occupational facts (RP-1, NFR-15), must change when an expert revises a seed (BR-14), and must not tie the platform to one provider (TC7).

\textbf{Decision: the whole seed in the prompt, with structured output and rule checks.} A seed fits in the context of every candidate model, so retrieval adds a way to miss a fact without saving anything that matters, and fine-tuning needs data the project will not have before Stage B. When the source fits in the prompt, long-\allowbreak{}context prompting outperformed retrieval on average\cite{r121}; holding authored structure outside free generation is the established approach in interactive narrative\cite{r120}.

Checking generated text while the explorer waits. Every narration and follow-up must be checked before it is shown (NFR-15), within the five seconds of NFR-01.

\textbf{Decision: deterministic rules at run time, and a model of another family reviewing the logs afterwards.\allowbreak{}} The rules catch exactly the failures that matter most to fidelity, invented names and numbers, and they cost no waiting time; what rules cannot judge, tone and plausibility, is left to a review that does not delay the explorer. A text that fails the rules is regenerated a bounded number of times and then handled as a model failure (Section 5.3.6).

Scoring free-text answers. The scorer (AI-5) must agree with experts (RP-2, NFR-14) without reference answers, be independent of the narrator (DC-01), and tell the expert which cases to review (AI-3).

\textbf{Decision: a panel of three judges from three model families, scored by the median.} The panel costs three calls, but it removes the need to know the best judge in advance, keeps the generator's family out of scoring, and its disagreements tell the expert which cases to review. The median is used rather than the mean or a majority vote because levels are ordinal: the median of three levels is always a level of the scale, it is unaffected by one outlying judge, and it is defined when all three judges differ, where a majority vote is not. A five-judge vote matched the best single judge, with a Pearson correlation with humans of 0.627 against 0.623\cite{r123}; the design therefore does not claim that a panel is more accurate than the best judge, only that it reaches that judge's level without knowing in advance which judge it is.

Obtaining well-formed output. Every model output is data that the platform acts on: a judgment must carry a quote, a rationale, and a level, in that order, for each criterion (Section 5.3.6), and a narration must carry its text and any follow-up separately.

\textbf{Decision: the provider's schema-\allowbreak{}constrained mode where it exists, JSON mode elsewhere, and validation of every output against the same schema.} An output that fails validation is retried once and then treated as a failed call, which triggers failover. A repairing model is not used, because a model that rewrites a judgment can change its level, and a changed level is exactly the error the evaluation must not hide.

Reaching model providers. Providers must be replaceable by configuration (DC-02, NFR-12), judge families kept apart (DC-01), and personal data kept off any path it does not need (TC3).

\textbf{Decision: an adapter layer written by the project, with the OpenAI-\allowbreak{}compatible API as the common format and native adapters where needed.} Most candidates accept the common format, so a provider changes by configuration; the Anthropic judge uses a native adapter, because its compatibility layer does not guarantee schema-\allowbreak{}conforming output. A chain-\allowbreak{}composition framework such as LangChain\cite{r197} is not used: its abstractions hide the per-call token counts and steps that the cost ledger and the evaluation depend on.

Models. Four model families are needed: one for the narrator and three for the judging panel, each different from the others (DC-01). The criteria are Vietnamese capability (NFR-08), price (NFR-11), schema-\allowbreak{}constrained output, knowledge of IT work, and response time from Vietnam; the last two have no public benchmark for the pilot roles and are measured in Section 7.3. The table gives Vietnamese scores from the SEA-HELM leaderboard of 18 September 2026\cite{r132} and list prices read on 8 and 9 October 2026\cite{r133}; where a candidate is not on the leaderboard, the score shown is that of the closest listed model of the same family, under that model's name.

The candidate models, with list prices, Vietnamese benchmark scores, and structured-\allowbreak{}output support, are tabulated in Appendix 3.

\textbf{Decision: judges from the Anthropic, OpenAI, and Google families; qwen3.\allowbreak{}8-\allowbreak{}flash as the provisional narrator, with deepseek-\allowbreak{}flash as its failover.} Scoring is the role whose accuracy the project must demonstrate (RP-2), so the three families with the highest Vietnamese scores judge; the narrator writes short connecting text held by the rules above, so it comes from a fourth, cheaper family. Within each judge family, the tier is chosen by the test of Section 7.3: the cheaper tier is kept unless it is clearly worse, and the other tier is its failover. With the token counts assumed in Section 5.3.6, one playthrough costs about USD 0.010 with small-tier judges and USD 0.054 with mid-tier judges, narrator included. The data terms of every provider, no training on submitted data and limited retention (NFR-05), are confirmed in writing before any explorer's text is sent.

Testing the composition offline. The test harness must run every judge repeatedly, compute ordinal agreement, and keep every judgment reviewable (Section 7.3).

\textbf{Decision: Inspect AI.} It has the repeated runs, multiple graders, and median reducer the panel needs, and its logs make each judgment reviewable; prompts and rubrics stay in data files that both the harness and the product read. In the product, the judge is the project's own TypeScript module with schema-\allowbreak{}validated output. These tools have no peer-\allowbreak{}reviewed papers; the cited science is in the method, rubric-\allowbreak{}based scoring with reasoning before the score\cite{r58} and panels of judges\cite{r74}.

Strength of the judge relative to the generator. The judge does not need to be stronger than the generator, because it scores text written by a person, not text the generator wrote. It does need enough capacity: on German clinical-\allowbreak{}reasoning transcripts, a small model agreed poorly with human raters (intraclass correlation below 0.20) and a larger one moderately (0.41 to 0.57), against 0.45 between the two human raters\cite{r124}. Whether a larger tier, or a reasoning model, is worth its cost for this task is not settled by published evidence, so each judge family is tested at two tiers and the cheaper tier is kept unless it is clearly worse.

Reliability of a language-\allowbreak{}model judge. Published evidence supports a conditional answer, not reliability by default, and none of it concerns Vietnamese or role-play. A synthesis of 65 studies of essay scoring found agreement between language models and human raters to vary widely and to depend on context\cite{r101}. Untrained general models fall well short of dedicated scorers: on the ASAP essays, ChatGPT and Llama reached an average quadratic weighted kappa of 0.313 and 0.297, against 0.817 for a supervised system\cite{r125}. Agreement comes close to human level only under conditions: GPT-4 given a few scored examples reached a kappa of 0.70 on short answers in school subjects, against 0.75 between humans\cite{r102}, and approached dedicated scoring systems on CEFR essays once calibration examples were supplied\cite{r103}. Three weaknesses recur. Scorers handle fully correct and fully incorrect answers well and degrade on partially correct ones, where human agreement stays stable\cite{r126}. Without a reference answer, judges agree with experts mainly on questions they can answer themselves\cite{r127}, and a model's accuracy as a judge closely follows its accuracy at solving the same problems\cite{r60}; JobQuest has no reference answers, so the judge must know IT work, and the level descriptions of the rubric must carry the domain knowledge. And consistency is not validity: GPT-4's repeated ratings agreed with each other at an intraclass correlation of 0.94 to 0.99\cite{r105}, which says nothing about agreement with people. The design answers each weakness: a strong model and an explicit rubric from the start, scored examples later from expert rulings, a panel whose disagreements send partially correct cases to the expert, and level descriptions written by the expert. The project then measures reliability on its own task instead of assuming it (RP-2), against the criteria of Williamson, Xi and Breyer\cite{r71}: quadratic weighted kappa of at least 0.70, a standardised mean difference of at most 0.15, and agreement at most 0.10 below that between experts. The data, raters, measures, and thresholds are fixed in Section 7.3.

AI-native character. The definition adopted is that a system is AI-native when AI is constitutive of its core loop, so that removing the AI makes the central activity collapse or change fundamentally\cite{r130}, and when AI is a natural part of design, deployment, operation, and maintenance rather than a component added to a system built without it\cite{r131}. The test is therefore applied to JobQuest's core loop, the cycle an explorer repeats in every situation of a scenario.

Removing AI leaves steps 1 and 2, the parts any quiz has; steps 3 to 5, which make the scenario a performed task rather than a questionnaire, either disappear or change fundamentally, so JobQuest meets the first part of the definition. It meets the second because AI also works outside the loop: it collects expert knowledge and drafts seeds (AI-1, AI-2), finds the cases an expert must see (AI-3), and reviews logs in operation. For the same reason there is no path without AI: when a model fails, the system switches to another model in the same role that meets the same constraints, which is still AI (DC-02); if no model is available, the session pauses with answers and progress saved, and resumes when a model returns (NFR-04, Section 5.3.6).

Items awaiting game design. Several items cannot be settled before the game design is fixed: the scoring scale, where $-$1 / 0 / +1 / +2 is planned and not final and the current sample scenarios describe three levels only; the competency scoring table for a playthrough and the specific criteria given to the judge; the list of required facts in a seed, needed to measure fact preservation (NFR-15); and how evidence from the play process, such as hints, time, and follow-ups, counts and how scores combine into a competency. The AI design does not depend on them: the judge scores any criterion that comes with level descriptions, on any ordinal scale (Section 5.3.6), and agreement with experts can be computed on any such scale.

Architectural style. The drivers call for a design in which nothing slow or fallible sits inside a request, the server owns every score, a run can be replayed, and the model-\allowbreak{}facing work can grow on its own.

\textbf{Decision: a modular monolith with asynchronous workers.} One codebase is deployed as API processes, which answer requests, and worker processes, which run queued jobs. Modules follow the use case groups of Section 5.1.2 and communicate only through public interfaces, so any could later be extracted; the one part that must scale independently, model-\allowbreak{}facing work, is already separated by the worker tier, following the practice of extracting services only once a boundary has proved itself\cite{r163}. Inside, model providers, the database, and email are reached through ports with replaceable adapters\cite{r150}, which meets DC-02; and runs are event-\allowbreak{}sourced\cite{r164}, their state being the fold of their events through \textit{game-\allowbreak{}core}. Four alternatives were rejected, each for a specific requirement.

Each module is described below with its responsibility and its state in the prototype.

Explorer application. The occupation map, levels, the scenario player for the four response forms of FR-10, hints, follow-ups, results, the personal record, ratings, and feedback (FR-03 to FR-20). It runs \textit{game-core} for instant provisional feedback on closed formats, queues unsent turns locally, and receives narration, follow-ups, and assessment results through a server-sent event stream. Prototype: screens, the 3D map, and the four forms exist; the turn queue, event stream, and 2D map do not.

Staff application. The thirty-\allowbreak{}three staff use cases: the AI-1 interview through which a domain expert gives the knowledge a seed needs, review, test playthroughs, reliability, and blind rating for domain experts; seed drafting with AI-2, catalogue, presentation, publication, and statistics for content administrators; accounts, model configuration, monitoring, and feedback for system administrators. It shares the sign-in screen and origin with the explorer application (FR-41) but is a separate download. Prototype: absent.

Game core. A library with no framework, network, or storage dependency: the reducer folding run events into state, scoring of closed formats against expert keys, credit with the per-\allowbreak{}scenario cap, unlocking and adjacency offers, content schemas, and the rule checks on model output of Section 5.2.3 --- numbers and names in narration found in the seed or the player's text, the follow-up count, each judge's quotation found verbatim in the answer, levels within the scale. It runs unchanged in browser and server. Prototype: present; its free-text grader is a synchronous keyword matcher inside the reducer, which must become an asynchronous assessment entering the run as an event.

Identity and access. Accounts, sign-in and recovery, sessions, occupation-\allowbreak{}scoped roles, disabling, deletion, and the activity log (FR-01, FR-02, FR-40, FR-41); every module asks it whether an actor may act on an occupation. Prototype: registration, email and Google sign-in, rotating sessions, and deletion exist; reset, idle expiry, scoped roles, disabling, and the log do not, and the three prototype roles must become the four actors of Section 4.2.1.

Exploration. Everything outside the assessment itself: orientation, catalogue and profiles, enrolment, runs and their events, credit, unlocking, adjacency offers, side quests, ratings, records, and feedback (FR-03 to FR-09, FR-15 to FR-20). It validates turn events with \textit{game-core} and stores them; each free-text answer is handed to Assessment, which stores it with an assessment request in the same transaction. Prototype: present, but runs are submitted as one batch at the end.

Assessment. Turns answers into results, feedback, and guidance (FR-10 to FR-14). Closed formats are scored synchronously by \textit{game-\allowbreak{}core}; each free-text answer, follow-up answers included, is graded by the judge panel as soon as it is submitted (AI-5), and only then is a follow-up decided (FR-12). The grade and each activity's checked feedback note are written back as events, and at the end of the scenario the notes are combined into the feedback shown on the summary screen in the three separate parts of FR-14. It also routes explorers' flags of wrong content to the occupation's domain experts. Prototype: closed formats scored by replaying the run; free text matched by keyword.

Content lifecycle. Occupations, knowledge, scenarios and drafts, generation history, review criteria, assignments and decisions, presentation, assets, and publication (FR-21 to FR-38). It is the only writer of content, implements the state machine SM-01, writes each transition with its log entry in one transaction, and publishes immutable, versioned releases. It uses AI orchestration for generation, validation, presentation, and missing assets; the scheduler for deadlines and annual revalidation; and the importer for O*NET and ESCO, whose interest codes also drive orientation matching (FR-03). A domain expert or content administrator can send a published scenario back for revalidation while it stays playable (FR-27). Prototype: versioned immutable documents, written only by a seed script.

AI orchestration. Runs every model use of the five AI components of Section 5.2.3 as a job: the expert interview (AI-1) and seed drafting (AI-2) at authoring time, with presentation design and missing assets (FR-35, FR-36); test playthroughs (AI-3); narration, follow-ups, and feedback notes during play (AI-4, FR-12, FR-14); and grading of each answer by a panel of three judges (AI-5, FR-11). The Get-\allowbreak{}to-\allowbreak{}know-\allowbreak{}me quiz uses no AI (FR-04). Per job it loads the pinned content version, removes identifying details from explorer text, checks the session budget, calls the model port, applies \textit{game-\allowbreak{}core}'s rule checks, and records model, prompt version, cost, and latency. There is no scripted substitute for a model's output: a job that cannot complete is retried or waits, and a session pauses until it can. It rechecks at job time that no judge shares the narrator's family or another judge's. Prototype: absent; queue and model-\allowbreak{}client libraries are declared but unused.

Model gateway. One adapter per provider and a registry recording each model's role --- text generation, assessment, image generation (FR-36) --- with its provider and family; configuration is refused when the narrator and a judge, or two judges, share either (FR-42, DC-01). It applies per-\allowbreak{}provider concurrency limits, timeouts, retries, and a circuit breaker. Adapters are written by the project over the OpenAI-\allowbreak{}compatible format, with native adapters where that format falls short (Section 5.2.3). Prototype: absent.

Analytics. Everything shown only in aggregate: ratings and statistics above the population threshold, play-tests excluded (FR-19, FR-39, NFR-06, DR-11); coverage; agreement by difficulty stratum and the disagreement list (FR-28, NFR-14); and blind ratings (FR-29). Occupation ratings are re-\allowbreak{}aggregated in the transaction that stores each rating (FR-19); heavier statistics refresh periodically, later from a replica. It never returns an individual's record. Prototype: personal summaries only.

Operations. Telemetry of usage, cost, latency, and errors (DR-10); failed jobs and re-runs (FR-43); versioned settings, including the cost ceiling (DR-17); and feedback review (FR-20). Prototype: absent.

Notification, scheduler, and import. Notification sends email and in-app notices: reset links, review assignments, reminders, play-test invitations. The scheduler runs recurring jobs: review reminders and overdue notices (FR-34), annual revalidation (BR-15), retention, refreshing statistics, and the reconciliation that re-queues any narration or scoring request left without a result. The importer loads O*NET and ESCO and lists unresolved records (FR-31). Prototype: absent.

Data stores. The operational database is the system of record: relational tables for accounts, assignments, runs, events, evidence, reviews, ratings, and logs, and documents for content. The queue store holds jobs, rate limits, budget counters, and the publish--subscribe channel feeding event streams --- nothing that cannot be rebuilt from the database. The object store holds assets and exports. Prototype: the database, with 19 tables.

The assessed turn. A free-text answer in Perform scenario tests the architecture hardest, because it involves the explorer, the authority over scores, the models, and the failure of any of them. The answer is graded first, by the three judges (AI-5); only then does the narrator decide whether to ask a follow-up (AI-4), and each follow-up answer goes through the same loop. The figure below shows the sequence for one activity.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth,height=0.9\textheight,keepaspectratio]{figures/SD-01_Explorer_AnswerActivity.png}
\caption{Sequence of one activity (SD-01): the answer stored before any model call, grading by three judges in parallel, the follow-up decided after the grade, the activity result pushed to the explorer, and the hidden feedback note drafted and checked}
\end{figure}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em]
  \item Submission: the explorer application keeps a local copy of the answer and sends it with the run's next sequence number and an idempotency key, so a retry cannot record it twice (NFR-03, NFR-13). Exploration checks it with \textit{game-\allowbreak{}core}, and Assessment stores it with an assessment request in one transaction and enqueues the job directly after commit --- a transactional outbox\cite{r165} guarantees a job for every stored answer --- before replying that the answer is saved. Progress shows at once, and a status line after two seconds (NFR-01).
  \item Grading: a worker removes identifying details and sends the answer, as delimited data the models are instructed never to obey, to three judges of three families in parallel, at most three criteria per call. Each judge returns, per criterion, a verbatim quotation, a rationale, and a level; a judgment is kept only if the quotation occurs in what the player typed (FR-11, NFR-17). The judges' scores are combined into one grade, judges more than one level apart flag the case for the expert (AI-3), and each judge's score, the grade, and the cost are stored (DR-07).
  \item Follow-up: with the grade known, the narrator is sent the graded exchange and the seed, without the rubric or scoring keys, and returns a follow-up or none. A follow-up is shown only if it passes the rule checks --- numbers and names from the seed or the player's text, follow-ups within the count the engine allows --- and otherwise, or on a provider error, the expert's default follow-up is shown (FR-12, NFR-15). It reaches the explorer through Redis and the event stream, and its answer returns to step 1.
  \item Result: once no further follow-up is asked, \textit{game-core} applies the hint cap and the credit within the cap of BR-11; the activity result is stored as an event with its provenance, and Redis carries it to the API replica holding the explorer's stream, which shows the NPC's closing line. Each event carries its sequence number, so a reconnecting stream replays whatever it missed from the database.
  \item Feedback note: a further job asks the narrator for a hidden feedback note built from the rubric anchors, checks it, and redrafts it until it passes; the note is stored with its check result (FR-14, DR-07). At the end of the scenario the notes are combined, and result, feedback, and guidance appear as separate parts on the summary screen.
  \item Failure: the gateway retries, then switches to another model in the same role: another narrator model for narration, and for a judge the other tier of the same family, so that the panel keeps three families (DC-01); this is still AI (DC-02). If every judge fails, the job is marked as failed and the explorer receives a delay notice within ten seconds (NFR-04); the session pauses with every answer and all progress saved, play resumes when a model is available again, and pages that do not use AI, such as sign-in, the map, and the record, stay available. The scheduler re-queues any request left without a result, so not even loss of Redis loses an answer.
\end{enumerate}

A closed-\allowbreak{}format answer skips steps 2 and 3: the client shows \textit{game-\allowbreak{}core}'s score at once, the server recomputes it from the stored event, and the feedback note is still drafted for the activity. Resuming after an interruption (flow 7c) folds the stored events and returns the state at the last recorded position on any device.

Latency budget. Under the Lighthouse mobile profile\cite{r137}, the platform's own share of each model step is about 0.4 s: a 150 ms round trip to submit, 20 ms for the transaction, 10 ms to enqueue, 50 ms of preparation and checks, 20 ms to store the result, and half a round trip to push it. That leaves about 7.6 s of NFR-02's eight seconds for the judging panel, and about 4.6 s of NFR-01's five seconds for the narrator to write a follow-up, the budgets against which Section 7.3 measures candidate models; because the panel waits for all three judges, its time is that of the slowest. Since a follow-up is decided only after the answer is graded, the explorer's wait for a follow-up after a free-text answer includes both steps; whether NFR-01 counts from the submission or from the grade is to be settled with those measurements. Generated text is not streamed token by token, because it must pass the rule checks before display.

Infrastructure and scalability. JobQuest's load is not where web systems usually feel it: the binding constraint is the model tier, not the web or database tier. The estimate below states its assumptions so pilots can revise them. A scenario lasts ten to fifteen minutes (Section 4.1.2) and is assumed to hold three situations, each scored by three judges, plus about four narration and follow-up calls: about thirteen model calls per scenario, or one per minute of play. An active explorer sends a request every five to ten seconds. For Stage 4 the reference is the 1.12 million grade-12 candidates of 2025\cite{r167}: if a fifth of a cohort plays half an hour a week over a ten-week guidance season, concentrated in about twenty peak hours, peak concurrency is about five thousand.

Appendix 3 gives the assumed users, request rates, and topology of each stage.

Each tier scales by its own mechanism, summarised below for Stage 4.

The in-flight figure follows from Little's law: 85 calls per second, each lasting three to eight seconds. Two sizing assumptions remain to be measured by the load tests of Chapter 7: if one single-vCPU API replica serves about 300 light requests per second, Stage 4 needs four replicas, six with headroom across two zones; if one worker holds 50 calls in flight, it needs six to fourteen workers.

The architecture scales for seven reasons. Processing tiers are stateless, so capacity grows by adding replicas. Fast and slow work are separated: a request stores and acknowledges in about 0.2 s, and slow work waits in a queue, so a class of forty starting together lengthens the queue rather than overloading the API. The binding constraint, the model tier, is isolated and limited on its own. Published content is immutable and therefore almost entirely served from cache. Reads for statistics are separated from writes by a replica. Load is shed by priority rather than by failure: under pressure, live grading and narration go first, feedback notes wait a little longer for the summary screen, and authoring work waits for quiet hours. And no transaction spans services, so growth never requires distributed coordination. Canon content is approved once and served verbatim from releases, so run-time model calls are limited to narration, follow-ups, feedback notes, and grading; the cost per playthrough is estimated in Section 5.2.3.

Reliability and operations. Availability of 99.5\% a month is proposed for Stages 1 and 2 and 99.9\% once two zones run (NFR-18). The write-ahead log is archived continuously and backups taken nightly, both copied to a second region in Vietnam, for a proposed recovery point of five minutes and recovery time of one hour. Deployments roll replica by replica behind health checks, with backward-\allowbreak{}compatible migrations so old and new versions run side by side. Monitoring tracks queue length, 95th-\allowbreak{}percentile latency per provider, cost per session, and error rates, alerting at the thresholds of NFR-01, NFR-02, and NFR-11. A web application firewall and TLS guard the entry, secrets are held in a secret manager, and the egress proxy admits only allowlisted model endpoints.

From the prototype to the target. Four prototype mechanisms are kept: rules in a framework-\allowbreak{}free \textit{game-core} shared by client and server; the server as score authority; content in versioned, immutable releases pinned to each run; and bearer-\allowbreak{}token sessions with rotating refresh tokens. Six changes reach the target: free-text assessment becomes an asynchronous job whose result enters the run as an event; runs are submitted turn by turn (NFR-03); content is written through the lifecycle module instead of a seed script; three roles become four occupation-\allowbreak{}scoped actors; the staff application, AI orchestration, gateway, queue, workers, and scheduler are added; and the accessible 2D map is added. Each change extends a prototype component along a boundary it already has; none discards one.

\subsubsection{Sequence Diagrams}

The sequence diagrams below show how the components of Section 5.3.1 cooperate in the main flows of each actor; the assessed turn (SD-01) is shown in Section 5.3.1. Lifelines are the actors, the two applications, the modules, the data stores, and the external services; a solid arrow is a call and a dashed arrow a reply or a pushed event. All diagrams follow the conventions of SD-01: a request is stored in PostgreSQL before slow work is queued in Redis, so the reply comes at once; AI orchestration takes model work from the queue and reaches the providers only through the model gateway; results reach the open application through Redis and the result stream rather than by polling; and each state change of a scenario is written with its log entry, following SM-01. Of the twelve diagrams, SD-01 (Section 5.3.1), SD-05, and SD-10 are shown here, because they carry the central mechanisms: the assessed turn, the AI-assisted authoring, and the shift turn. The other nine (SD-02, SD-03, SD-04, SD-06 to SD-09, SD-11, SD-12) are in Appendix 2, each with its description.

Access the platform. The explorer application is a static bundle served by the content delivery network. Sign-in takes one of three paths: a password, checked against the stored hash; Google, for which Identity and access exchanges the authorization code for an ID token and verifies it before finding or creating the account; or registration. Wrong details or a disabled account end the flow with an error (FR-01, FR-41). Otherwise a session is started and the sign-in logged, and the reply says whether the Get-\allowbreak{}to-\allowbreak{}know-\allowbreak{}me quiz is done, which decides between the quiz and the Galaxy Map.

Get-\allowbreak{}to-\allowbreak{}know-\allowbreak{}me quiz. The questions are loaded once and the answers kept by the application until submitted. Exploration computes the RIASEC profile and social index, picks the five occupations whose interest codes are closest and the portrait of the top type, and labels the result as self-\allowbreak{}reported; no model is called (FR-03, FR-04). The explorer then opens a suggested occupation or browses the Galaxy Map.

Finish a scenario. If the hidden feedback notes of the last activities are still being written, the explorer sees progress, and the summary is requested again when AI orchestration announces the notes ready. Exploration then totals the activity scores, picks the ending, combines the notes, and caps the competency points (FR-15); with enough credit it unlocks the next level, otherwise it picks practice tasks (FR-08). Progress, history, and badges are stored before the ending, feedback, and guidance are shown (FR-14), and the explorer returns to the Island Map or opens the profile.

Generate scenario content. The flow has three parts. The content administrator opens a scenario for a task; Content lifecycle refuses if the task already has an active scenario (FR-33), and otherwise creates an empty one, to which the administrator assigns an author and a reviewer with a review deadline (FR-34); the author is emailed through a queued notification. The author chooses situation options and writes the context; the generation job is stored and queued, and AI orchestration calls the model through the gateway, checks the output, stores the generation record, and pushes the content to the staff application (FR-23). Each revision the author requests of a part follows the same path and is kept in the generation history. The author then sets task types and option scores, checks the rubric anchors, adds sample answers, and writes hints and events (FR-22, FR-24); the automatic content checks must pass before the scenario can be submitted, which sets it submitted and emails the reviewer.

Review a scenario. Identity and access refuses a reviewer who is the scenario's author or is not assigned to it. Opening the scenario sets it in review. The reviewer answers each review criterion and then rejects, requests changes, or approves; a decision with a criterion unanswered is refused (FR-25, FR-32). Rejection retires the scenario, a request for changes returns it to draft with the comments, and approval sets it approved. The outcome is pushed to the author's open staff application and emailed to the author and the content administrator.

Gamify and play-test. The content administrator asks for presentation ideas; AI orchestration sends the approved content and the allowed task types to a model and returns suggested task types, characters, and backgrounds, storing their cost. The administrator's choice of task types is checked against the set the author allowed (FR-35). A missing asset is generated by the image model, checked for bias, stored in object storage, and added to the asset library (FR-36). Confirming the presentation builds the draft game and invites the author to play it; the staff application runs \textit{game-core} locally, and each answer follows SD-01 in a session flagged as a play-test, so it is excluded from statistics. The author's findings are pushed to the administrator: a display issue returns the scenario to presentation, a content issue returns it to drafting, and confirmation that the content is final sets it ready (FR-26).

Publish a scenario. Content lifecycle refuses to publish a scenario that is not ready. Otherwise it compares the release with the approved content, stores the release by version in object storage and the locked release in PostgreSQL, and sets it published at once or keeps it ready until the scheduler publishes it on the effective date (FR-37). When an explorer then starts the main quest, Exploration checks the status and loads any saved position; an unavailable scenario returns the explorer to the Island Map. The explorer application fetches the release by its version through the content delivery network, which reads object storage only when the file is not cached, and shows the scenario introduction with its optional context (FR-09).

Import reference data. The uploaded O*NET and ESCO files are stored in object storage and an import job is queued. The importer reads the files, looks up the source identifiers in O*NET and ESCO, matches the records, stores matched and unmatched records, and pushes the result; the content administrator fixes or skips each unmatched record (FR-31) and then adapts the data to Vietnamese practice. The adapted data is stored with its log entry, and the occupation's domain experts are emailed.

Play one turn of a shift. The explorer acts, says something, chooses, asks for a hint, or lets time run out; the application computes the provisional state with the shared rules and submits the turn with a client-\allowbreak{}generated identifier. The play module locks the run, appends the turn to the event log in one transaction, and enqueues a job, or returns the earlier result if the identifier was already recorded; the response is ``accepted'' and the screen shows ``saved, assessing''. AI orchestration takes the job, folds the events through the shared rules to find what the engine is waiting for, asks the model gateway for a narrated beat or a yes-or-no judgement on de-\allowbreak{}identified text, appends the results with their provenance, and publishes them on the event stream. When a segment of the shift closes, the segment is graded against its checklist by the judge seats; with too few judges the answer is kept pending and a provider-\allowbreak{}delay event tells the screen to show a wait message (NFR-04, D-108).

\subsubsection{AI Model Design}

JobQuest does not design, train, or fine-tune a model (Section 5.2.3). This section designs how existing language models are composed with rules, data, and expert oversight into the AI components of the platform. Only its structure is fixed here: each part is written once its content has been verified, and the parts that depend on the game design stay parameterised until the game design is fixed. The parts fall into four groups: the overview (parts 1 and 2), the components and their data (parts 3 to 6), scoring and model calls (parts 7 to 10), and operation (parts 11 to 15).

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em]
  \item Design principles: the principles that decide which decisions a model takes and which a rule takes.
  \item End-to-end flow: one figure of the authoring, play, and review loops, and of the model gateway that every call passes through.
  \item The AI components: the contract of each of AI-1 to AI-5, namely when it runs, what it receives and returns, how its output is checked, and what happens on failure.
  \item Feedback writer: how the result of a judgment becomes feedback the explorer can use.
  \item Other free text: how every other free-text answer in the game reaches the same grader.
  \item Data contracts: the seed that experts author and the judgment that the graders return, with what is stored and versioned.
  \item Scoring and aggregation: how the levels given by the judges are combined into a grade and a competency score, with the parameters left open until the game design is fixed.
  \item Edge cases: invalid output, an empty answer, hints, and a missing judge.
  \item Model call configuration and prompts: the parameters of each kind of call, their translation to the API of each provider, the system prompt, and prompt caching.
  \item Run-time rules of AI-4: the checks applied to the narrator's text before the explorer sees it.
  \item Privacy and safety: de-\allowbreak{}identification and the defence against prompt injection; the policy on third parties is in Section 5.3.10.
  \item Failure behaviour: the sequence of fallbacks when a call fails, and the idea of a local model as the last fallback, which is not implemented and is to be shown in the component and deployment diagrams.
  \item Cost and time: the cost of a playthrough and the time budget of each call.
  \item Feedback loop and versions: how expert rulings improve the rubrics and the calibration examples, and how versions of models, prompts, and rubrics are tracked.
  \item Deferred AI functions: the AI functions left for a later phase and where they attach to the design.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Test Case Design}

The test plan covers the whole data model of Section 5.3.5, including parts that have no code yet. It tests against the design, not against the prototype, whose database does not follow the data model. The plan is a separate document, \texttt{docs/\allowbreak{}plans/\allowbreak{}2026-\allowbreak{}11-\allowbreak{}20\_\allowbreak{}testing-\allowbreak{}plan.\allowbreak{}md}, with twelve files of test cases, one per group of the data model; this section gives its strategy, coverage, levels, and the cases still blocked, and Appendix 5 gives all 187 cases as test scenarios with step-\allowbreak{}by-\allowbreak{}step tables. It is a proposal that waits for the team's approval.

Principles. Four rules shape every test case.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
  \item Each case has one of four states. \textit{Runnable}: the code already follows the design. \textit{Awaiting implementation}: the design is fixed but the code is missing or differs, so the case is written first as an acceptance criterion. \textit{Blocked, awaiting decision}: it rests on an entry of the data model or the research still marked as to be decided, so only the aim and the reason are recorded. \textit{Blocked, contradiction}: two sources disagree and the research has not ruled; none remains after 20 November.
  \item Where a number is not yet fixed (shop prices, health recovery, the cost of replaying a scenario, the factor by which a hint lowers a score), the test checks the relation (a ceiling, never negative, never falling), not the value.
  \item There is no test path without AI (D-92). The fake model (\texttt{AI\_\allowbreak{}MODE=fake}) checks the flow only: queue, events, failover, provenance. The quality of grading is measured by the method of Section 7.3 and is not repeated here.
  \item Where research and an older requirement disagree, cases follow the research and say so. Twelve such readings (for example that side quests are not graded, and that several scenarios may share a task) are listed in the plan; they are a way of reading for writing tests, not decisions of the team.
\end{itemize}

Levels and tools. Each case names its level in the plan: unit (pure functions and services, Vitest and Jest), integration (API with PostgreSQL, Redis, and the worker, using the fake model), system and end-to-end (flows in a browser, accessibility and performance audits; the browser test tool is not chosen yet), and acceptance (review by experts and users, manual). The table of levels, the assignment of use cases to groups, the 38 white-box targets, and the eleven cases blocked by a decision of the team are in Appendix 5.

Coverage criteria, proposed. No project sets a coverage threshold yet and the continuous integration does not measure it. The plan proposes branch coverage of at least 85\% for the pure functions of \textit{game-core} and 75\% for the multi-\allowbreak{}branch services of the API, 90\% for the model gateway and the judges, MC/DC for the guards of SM-01 and the rules of unlocking, overtime, and buffs, and one def--use case for each of the 27 derived attributes. Mutation testing is optional.

Entry and exit, and defects. A round of testing starts when the design of the part is fixed or its cases are marked blocked, the environment is ready, and the fixtures exist. It ends when every high-\allowbreak{}priority case of the group has run and passed, no high-\allowbreak{}severity defect is open, and coverage meets the threshold for the part that has code; cases still awaiting implementation are listed and do not count as passed. A defect is recorded with the case, the group, a severity (high when progress is lost, a score is wrong, personal data leaks, or an invariant is broken), the steps, and the design source it breaks. A defect that shows a missing or wrong design is not mended by changing the design to fit; it goes to the list of open issues.

Environment and priority. Tests of the database run on a local PostgreSQL, never on the shared database of the project. The plan proposes a PostgreSQL and Redis service in the continuous integration, a browser test tool (a new dependency), data factories for the entities, and coverage in the build; each needs the team's agreement. Cases are written and run in this order: groups F, J, and E first, since they have code and carry the highest risk; then A and L (access, cost, failover); then H, I, C, D, and G, which depend on research not yet connected to the runtime; and K last, whose cases serve as acceptance criteria until it is built. Two risks are named: the schema changes to follow the data model, which would break integration tests written now, so they are written at the level of behaviour with data access behind an interface; and the grading by models, which does not repeat exactly, so it is measured by agreement (Section 7.3) and not by comparing strings.

\subsubsection{Policies for the Processing of the Proposed System and Third Parties}

This section sets the rules under which JobQuest processes personal data and shares any data with third parties, completing the policies of Section 4.1.4. Its central problem is a conflict between two parts of the design. The system of record must be in Vietnam (DC-03), as Vietnam's Law on Personal Data Protection and its implementing decree require\cite{r99,r100}; but scoring and narration depend on language models whose providers all process requests outside Vietnam. Under the law, using a platform abroad to process data collected in Vietnam is a transfer of personal data abroad, whichever region the provider is asked to use. The policies below decide what may cross that border, under what conditions, and what the platform owes its users when it does.

Options for using foreign models. Four ways to reconcile scoring by language models with data kept in Vietnam were compared.

\textbf{Decision: de-identify before any model call, send only what the task needs, and treat what is sent as a transfer of personal data regardless.\allowbreak{}} Removing identifiers is what makes the transfer small; treating it as a transfer anyway is what makes the design safe if de-\allowbreak{}identification of free text misses something. The law treats data that no longer identifies a person as outside its scope, but a free-text answer can name a colleague, a company, or a place, and the platform cannot prove that every such detail is caught, so it does not rely on that exemption. Models served in Vietnam would remove the transfer altogether; they are recorded as later work, starting from the local fallback of Section 5.3.6, once their scoring quality can be measured against the same thresholds.

What may leave the country. The egress proxy admits a request only to an allowlisted model endpoint, and the request may contain only three things: the seed and rubric of the scenario, which are expert content and contain no personal data; the explorer's answer text after de-\allowbreak{}identification; and an opaque run identifier that maps to the explorer only inside the platform. Names, usernames, e-mail addresses, dates of birth, account identifiers, and profiles never leave. De-\allowbreak{}identification removes the explorer's own name, username, and e-mail address, which the platform knows, and any text that matches patterns for e-mail addresses, Vietnamese phone and identity-\allowbreak{}card numbers, and links. Scenarios address the explorer by role and give every other character a name, which reduces the reasons to write a real person's name; the remaining risk is stated in the privacy notice. The proxy logs every request's time, endpoint, size, and run identifier, but not its content, so that what left the country can be accounted for without keeping a second copy of it.

Conditions on model providers. A provider is admitted to the model registry only when four conditions are confirmed in writing, in its terms or a data-\allowbreak{}processing agreement: submitted data is not used to train or improve models; retention is the shortest the provider offers, with zero retention where it is offered; data is processed only to answer the request; and the provider notifies a change of these terms. Provider-\allowbreak{}side features that store requests for evaluation or fine-tuning are switched off. A provider whose terms stop meeting the conditions is removed from the registry, and because every provider sits behind the model port (DC-02), removal is a change of configuration, not of code.

Obligations to users and to the authority. The platform treats the calls to providers abroad as a transfer of personal data and meets the law's conditions for one\cite{r99,r100}. Before an explorer's first scenario, the privacy notice names the providers, the countries, the data sent, and the purpose, and the explorer consents to it; an explorer who declines can still use the parts of the platform that make no model call, such as the occupation map and occupational profiles. A transfer impact assessment is prepared and filed with the competent authority of the Ministry of Public Security within the period the law sets after the first transfer, and updated when a provider is added or removed; a data protection impact assessment covers the processing as a whole. Account deletion removes the explorer's records from the platform; copies held by providers end with their retention period, which is why the shortest retention is a condition of admission.

Other third parties. Google sign-in is optional and receives nothing beyond what the user approves at Google; an account can be used with a password only. The e-mail provider receives only the address and the message. The content delivery network serves files that contain no personal data, so its edge locations abroad raise no transfer.

Open questions for the legal office. Three points are to be confirmed with the university's legal office before the first real users, in Stage 1: whether a university project counts as a controller under the law, or the university does; whether logs of play behaviour fall within the decree's sensitive categories, which would add obligations; and whether de-\allowbreak{}identified answer text, as specified above, is still personal data in the authority's reading. None of the answers changes the architecture: the egress path, the de-\allowbreak{}identification, and the registry of providers are the same either way, and only the paperwork differs.
