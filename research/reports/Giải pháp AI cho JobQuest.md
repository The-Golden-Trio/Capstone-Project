# Evidence supports JobQuest's AI design, pending measurement

JobQuest's five AI components can be defended from published evidence as a structure: a human-authored interview guide with model probing inside each topic (AI-1); section-by-section drafting of the seed with deterministic validation and expert sign-off on every key and anchor (AI-2); simulated plus human playthroughs triaged by a union of signals and audited by random sample (AI-3); an engine-owned plot in which the model only fills gaps from a seed held whole in the prompt (AI-4); and reference-and-rubric scoring by three judges from different model families (AI-5). What the evidence does not supply is any measurement in the project's own setting. No source in the research notes covers IT occupations, Vietnamese, a four-level ordinal rubric applied to short free-text answers, or volunteer experts, so every figure quoted below was found in another setting and reaches JobQuest by inference. Three fixed decisions are put in doubt by specific findings and should be carried into Stage A as hypotheses: scoring all competencies in one call per judge, taking the panel median in preference to the best single judge, and flagging for review only the answers on which judges differ by more than one level. The NFR-14 target of QWK ≥ 0.70 lies above every verified average for prompted LLM scoring on the standard English datasets (about 0.31–0.47 on ASAP-AES, 0.26–0.68 on ASAP-SAS), so meeting it rests on design features whose benefit is itself untested. On the cross-cutting questions, "AI-native" has no standard definition and the defensible claim is a counterfactual one, paired with designed degradation per component; four model families follow from DC-01 and are affordable at small-tier list prices read on 8 October 2026; and sending a player's answer to a foreign model API is, on the face of Law 91/2025/QH15 as read in an unofficial reproduction, a cross-border transfer whose risk de-identification reduces but does not remove. The report ends with the measurement that would close each gap and with the questions for the advisor and the university's legal office.

## AI-native means the core loop collapses without the model

### No standard defines the term, so the report must name the definition it adopts

Every serious source opened says the term is unsettled. ITU-R Recommendation M.2160-0 uses "AI-native" for the IMT-2030 air interface and radio network without defining it, and its glossary has no entry for the term ([ITU-R M.2160-0, standard](https://www.itu.int/dms_pubrec/itu-r/rec/m/R-REC-M.2160-0-202311-I!!PDF-E.pdf)). No 3GPP or ETSI normative definition was found. The definition that other bodies cite is Ericsson's: AI capabilities are intrinsic and trustworthy, AI is a natural part of functionality across design, deployment, operation and maintenance, and the system consumes and produces data and knowledge; the same paper states the negative criterion that adding or replacing functions with AI in some or all components does not by itself make an implementation AI-native ([Ericsson white paper, vendor, undated; published between November 2022 and September 2023](https://www.ericsson.com/en/reports-and-papers/white-papers/ai-native)). The ITU-T focus group on AI-native networks describes the paradigm as one in which AI is embedded in the core architecture and is not an add-on ([ITU-T FG-AINN, focus-group page](https://www.itu.int/en/ITU-T/focusgroups/ainn/Pages/default.aspx)), and the O-RAN research report contrasts native AI with AI used as an additional offline component of a network function ([O-RAN nGRG RR-2023-03, non-normative report](https://mediastorage.o-ran.org/ngrg-rr/nGRG-RR-2023-03-Research-Report-on-Native-and-Cross-domain-AI-v1_1.pdf)).

Two software sources bring the term closer to an application. A grey-literature review finds that AI-native applications still lack a unified engineering definition and distinguishes them by two pillars, the central role of AI and an inherently probabilistic nature, in contrast with AI-assisted applications that add AI features to a deterministic workflow ([Cao et al., arXiv:2509.13144, preprint](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.13144)). A survey of 53 AI-native games gives the most testable criterion: a game is AI-native when run-time generative AI is "constitutive of the core loop", so that removing or trivially replacing the AI makes the central form of play collapse or change fundamentally; the same survey adds that generation alone does not make a game AI-native and that such designs depend on fixed goals, rules, state, feedback, pacing and player agency ([Xu et al., arXiv:2607.00527, preprint](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.00527)).

The definitions conflict, and the report should say so. A stricter systems definition reserves the term for systems in which an AI autonomously rewrites the system's own implementation, and requires an escalation detector, a verification procedure and a verified fallback ([Tan, arXiv:2607.21659, preprint](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.21659)); under that definition **JobQuest is not AI-native**. "AI-native software engineering" describes how software is built, not what the product does, and citing it for a product claim would be a category error ([Hassan et al., arXiv:2410.06107, preprint](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.06107)). The defensible position is to adopt the counterfactual core-loop criterion of Xu et al. together with Ericsson's lifecycle clause, and to state that these anchors are a preprint and a vendor white paper, that the telecom sources transfer only by analogy, and that no peer-reviewed software-engineering definition was found.

### Five testable criteria, stated as the team's own operationalisation

The criteria below are derived from the sources above by the research team. They are an operationalisation, not a published standard, and each row names the evidence that would show the criterion is met.

| Test | Criterion and its source | Evidence JobQuest would present |
|---|---|---|
| T1 Ablation | Counterfactual dependence (Xu et al.; Ericsson's negative criterion) | With all model calls disabled, list which use cases still complete. The claim holds only if the core loop (free-text answer, fitted follow-up, per-competency score, unlock) cannot be delivered or is fundamentally different. The current no-AI prototype with two hand-written scenarios and keyword grading is the ablation baseline. |
| T2 Lifecycle coverage | AI present in design, deployment, operation and maintenance (Ericsson) | A stage-by-component table: content design (AI-1, AI-2), verification before release (AI-3), operation (AI-4, AI-5), maintenance (scheduled re-scoring of a frozen benchmark, drift checks). |
| T3 Data loop | Data and knowledge consumed and produced (Ericsson) | Seeds, expert keys, flagged disagreements and expert rulings feed the benchmark and prompt revisions. |
| T4 AI-aware control | Model lifecycle management and monitoring (Ericsson's level-1 bar as reported on its page; reliability and AI-specific observability in Cao et al.) | DC-02 (replaceable provider), versioned prompts and pinned models, a per-call audit record, monitoring of judge agreement. |
| T5 Mechanical invariants | Fixed rules, state and feedback around a probabilistic core (Xu et al.; Cao et al.) | Engine-controlled canon order, verbatim canon text and deterministic run-time checks (D-89). |

T5 matters for the argument: tight engine control is what the games survey says AI-native design depends on, so D-89 is consistent with the claim and is not evidence against it. A per-component view is also supported by a taxonomy that characterises each LLM component of an application separately ([Weber, arXiv:2406.10300, preprint, abstract only](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.10300)); on that view AI-4 and AI-5 are constitutive at run time, while AI-1, AI-2 and AI-3 are authoring and verification aids. Ericsson's maturity model sets a precedent for a checklist claim across independent dimensions in place of a yes-or-no label, although its per-level text could not be read because it is an image on the page.

### What still works when the provider fails, component by component

The advisor's two demands, AI-native and still running when the AI is down, are compatible once "runs" is defined as in the AWS reliability guidance: a component keeps its most critical function and a predictable, recoverable state when a dependency is unavailable, possibly serving alternate data or none ([AWS Well-Architected REL05-BP01, official guidance](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/wellarchitected/latest/reliability-pillar/rel_mitigate_interaction_failure_graceful_degradation.html)). Under the counterfactual criterion a system whose core loop works unchanged without AI would not be AI-native at all. The honest claim (an inference recorded in the notes) is therefore that without the models JobQuest cannot deliver its core value, yet it fails safely, loses no committed progress (NFR-04), keeps authored content available and completes deferred work when the provider returns. A designed fallback is treated as part of AI-native design in Tan's definition and in the ETSI ZSM management services, which list ML fallback management (read only through the O-RAN report).

The size of the risk is documented for other providers and an earlier period. A peer-reviewed study of public status pages up to 31 August 2024 reports a mean time between reported incidents of 5.64 days for the OpenAI API and 5.22 days for the Anthropic API, mean recovery of 2.56 and 2.81 hours (medians 1.23 and 0.77 hours), over 90% of incidents ending within 10 hours, and no correlation between services of different providers ([Chu et al., ICPE 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.12469v1)). The data are self-reported, count partial degradation as incidents, and do not cover Google or access from Vietnam. Carried to JobQuest by inference: a 10–15 minute session will sometimes overlap an incident, and with three independent judge providers the chance that at least one is degraded is higher than for one provider, which is why a quorum rule matters.

| Component | Dependency type (notes' classification) | Behaviour when the model provider fails | What still works |
|---|---|---|---|
| AI-1 elicitation | Soft for players; offline, staff-facing | Persist the partial transcript, retry with backoff, resume later | No live session is touched; the human-authored topic guide remains usable by a human interviewer (this report's inference) |
| AI-2 structuring | Soft for players; offline | Persist drafts, retry, resume | Deterministic schema validators and manual editing by the Content Administrator |
| AI-3 testing and triage | Soft for players; offline | Queue playthrough generation and offline checks | Review of already-collected cases and random-sample audits |
| AI-4 narration | Partly soft | Continue with verbatim canon events in engine order and seed hints; connecting narration and fitted follow-ups are unavailable | The session continues, in a form closer to the non-AI prototype |
| AI-5 scoring | Hard for the product's value, but deferrable | Store the answer durably, queue scoring, complete asynchronously; a two-of-three quorum is a candidate degraded mode | Progress is kept; the score and feedback arrive later, labelled as deferred |

Three design rules follow from the guidance. Timeouts, retries with exponential backoff, circuit breakers and queues for deferrable work are the common pattern set of the AWS, Google Cloud and Microsoft architecture frameworks ([AWS Generative AI Lens GENREL03-BP01](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/wellarchitected/latest/generative-ai-lens/genrel03-bp01.html); [Google Cloud AI/ML reliability](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/framework/perspectives/ai-ml/reliability); [Azure gateway guide](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/architecture/ai-ml/guide/azure-openai-gateway-guide); all official guidance). AWS adds that failure paths must be simpler than the primary path, must be tested, and that fallback strategies should generally be avoided; combined with RP-2 this argues against silently substituting keyword matching during an outage, because an unvalidated scorer would write into the competency record. Deferring the score is the safer degraded mode. Whether a two-judge result meets NFR-14 is an empirical question that Stage A can answer by scoring with each pair of judges.

No study was found that measures how users react to an AI feature being unavailable or to a disclosed degraded mode. The nearest evidence is that explaining a chatbot's delay raised perceived transparency and trust in one of two conditions in a 194-participant study ([Zhang et al., CUI 2024, publication page only](https://research.tudelft.nl/en/publications/explaining-the-wait-how-justifying-chatbot-response-delays-impact/)), and that design guidance asks systems to state their limits and give users a way forward ([Amershi et al., CHI 2019](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/guidelines-for-human-ai-interaction/); [Google PAIR Guidebook, vendor design guidance](https://pair.withgoogle.com/chapter/errors-failing/)). The design suggestion that follows, to tell the player the answer is saved and feedback will arrive later, is untested.

The observation from these pages is that products which generate freely do not assess, and products which need accuracy avoid generation; the cell JobQuest intends to occupy is not occupied by any product whose page was opened. Among research prototypes for career exploration, the LLM is a generator of exploratory narrative, and the one evaluation that could be read is a within-subjects self-report study with 24 participants: engagement and satisfaction were significantly higher than with a plain chatbot, perceived authenticity was not (4.04 against 3.75, p = 0.22), and 6 of 24 participants found the generated timeline inconsistent ([CareerPooler, preprint](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.11461v2)). Read by analogy, not as a tested result, those two weaknesses are what expert-seeded content and engine-controlled order are meant to prevent. The games survey also reports that AI judging of player input is among the less represented categories in its corpus ([Xu et al., preprint](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.00527)).

The nearest research precedent for model-based competency scoring inside a serious game comes from another domain: a financial-literacy game for school pupils in which an LLM labels each action on a four-point rubric validated against three domain experts (Fleiss kappa 0.624) and feeds per-competency knowledge tracing, with mastery estimates correlating with learning gain at r = 0.276 against r = 0.095 for a single-LLM baseline ([Santos et al., arXiv:2606.25358; IEEE CoG 2026 per the authors' arXiv comment; listing page only](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.25358)). A second capstone under the same advisor, aBridgeAI (HCMUT, September 2026; unpublished, about 30 of 493 pages read by the coordinator), selects its quiz-generation model by measurement with an independent judge model and bootstrap intervals, keeps assessment authority away from the conversational model, and states as a limitation that its judge was not validated against humans. JobQuest's planned agreement study is the step that precedent leaves open.

## Five components, each chosen against requirement-derived criteria

Each subsection states the problem, compares the alternatives against criteria drawn from the project's requirements, gives the choice and its support, and names the remaining risks and the verification. The ratings in the comparison tables are this report's judgement from the evidence cited in the text beneath each table.

### AI-1: a fixed topic guide with model probing, because under-probing is the documented failure

**Problem.** A seed needs incidents, concrete behaviours, required facts, effectiveness keys and rubric anchors from three or four volunteer senior practitioners per role, each giving about seven hours in Stage 1, and the input of several experts has to be combined without erasing their disagreements. The criteria follow from those facts: expert time, coverage of the seed's fields, concreteness of what is collected, absence of invented content with traceable provenance, preserved disagreement, Vietnamese and English (NFR-08), and testability in Stage A, where no real expert is available.

| Alternative | Expert time and scale | Coverage of seed fields | Concrete, non-inflated content | No invention, provenance | Testable in Stage A |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A. Human-led interviews only | Team time grows with every expert and role | Depends on interviewer skill | High with a trained interviewer | High | Low |
| B. Static written questionnaire | Low burden | Fixed, no probing | Low; no follow-up on vague answers | High | Medium |
| C. Free LLM conversation without a guide | Low team time | Uncontrolled | Uncontrolled | Low; documented inventions | Medium |
| **D. Human-authored guide, LLM probing within each topic, engine-side coverage and follow-up rules, summary confirmed by the expert (chosen)** | Low team time; expert time bounded by the guide | Tracked per seed field | Guided by incident-first, behaviour-first prompts | Verbatim spans linked to every seed entry | High, with a simulated respondent |

**Choice and reasoning.** The strongest evidence that an LLM can hold an interview to a guide comes from two general-population studies: across 766 AI-led interviews and more than 8,000 hand-coded question–answer pairs, 94.8% and 99.8% of questions were open-ended, 95.7% and 99.6% neutral, and 0.1% and 0.4% hallucinated, and each participant was shown a generated summary to confirm ([Chopra and Haaland, CESifo Working Paper 10666, working paper, 2026 revision](https://www.cesifo.org/DocDL/cesifo1_wp10666.pdf)). That study used a simple fixed guide with lay respondents; it does not test interviews with domain experts. A conversational survey with about 600 participants found that follow-up questions elicited more informative and specific answers than a static form, which is the case against alternative B ([Xiao et al., ACM TOCHI 2020](https://doi.org/10.1145/3381804)).

The case against alternative C, and for engine-side rules in D, comes from the failures on record. In a small controlled comparison of five AI-led and six human-led interviews, 88% of violations of the instruction to follow up on unclear or surprising answers were committed by the AI interviewer, which also occasionally judged respondents; a prompt change that had passed internal tests caused the under-probing ([Wuttke et al., LaTeCH-CLfL 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.01824)). A requirements-elicitation interviewer fully elicited at most 60.94% of requirements (73.7% including partial), invented a project price in one interview and asked for an e-mail address in another ([Korn et al., LLMREI, arXiv:2507.02564, preprint](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.02564)). In an evaluation environment with simulated users, seven LLMs elicited less than half of the implicit requirements ([Jin et al., ReqElicitGym, arXiv:2602.18306, preprint, abstract only](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.18306)). On the positive side, GPT-4o follow-up questions were rated no worse than human-written ones and better when generation was guided by a list of common interviewer mistakes ([Shen et al., IEEE RE 2025, abstract only](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.02858)). The design therefore keeps the topic order and the follow-up obligation in the engine, restricts the model's scope (no opinion on answers, no invented facts, no request for contact details), and gives the prompt an explicit list of interviewer mistakes.

The content of the guide follows job-analysis practice. Incumbents inflate ability statements more than task statements, so the guide asks for tasks and concrete behaviours before abilities ([Morgeson et al., Journal of Applied Psychology, 2004, abstract](https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.89.4.674)). Anchoring on a specific non-routine incident with probes on cues, discriminations and typicality follows the critical decision method ([Klein et al., IEEE Trans. SMC, 1989, abstract only](https://doi.org/10.1109/21.31053)) and applied cognitive task analysis ([Militello and Hutton, Ergonomics, 1998, abstract only](https://doi.org/10.1080/001401398186108)); the specific interview phases of both methods are unverified from the primary papers and must not be listed as if they had been read. Returning a summary to the expert is member checking ([Birt et al., Qualitative Health Research, 2016, abstract](https://doi.org/10.1177/1049732316654870)). Combining several experts follows the Delphi pattern of iterated rounds ([Hasson et al., Journal of Advanced Nursing, 2000, abstract](https://doi.org/10.1046/j.1365-2648.2000.t01-1-01567.x)), with support from a study in which 5,734 participants preferred AI-generated group statements to those written by human mediators and in which successful statements incorporated dissenting voices ([Tessler et al., Science, 2024, abstract](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.adq2852)); that study concerned social and political opinions, not expert knowledge. The merged content must keep disagreements as explicit alternatives, because two sources the team has not verified report that LLM summarisers smooth disagreement (Aghaebe et al., arXiv:2601.04889; Wang et al., Findings of EMNLP 2025; both unverified).

**Remaining risks.** No study was found of an LLM conducting critical-incident or job-analysis interviews with domain experts, and nothing covers IT occupations, Vietnamese or time-limited volunteers. Under-probing can recur after any prompt change. Experts' transcripts and identities are themselves personal data that would leave Vietnam if a foreign model is used, which is one of the questions for the legal office.

**Verification.** In Stage A the team builds a harness on the ReqElicitGym pattern: a hidden incident sheet per role, a simulated respondent that answers only what is asked, and two measures, the share of sheet items elicited and the interviewer-mistake rate hand-coded on the markers used by Chopra and Haaland (open, neutral, relevant, hallucinated, follow-up given when required). This shows that the machinery works; it does not show that real experts respond the same way. In Stage B, with real experts, the measures are coverage of seed fields per hour of expert time, the share of summary statements the expert corrects, and the number of invented or out-of-scope model turns per interview.

### AI-2: draft the seed section by section, validate deterministically, and let experts own every key

**Problem.** The Content Administrator has to turn experts' answers into a structured seed with context, characters, ordered canon events, required facts, response options with effectiveness keys, a rubric with behavioural anchors, hints, follow-up goals, random events and endings. The decision already taken is that the Domain Expert sets every key and anchor and approves the seed. The criteria are fidelity to the expert's input (above all no invented keys or anchors), schema validity and playability, review effort, no code change per occupation (NFR-10), provider replaceability (DC-02) and auditability.

| Alternative | Fidelity to experts | Valid, playable seed | Expert and CA effort | NFR-10 and DC-02 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A. Content Administrator writes the seed by hand | High | High | High CA effort per scenario | Met, but does not scale |
| B. One LLM call fills the whole schema under strict structured output | Medium; inventions hard to see | Low for wide schemas | Low drafting, high checking | Strict mode differs by provider |
| C. LLM writes the seed, keys and rubric from the role name | Low; no expert grounding | Medium | Low | Met, but defeats RP-1 and RP-2 |
| **D. Free-text draft from verbatim incident text, conversion to the schema one section at a time, deterministic validation and repair, expert sign-off; keys and anchors transcribed, never generated (chosen)** | High, with provenance per field | High after repair | Medium, concentrated on review | Met with client-side validation |

**Choice and reasoning.** The schema follows evidence-centred design, which organises assessment into layers from domain analysis to task and evidence models ([Mislevy and Haertel, Educational Measurement: Issues and Practice, 2006, abstract](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1745-3992.2006.00075.x)); the earlier claim about what a PADI design template contains could not be verified and is not relied on. Filling the seed by section is supported by a structured-extraction benchmark in which frontier models degraded as schemas widened and produced no valid output on a 369-field schema ([Ferguson et al., ExtractBench, arXiv:2602.12247, preprint, abstract only](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.12247)). Drafting in free text before converting to the schema is supported by a study in which format restrictions reduced reasoning performance, more so as constraints tightened ([Tam et al., arXiv:2408.02442, preprint; venue unverified](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.02442)). Deterministic validation with a repair loop is supported by a pipeline for interactive-fiction serious games in which finalist configurations reached roughly 68–86% on a joint criterion of compilation, playability and learning-goal fidelity over 240 seeds, with repair iterations central and grammar masking of no consistent benefit ([Rogosch and Schrader, Applied Sciences, 2026, abstract only](https://doi.org/10.3390/app16062932)). That result is for open-weight models writing knowledge-assessment fiction; its transfer to occupational seeds with ordinal anchors is an inference.

Expert ownership of keys and anchors rests on two findings. Rubrics that LLMs generate differ from human grading rubrics and LLM graders take shortcuts, while supplying high-quality analytic rubrics that reflect human grading logic narrows the gap ([Wu et al., arXiv:2407.18328, accepted at Technology, Knowledge and Learning](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.18328)). When GPT-4 drafted a 210-question medical examination, blinded specialists judged 0.5% of questions false and required revision of 15% ([Klang et al., BMC Medical Education, 2023, abstract](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12909-023-04752-w)). Schema enforcement cannot be delegated to the provider: as read on 8 October 2026, schema-constrained output is documented for OpenAI, Anthropic, Google and Mistral, DeepSeek documents only a JSON-object mode that can return empty content, and Qwen's schema mode is not yet available in the Singapore region ([OpenAI](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/structured-outputs); [Anthropic](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/structured-outputs); [Google](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/structured-output); [DeepSeek](https://api-docs.deepseek.com/guides/json_mode); [Alibaba](https://www.alibabacloud.com/help/en/model-studio/qwen-structured-output); official documentation). DC-02 therefore requires validation with zod in the application and a retry path whichever provider is used.

**Remaining risks.** A seed can pass every structural check and still contain a plausible invention. No source measures how much revision effort an expert spends on an LLM-drafted seed with ordinal anchors. Two unverified sources suggest LLM assistance homogenises content and attracts socially desirable phrasing (Salecha et al., arXiv:2405.06058; Anderson et al., arXiv:2402.01536; both unverified).

**Verification.** In Stage A the team writes a small gold set of transcripts paired with gold seeds and reports per-field precision and recall with a matching rule per field type, on the ExtractBench pattern, counting invented fields separately because an invention in a key or anchor is the costly error; it also reports the schema-valid rate before and after repair and the number of repair iterations. In Stage B the measures are the share of fields the expert edits and the minutes spent per seed, and, for RP-1, the accuracy of scenarios generated from an expert seed against unseeded generation.

### AI-3: triage by a union of signals and audit what was not flagged

**Problem.** Playthroughs contain many free-text answers and experts cannot review all of them. AI-3 produces or collects test playthroughs and surfaces the cases that need the expert's judgment; the expert decides. It uses simulated answers and real human playthroughs, and in Stage A the team stands in for players and experts, so results there show only that the machinery works. The criteria are expert time, the ability to catch scoring errors including confident ones (which bears on NFR-14), coverage of all quality levels, independence from the judges (DC-01), cost, and an honest account of what Stage A demonstrates.

| Alternative for choosing what the expert sees | Expert time | Catches confident, consistent errors | Covers mid-range answers | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A. Expert reviews everything | Not feasible at about seven hours per expert | Yes | Yes | None in model calls |
| B. Random sample only | Low | In proportion to the sample | In proportion | None |
| C. One uncertainty signal (verbalised confidence, repeated-run spread or panel disagreement) | Low | No | Weak | One to five times the scoring cost |
| **D. Union of signals to rank a queue, a blind random audit of unflagged cases, thresholds chosen on the project's own labelled set (chosen)** | Low and adjustable | Partly, through the audit | Targeted by stratified sampling | Moderate |

**Choice and reasoning.** Simulated players are useful for finding relative problems and unsuitable for estimating absolute score distributions. Forty-five GPT-4 student profiles answering 20 multiple-choice items correlated with 100 real students at r = 0.72 ([Lu and Wang, L@S 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.11591)); simulated classrooms reached correlations as high as 0.75, 0.76 and 0.82 with real item difficulty in their best configurations, weaker models predicted difficulty better than stronger ones, and LLMs were poor direct judges of difficulty ([Acquaye et al., arXiv:2601.09953, preprint](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.09953)). Against this, persona prompting produced near-perfect, uniform accuracy unlike 2,359 real students ([Balog and Bakken, Findings of EMNLP 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.30033)). All three results are for multiple-choice knowledge items; applying them to free-text occupational answers is an inference, and it is the reason the design generates answers at assigned quality levels and adds real human playthroughs.

On which cases to flag, the evidence conflicts and supports no single signal. On a six-point essay rubric, neither verbalised confidence nor the spread of five repeated runs flagged the essays where the model disagreed with the human rater, majority voting did not improve agreement, and agreement fell sharply on high-scoring essays ([Yao, Yang and Kuang, AIME-Con 2026](https://aclanthology.org/2026.aimecon-main.31/)). Self-reported confidence was the best-calibrated signal in short-answer grading but separated right from wrong grades only weakly, with area under the curve between 0.570 and 0.639, and self-consistency was worse at about five times the inference cost ([Ferrer et al., arXiv:2603.29559, preprint](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.29559)). Verbalised confidence is overconfident in general ([Xiong et al., ICLR 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.13063)). The opposite practical conclusion, that flagging low-certainty answers cuts manual grading time by 40–90%, comes from a study with 20 runs per answer and simulated regrading ([Korthals et al., Machine Learning and Knowledge Extraction, 2026, abstract only](https://doi.org/10.3390/make8030074)). The two sides differ in run count and task, so the conflict stands, and the design consequence recorded in the notes is to rank by a union of signals and to audit unflagged cases by a blind random sample. A sample of n unflagged cases with no error bounds the error rate at about 3/n with 95% confidence, a rule stated for n above 30 ([Eypasch et al., BMJ, 1995](https://europepmc.org/articles/PMC2550668), restating [Hanley and Lippman-Hand, JAMA, 1983, opening paragraph only](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.1983.03330370053031)).

What the expert sees is a ranked queue with the answer, the three judges' scores and rationales, the reason for the flag and the nearest scored examples, with the ability to edit a rubric anchor during review, because people refine their criteria as they grade ([Shankar et al., UIST 2024](https://doi.org/10.1145/3654777.3676450)). Rulings are fed back as scored examples and concentrated on partially correct answers, where automated scorers degrade while human agreement stays constant; in that study, six to ten balanced examples performed similarly and two examples degraded mid-range scoring substantially, for the one model tested at several shot counts ([Schleifer et al., arXiv:2605.07647, preprint accepted at BEA 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.07647)).

The offline check of generated text against the seed does not need a model stronger than the generator, because the seed is the reference, and it should come from a different family. A 770M-parameter checker reached 74.7% balanced accuracy on LLM-AggreFact against 75.3% for GPT-4 at a small fraction of the cost ([Tang, Laban and Durrett, MiniCheck, EMNLP 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.10774)), and the leaderboard read on 8 October 2026 tops out at 77.4% ([LLM-AggreFact leaderboard](https://llm-aggrefact.github.io/)). No checker is reliable on subtle cases: on summaries selected because detectors disagreed, the best balanced accuracy was 57.65% ([Bao et al., FaithBench, arXiv:2410.13210, preprint](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.13210)).

**Remaining risks.** Confident and consistent scoring errors are invisible to confidence and disagreement signals. Simulated runs are not exchangeable with real users. No source was found on automation bias among reviewers who see a pre-triaged queue. This finding puts in doubt the sufficiency of the fixed rule that flags only answers on which judges differ by more than one level: the rule is kept, and the random audit is what covers the errors it cannot see.

**Verification.** In Stage A, on the synthetic benchmark where each variant's level is known, the team computes for each signal and for their union the risk–coverage curve for detecting a wrong panel score, chooses the review threshold from that curve, and reports the error rate found in a blind random sample of unflagged items with its rule-of-three bound. The offline checker's balanced accuracy is measured on a Vietnamese set of narrations built by inserting one inconsistency into a faithful narration, on the SummEdits recipe ([Laban et al., arXiv:2305.14540, preprint](https://arxiv.org/html/2305.14540)), over-sampling contradictions, which were the hardest class in the Vietnamese ViHallu challenge ([Nguyen et al., arXiv:2601.04711, preprint, abstract only](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.04711)). In Stage B the measures are expert minutes per reviewed case and the share of flagged cases in which the expert changes the score, compared with the same share in the unflagged random sample.

### AI-4: the engine owns the plot and the model fills the gaps from a whole seed

**Problem.** At run time the system must write connecting narration and follow-up questions that fit a player's free-text answer while staying inside the seed. The criteria are preservation of required facts (NFR-15, proposed at 95%), adherence to canon order, generation within 5 seconds at the 95th percentile (NFR-01), cost per session (NFR-11), no code change per occupation (NFR-10), robustness to off-script and adversarial input, no leakage of scoring keys, and graceful degradation (NFR-04).

| Alternative | Fact preservation and canon order | Latency and cost | New occupation without code change | Off-script and adversarial input | Degrades gracefully |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A. Fully authored branching (the current prototype) | Complete | Best | No; each scenario is hand-written | Cannot respond to free text | Not applicable |
| B. Free generation by the model | Not controlled | Medium | Yes | Open to drift and override | No authored content to fall back on |
| C. Retrieval over the seed | Risk of missing a required fact | Cheaper prompts | Yes | As B within retrieved text | Partly |
| D. Fine-tuned model per occupation | Inconsistent for facts | Low per call, high to prepare | No; training per occupation | As B | Partly |
| **E. Engine-controlled order, verbatim canon, whole seed in the prompt, model writes connectors and follow-ups, deterministic checks at run time, model-based checks offline (chosen; D-89)** | Canon exact by construction; connectors checked | One short call per turn | Yes; the seed is data | Engine counters and in-character deflection | Yes; canon and hints remain |

**Choice and reasoning.** Keeping author structure outside free generation is the established approach in interactive narrative, where an experience manager holds a plot graph and decides between intervening and accommodating when the player deviates ([Riedl and Bulitko, AI Magazine, 2013](https://doi.org/10.1609/aimag.v34i1.2449)), and a recent planning framework structures narratives into cells with explicit plans to prevent drift ([Bang et al., SNAP, arXiv:2601.11529, preprint, abstract only](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.11529)). No LLM system was found that reports a quantitative rate of adherence to authored structure. Engine-side control is further supported by evidence that text in the user turn can override system-level rules: when instructions at different levels conflicted, all models degraded and the best open-source model resolved conflicts correctly 48% of the time ([Zhang et al., IHEval, NAACL 2025, abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.08745)), and a dataset of over 126,000 prompt-injection attacks shows many models are vulnerable ([Toyer et al., Tensor Trust, arXiv:2311.01011; venue unconfirmed](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.01011)).

Putting the whole seed in the prompt, without retrieval or fine-tuning, rests on three results and one inference. Long-context prompting outperformed retrieval on average when sufficiently resourced, while retrieval was much cheaper ([Li et al., EMNLP 2024 industry track](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.16833)); retrieval consistently beat unsupervised fine-tuning for injecting facts ([Ovadia et al., EMNLP 2024](https://aclanthology.org/2024.emnlp-main.15/)); and models use information at the start and end of a context better than in the middle ([Liu et al., TACL 2024](https://aclanthology.org/2024.tacl-1.9/)). Applying these to a closed seed of 1,000–2,000 tokens is an inference: at that size the cost advantage of retrieval is small and the risk of omitting a required fact is avoided.

The run-time checks target what generation gets wrong most often. In a gold-standard annotation of 21 generated sports summaries, 184 of 418 accuracy errors were wrong numbers and 105 were wrong names ([Thomson and Reiter, INLG 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.03992)), which is the basis for deterministic matching of numbers and named entities against the seed. For input the system does not understand, a study of the interactive drama Façade with twelve players found that failing to understand broke the experience less than acting on a shallow interpretation, and that in-character deflection was absorbed into the story; the analysis is qualitative ([Mehta et al., AAMAS 2007](https://www.ifaamas.org/Proceedings/aamas07/html/pdf/AAMAS07_0464_d2a87474872bb6c315649be862b44b8c.pdf)). Follow-up selection is a separate skill: on a benchmark that asks for the one necessary clarifying question, models reached only 40–50% on two task families ([Li et al., QuestBench, NeurIPS 2025 Datasets and Benchmarks, abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.22674)). The design therefore gives each follow-up one goal from the seed and lets the engine enforce the cap.

Two further design points are the earlier research agent's inference and are not sourced: keeping the rubric and scoring keys out of AI-4's prompt, and re-injecting the character card every turn. The second has partial support: instruction drift appeared within eight rounds of dialogue, and repeating the system prompt was one of two baselines in that paper, not its proposed method, with no figure read for how much repetition alone helps ([Li et al., COLM 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.10962)); persona fidelity also degrades over dialogues of more than 100 rounds ([Luz de Araujo et al., EACL 2026, abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.12775)), far longer than a 10–15 minute scenario. The model family for AI-4 should be chosen by measurement and cost, since a newer large model had the same persona score as an 8B model on one benchmark ([Samuel et al., PersonaGym, Findings of EMNLP 2025, abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.18416)).

**Remaining risks, and where D-89 is in doubt.** Rule-only checks at run time cannot detect a semantic invention assembled from tokens that are all in the seed; an unverified source reports that no automatic system in a shared task detected context errors (Thomson and Reiter shared task, arXiv:2108.05644, unverified), and another that rubric content can leak through paraphrase (Zhao et al., arXiv:2604.18660, preprint, unverified). Authority violations and persona drift are semantic. No cited check was evaluated on Vietnamese and no published comparison of rule-only against model-based checking was found. Model-based checking offline does not close the gap either, given best-case balanced accuracy of about 75–77% on mixed data. The notes' inference is that NFR-15 can be certified only for required facts and numbers by deterministic matching, with contradiction and invention estimated offline at a measured error rate. A pilot figure is a caution: 20% (55 of 276) of GPT-4 role-play responses failed to follow all principles or fit the dialogue, as rated by the paper's co-authors ([Louie et al., Roleplay-doh, EMNLP 2024](https://aclanthology.org/2024.emnlp-main.591/)).

**Verification.** No public benchmark supplies the needed items, so the project builds its own AI-4 test set: scripted player turns per seed in four classes (on-script, off-script, adversarial, rubric-fishing), scored on required-fact preservation by deterministic matching, canon order from the engine log, leakage of keys, one goal per follow-up and respect of the follow-up cap. Latency is measured from the deployment region with the real prompt size and reported at the median and 95th percentile. In Stage A the team writes the scripts; in Stage B experts rate a sample of real sessions for accuracy and for whether the scenario elicits the target competency, seeded against unseeded (RP-1).

### AI-5: three judges with an expert reference, an anchored rubric and scored examples

**Problem.** AI-5 scores a player's free-text answer per competency on the ordinal scale −1, 0, +1, +2 as a Domain Expert would, and the feedback shown to the player is written from its output. The requirements are agreement with experts (NFR-14: QWK ≥ 0.70, standardised mean difference ≤ 0.15, at most 0.10 below expert–expert agreement), scoring within 8 seconds at the 95th percentile (NFR-02), cost within a ceiling (NFR-11), a different family from the generator (DC-01), a replaceable provider (DC-02), a new occupation without code change (NFR-10) and an auditable record.

| Alternative | Agreement with experts | New occupation without training data | Latency and cost | Independence and availability | Auditability |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A. Keyword matching (current prototype) | Not validated; written per scenario | No | Best | Not applicable | Rule is visible but shallow |
| B. Fine-tuned supervised scorer | Highest on public data | No; needs scored responses per item | Low per call | Single model | Low |
| C. One strong prompted judge | Below target on public averages | Yes | One call | Single provider | Rationale available |
| D. One reasoning model | Lower validity in one study | Yes | Slow and costly | Single provider | Rationale available |
| E. Cheap judge first, escalate on low confidence | Validated for pairwise tasks only | Yes | Lowest average cost | Changes the instrument per answer | Mixed |
| **F. Panel of three families, one structured call each (quote, rationale, level), median, flag on spread, expert reference and scored examples in the prompt (chosen; fixed decision)** | To be measured; design features have indirect support | Yes | Three parallel calls | Three uncorrelated providers; quorum possible | Quote and rationale per judge |

**Choice and reasoning.** The gap between alternatives B and C is the central fact. On ASAP-AES, fine-tuned models reach about 0.79–0.82 QWK against human–human 0.752, while prompted LLMs average about 0.31–0.47 ([Ormerod and Kwako, arXiv:2407.01873, preprint](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.01873); [Xiao et al., arXiv:2401.06431, preprint](https://arxiv.org/html/2401.06431); [Mansour et al., LREC-COLING 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.06149)). On ASAP-SAS, the closest public match in form (answers of about 50 words on three or four levels), human–human QWK is 0.874, supervised models reach about 0.75–0.80, and prompted GPT-4 ranges from 0.26–0.30 on a five-task subset to 0.677 in another study that was read only as cited by Ormerod and Kwako ([Chamieh et al., BEA 2024](https://aclanthology.org/2024.bea-1.25.pdf); [Ormerod and Kwako, preprint](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.01873)). The prompted figures are for 2023–2024 models. Alternative B is nevertheless excluded by the project's constraints: fine-tuning needed a median of about 50 responses per score point for an item to pass operational criteria ([McGrane and Torres Irribarra, AIME-Con 2026 Works in Progress, abstract](https://aclanthology.org/2026.aimecon-wip.39/)), JobQuest has no scored responses, and NFR-10 forbids per-occupation work. **NFR-14 therefore sits above every verified prompted average**, and the design's claim to reach it rests on four features whose combined benefit is untested.

The first feature is an expert-written reference answer. Without a reference, a judge agreed with experts mainly on questions it could answer itself (kappa 0.78 against 0.30 for GPT-4o); with a human-written reference the two figures became 0.92 and 0.83, and a 7B model reached 0.78 ([Krumdick et al., arXiv:2503.05061, preprint](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.05061)). That is pairwise grading of model answers, not ordinal scoring of human answers. The second is a rubric with a description per level: adding level descriptions raised one model's weighted kappa with human coders by about 0.08–0.10 on most features of a situational-judgment test, where the two humans themselves agreed at only 0.356–0.788 ([Walsh et al., arXiv:2507.13881, preprint](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.13881v1)), and the closest published benchmark design uses an instance-specific rubric describing every score level ([Kim et al., BiGGen Bench, NAACL 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.05761)). The third is scored examples, which helped more than chain-of-thought in essay scoring ([Yoshida, Findings of EMNLP 2025](https://doi.org/10.18653/v1/2025.findings-emnlp.445)) and whose number matters most in the mid-range ([Schleifer et al., preprint](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.07647)). The fourth is the output order quote, rationale, level, which follows the reason-then-format finding of Tam et al. and the feedback-then-score template of Prometheus ([Kim et al., ICLR 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.08491)), and gives the expert a verbatim anchor to audit.

**The advisor's question on judge strength.** The conclusion adopted in the notes is that the judge's strength is not measured against the generator. On a benchmark of hard pairwise correctness without references, judging accuracy tracks the model's own ability to solve the problem, which the authors describe as highly correlated on the basis of a four-model ablation (GPT-4o 56.57 overall, Claude-3.5-Sonnet 64.29, o1-preview 75.43), and fine-tuned small judges fall to or below chance ([Tan et al., JudgeBench, ICLR 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.12784)). With a reference the dependence largely disappears (Krumdick et al., above). Size still matters for alignment with human raters under the same rubric: small-model raters agreed poorly with humans (ICC below 0.20) and larger ones moderately (0.41–0.57), comparable to the two humans' own 0.45 ([Jung et al., arXiv:2509.19329, accepted at NCME AIME 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.19329)). Reasoning models are not a safe upgrade: two had significantly lower validity than a small non-reasoning model on essay scoring (Yoshida, above). What matters for AI-5 is agreement with experts under an explicit rubric with scored examples, and panel composition is settled by measurement.

**Why different families, and what a panel does and does not buy.** Judges recognise and favour their own generations ([Panickssery et al., NeurIPS 2024](https://papers.nips.cc/paper_files/paper/2024/hash/7f1f0218e45f5414c79c0679633e47bc-Abstract-Conference.html)) and favour models whose mistakes resemble their own ([Goel et al., ICML 2025](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v267/goel25b.html)); DC-01 follows. Evidence for panels is mixed. A panel of three smaller models from different families agreed with humans better than GPT-4 on binary correctness and pairwise tasks at one seventh to one eighth of the cost ([Verga et al., arXiv:2404.18796, preprint](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.18796)). Against this, nine judges from seven families gave about two independent votes on an inference task and the best single judge matched or beat the panel ([Kohli, arXiv:2605.29800, preprint](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.29800)); 42 judges on eight Indic languages agreed with each other as much as humans do yet reached 58–66% of human agreement ([Mukherjee et al., EMNLP 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.03043)); and on the one ordinal rubric benchmark with human ratings, a five-judge majority vote (Pearson 0.627) was essentially equal to its best member (0.623) (BiGGen Bench, above). **The panel-median decision is therefore in doubt as an accuracy measure** and is better justified by the independence it gives from the generator, the disagreement flag, and availability across uncorrelated providers. High reliability does not establish validity: test–retest reliability above 0.95 coexisted with position bias above 0.10 in two judges in a large audit ([Norman et al., arXiv:2606.19544, preprint, abstract only](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.19544)), and a twenty-task study concludes that judges must be validated against humans on the task at hand ([Bavaresco et al., JUDGE-BENCH, ACL 2025, abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.18403)).

**A second fixed decision in doubt.** One call per judge that scores all competencies saves two thirds or more of the calls, and it fits prompt caching. Two findings count against it: batched verification of rubric items trades accuracy for efficiency ([Peng et al., RuVerBench, EMNLP 2026, abstract only, no figures](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.29920)), and LLM evaluators confuse criteria, so that a perturbation aimed at one aspect moves the scores of others ([Hu et al., ACL 2024, abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.12055)). The decision should stand only if a Stage A ablation shows no material loss against one call per competency.

**Feedback text.** Writing the player's feedback from AI-5's output keeps one source of truth, and the aBridgeAI precedent shows the same separation of assessment from conversation. One caution is on record: in mock-interview analysis, LLM scores were comparable to experts' while the models often failed to identify errors and give actionable advice ([Maity et al., arXiv:2504.05683, preprint, abstract only](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.05683)). Feedback quality needs its own expert rating in Stage B. The Decree's rule on automated processing, discussed below, also requires a notice and a way not to take part.

**Remaining risks.** The errors of strong models are correlated across providers ([Kim et al., ICML 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.07962)), so three families reduce shared error without removing it. Partially correct answers are scored worst. QWK itself moves with prevalence: on a 4×4 matrix it fell from 0.765 to 0.599 as prevalence shifted while accuracy stayed at 0.66 ([Doewes et al., EDM 2023](https://educationaldatamining.org/EDM2023/proceedings/2023.EDM-long-papers.9/index.html)), enough to move a scorer across the 0.70 threshold. Provider models change: accuracy of the same API model on one task fell from 84% to 51% in three months ([Chen et al., arXiv:2307.09009, preprint, abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.09009)), and deterministic settings did not give repeatable outputs ([Atil et al., arXiv:2408.04667, preprint, abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.04667)).

**Verification.** Stage A measures, on the synthetic benchmark described in the next section, each judge alone, each pair and the three-judge median against the known level, with the full reporting set; it runs the two ablations (one call for all competencies against one per competency; median against best single judge), the own-family test (mean signed error of each judge on answers written by each generator family), the bias probes with zero expected change, the stability of the panel median over repeated runs, and raw against redacted input. An English pre-check on ASAP-SAS and on the Mohler data-structures set costs no expert time. Stage B is the test of NFR-14: real responses rated blind by three experts per pilot role, the panel compared with the expert consensus by QWK, and expert–expert agreement by Krippendorff's alpha with the ordinal metric.

### Stage A: one targeted edit per variant, verified by a human, with stratified levels

The Stage A design, in which each free-text task has a strong reference answer and variants that each degrade exactly one rubric criterion to a known level, extends published perturbation benchmarks; no published benchmark was found that degrades a criterion to a known ordinal level, and the report should say that the design is an extension. The construction follows validated recipes. Start from the expert reference answer and apply one targeted edit per variant, as SummEdits does for inconsistencies and as a 2,400-item checklist benchmark does for 22 perturbation categories, in which five evaluator LLMs missed the quality drop in over half of cases on average ([Doddapaneni et al., FBI, EMNLP 2024, abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.13439)). Have a human confirm that each variant sits at the intended level and that no other criterion moved, as the annotators did in the criteria-confusion study (Hu et al., above); cross-criterion leakage is then a metric. Remove or relabel borderline variants, the step that raised annotator agreement to kappa 0.92 in SummEdits. Add variants that sound better but are worse, on the LLMBar adversarial pattern, where expert annotators agreed on 94% of a sample of 80 ([Zeng et al., ICLR 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.07641)), and add construct-irrelevant probes whose expected score change is zero, such as padding, spelling errors, duplication and off-topic text ([Walsh and Ivan, arXiv:2603.25674, short version accepted at AIED 2026, abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.25674); [Ye et al., arXiv:2410.02736, preprint, abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.02736)).

Synthetic answers must be written by a family different from every judge. The supporting literature defines relatedness and shows that bias falls from same model to same family, but it measures a three-step chain through a fine-tuned student model, so the definition transfers and the effect sizes do not ([Li et al., Preference Leakage, ICLR 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01534)). The notes propose the measurement that closes this: have each candidate family write the same degraded variants and test whether any judge scores answers from its own family above the known level.

### Sample size: about 100 items per competency for ±0.10, about 46 for ±0.15

The number of items should be planned from the width of a confidence interval, not from a significance test. The figures below are the research team's own arithmetic with the Bonett approximation for an intraclass correlation as restated by a secondary source, at the 95% level, treating QWK as an intraclass correlation; neither the Bonett paper nor the Fleiss–Cohen equivalence paper was read, and the figures must be re-run with `presize::prec_icc` before they are quoted ([presize documentation, secondary](https://search.r-project.org/CRAN/refmans/presize/html/prec_icc.html)).

|---|---|---|---|
| Two raters (judge against expert consensus or known level) | 0.60 | about 159 | not computed |
| Two raters | 0.70 | about 101 | about 46 |
| Two raters | 0.80 | about 51 | about 24 |
| Three raters (expert–expert–expert) | 0.70 | about 68 | about 31 |

These counts apply per competency and per language wherever a figure is to be reported separately. This report's recommendation, derived from the table, is to plan Stage A for about 100 labelled items per competency in each language reported separately, spread evenly over the four levels, and to accept about 46 where writing variants is the bottleneck, stating the wider interval. The approximation assumes balanced use of the scale; a natural distribution concentrated at 0 and +1 violates it, which is why Stage A items are stratified by level and why the final interval comes from resampling items. For Stage B's natural distribution the matching planning tool is `kappaSize::CI4Cats` with the pilot's level prevalences, with the caveat that its documentation does not say the kappa is weighted ([kappaSize documentation, secondary](https://search.r-project.org/CRAN/refmans/kappaSize/html/CI4Cats.html)); no sample-size method specific to quadratic weighted kappa on a four-level scale was verified, and a small simulation would close that gap. The advisor's check of about one fifth of the items bounds the label-error rate by the rule of three only where the checked sample exceeds 30 items with no error found, which is this report's inference from the condition stated in the 1995 restatement.

The reporting set, each element traceable to a source in the notes, is QWK with a 95% interval from resampling items; the full 4×4 confusion matrix; exact and adjacent agreement; linear weighted kappa; per-level agreement; the mean signed difference and standardised mean difference as the bias index ([Byrt et al., Journal of Clinical Epidemiology, 1993, abstract](https://doi.org/10.1016/0895-4356%2893%2990018-V)); the distribution of expert and judge scores; and, for three experts or three judges, Krippendorff's alpha with the ordinal metric. Interval methods for alpha are disputed: bootstrap intervals had near-nominal coverage in one simulation on nominal data ([Zapf et al., BMC Medical Research Methodology, 2016, abstract](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12874-016-0200-9)), while a later preprint finds a jackknife estimator much better than the customary bootstrap ([Hughes, arXiv:2210.13265, preprint](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.13265)); the report should name the method used. Alpha tolerates an expert skipping items, which volunteers will do. The NFR-14 thresholds come from Williamson, Xi and Breyer (2012), reference [71] of the project report, which was not re-opened in this research.

## Four model families: one to generate, three to judge

### A family is a developer's line plus everything derived from it

The definition proposed in the notes is built on the three kinds of relatedness in the preference-leakage paper and is the team's operational rule, since no source defines a family for direct generator-to-judge scoring. Two models are in the same family if they are the same model or snapshots of it; if one was fine-tuned, distilled, merged from or trained on outputs of the other or of any member of the other's family, in either direction; or if they are versions or sizes of one line from the same developer. The host or reseller is irrelevant, and a derived model belongs to the family of every ancestor. Measured leakage ordered the relationships: same model 23.6%, inheritance 19.3–22.3%, same family and series 8.9%, same family and different series 2.8% ([Li et al., ICLR 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01534)). Inheritance is thus the relationship that matters most to exclude, and it is common among current candidates: SEA-LION v4.5 is built on Qwen and distilled from a larger Qwen model ([model card](https://huggingface.co/aisingapore/Qwen-SEA-LION-v4.5-27B-IT)), Meta's open Muse Glimmer is distilled from Muse Spark ([Meta documentation](https://dev.meta.ai/docs/muse-glimmer)), and Viettel's Vietnamese model is trained from an NVIDIA base ([Viettel AI press page, vendor](https://viettelai.vn/en/tin-tuc/viettel-trains-120-billion-parameter-vietnamese-sovereign-ai-model)). Vietnamese-developed models are mostly fine-tunes and therefore inherit a foreign family; they add no independent family to a panel. Treating open-weight siblings such as gpt-oss and Gemma as members of their developers' closed families is a precaution, not a cited fact, except for Muse Glimmer.

### Why four families, and a provisional allocation

Four families follow from the constraints: DC-01 separates the assessor from the generator; the panel has three judges from different families so that a median exists and no judge shares lineage with another; and Stage A's synthetic answers must come from a family that is none of the three. One family therefore serves generation (AI-1, AI-2, AI-4 and simulated answers) and three serve AI-5. The supporting evidence is self-preference and similarity bias (Panickssery et al.; Goel et al.), the ordering of leakage by relatedness (Li et al.) and uncorrelated outages across providers (Chu et al.). The limit is equally documented: across more than 350 models, two models agreed 60% of the time when both erred on one dataset, and larger, more accurate models had highly correlated errors even across providers ([Kim et al., ICML 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.07962)). Family diversity is necessary and not sufficient.

This report's provisional allocation, to be confirmed or overturned in Stage A, places the three judges in the OpenAI, Anthropic and Google families and the generator in the Alibaba Qwen family. The reasoning is that the judges depend most on schema-constrained output and on reading Vietnamese, and those three families both document schema enforcement and sit in the top Vietnamese band at flagship level; the generator needs fluent Vietnamese dialogue at low cost, where the open Qwen models of 27B parameters and above score 84.7–87.2 on the leaderboard's multi-turn chat track, and its weaker schema support is tolerable because AI-2 is offline with client-side validation and AI-4 needs little structure. Mistral's models are among the weakest documented in Vietnamese, DeepSeek's first-party API processes data in China and offers only JSON-object mode, and Meta's hosted model is not on the Vietnamese leaderboard; they are reserves. Two conditions attach to the allocation. The Gemini API terms exclude services likely to be accessed by people under 18, so a Google judge presupposes an adult-only user base or a different route to the model. And because the generator family is fixed before the synthetic answers are written, Stage A should have at least two disjoint families write the variants, so that every candidate judge can be evaluated on answers that are not from its own family. A fully open-weight panel is licence-feasible across four developers (Gemma, Qwen, gpt-oss, Muse Glimmer; Apache or MIT), with Vietnamese scores of about 64–77, and is the only route to keeping inference on infrastructure the team chooses; an always-on GPU costs roughly $250–$800 per month at listed hourly rates, which the notes' arithmetic shows is far more than per-token prices at low volume ([Runpod pricing, vendor](https://www.runpod.io/pricing)).

### Direct provider calls behind an interface the team owns

Four integration approaches can reach four families, and they differ in who sees the players' text. No peer-reviewed comparison of them was found.

The chosen approach, an interpretation recorded in the notes, is a team-owned internal interface (for example a judge client that takes an answer and a rubric and returns a zod-validated object) with either provider SDKs or the AI SDK's direct provider packages behind it. The decisive property for DC-02 is ownership of the interface; which library sits behind it is secondary. One trap is documented: by default the AI SDK routes string model identifiers through the Vercel AI Gateway, so the code must use the direct provider packages explicitly to keep text going only to the named providers ([vercel/ai README](https://github.com/vercel/ai)). An abstraction layer is recommended by all three cloud frameworks, with Microsoft's warnings that a gateway can become a single point of failure and sees raw request data (Azure gateway guide, above). Abstraction also has limits: Anthropic's data-residency parameter is unavailable through its OpenAI-compatible endpoint ([Anthropic, data residency](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/manage-claude/data-residency)), and no single cloud catalogue page read on 8 October 2026 lists proprietary OpenAI, Anthropic, Google and Mistral models together. LLM APIs are updated silently and deprecated on schedule, so prompts need regression tests ([Ma et al., arXiv:2311.11123, preprint of a conference paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.11123)).

For audit and reproducibility, each call records the requested and returned model identifiers, prompt, rubric and schema versions, sampling and reasoning settings, uncached, cached and output tokens, computed cost with the date of the price table, latency, finish reason, validation result and parsed output, under pseudonymous keys. Judges use pinned snapshot identifiers, a frozen regression set from Stage A is re-run on a schedule and on every deprecation notice, and a change of judge model is treated as a new instrument that must be re-validated against NFR-14. Anthropic publishes retirement dates per model and OpenAI states at least six months' notice for generally available models ([Anthropic models overview](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/models/overview); [OpenAI deprecations](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/deprecations)). The OpenTelemetry conventions for generative AI, still in development status, treat prompts and outputs as sensitive and say content capture should be opt-in ([OpenTelemetry GenAI spans](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/open-telemetry/semantic-conventions-genai/main/docs/gen-ai/gen-ai-spans.md)); content therefore stays in the project's own database in Vietnam and external telemetry stays content-free.

### Vietnamese law treats a foreign model API as a cross-border transfer

This subsection reports what the texts say and is not legal advice. Law 91/2025/QH15 and Decree 356/2025/NĐ-CP were read in full on a third-party site that reproduces them; no official-portal copy could be opened, one law-firm alert was used as a partial cross-check, and every article number should be re-checked against the official gazette before it is quoted ([Law 91/2025, unofficial reproduction](https://ketoananpha.vn/luat-bao-ve-du-lieu-ca-nhan-nam-2025); [Decree 356/2025, unofficial reproduction](https://ketoananpha.vn/nghi-dinh-356-2025-nd-cp); [EY Vietnam Legal Alert, March 2026, secondary legal summary](https://www.ey.com/content/dam/ey-unified-site/ey-com/en-vn/technical/tax/documents/ey-vietnam-legal-alert-march-2026-decree-no356-2025-nd-cp-providing-detailed-guidance-for-implementation-of-personal-data-protection-law-eng.pdf)).

As read, Article 20(1)(c) of the Law lists the use of a platform outside Vietnam to process personal data collected in Vietnam as a case of cross-border transfer, and Article 20(2) requires the transferring party to prepare a transfer impact assessment dossier and send one original to the specialised data-protection authority, a unit under the Ministry of Public Security, within 60 days from the first transfer. Decree Article 18 sets the dossier's content, including recipient details, purposes, data types, consent, security measures and a risk assessment, and a 15-day response by the authority. The exemptions in Law Article 20(6) and Decree Article 17(3) cover cases such as state agencies, employee data on cloud services, the data subject's own transfer, press activity and certain transport, payment, visa and scholarship procedures; in the notes' interpretation none describes a career-exploration game, which confirms the team's reading in DC-03. The maximum fine for an organisation that violates the transfer rules is 5% of the previous year's revenue (Law Article 8). No exemption for research or educational institutions was found in the Law as read.

Three points in the texts bear directly on the design, and each is an interpretation to be put to the legal office. First, Decree Article 4(1)(l) lists behaviour-tracking and service-usage data on online services among sensitive personal data; if a record of how a user plays falls under it, the five-year relief for small enterprises and start-ups in Law Article 38 is unavailable regardless of size, and the duties to tell the data subject the data is sensitive and to protect it on transfer apply. Second, Decree Article 10(3) requires the controller to notify the data subject of automated processing, explain how the algorithm works and its impact, and offer a way not to take part; this maps onto AI-5 scoring. Third, Law Article 24 gives a child's legal representative the exercise of the child's rights, and neither text as read defines the age of a child. The two sources disagree on two points that only the legal office can settle: the alert describes an ex-ante approval while the Decree as read sets filing within 60 days after the transfer begins, and the alert lists the transfer assessment within the small-enterprise relief while the texts as read name only Articles 21, 22 and 33(2).

Provider terms read on 8 October 2026 add constraints. OpenAI, Anthropic and Google's paid tier state that API inputs are not used for training by default, with retention of up to 30 days for abuse monitoring at OpenAI and an unspecified limited period at Google; Anthropic's two official pages differ on its default retention ([OpenAI, your data](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/your-data); [Anthropic privacy centre](https://privacy.claude.com/en/articles/7996866-how-long-do-you-store-my-organization-s-data); [Gemini API terms](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/terms); official policies). Zero data retention is by approval at OpenAI, Anthropic and Mistral and excludes batch endpoints at OpenAI and Mistral. No first-party API read offers processing confined to South-East Asia, and a Singapore region would still be a transfer outside Vietnam. Google's free tier uses submitted content to improve its products and must not carry player text. A Vietnam-based host does not by itself remove the transfer, since closed models in its catalogue can only be served by forwarding requests abroad and neither host's page states where each model runs ([FPT AI Factory pricing, vendor](https://factory.fpt.ai/pricing/)). An adult-only pilot is the simplest way to stay clear of both the children's-data question and the providers' under-18 terms.

### De-identification reduces risk and cannot be claimed to remove the law's application

Law Article 2(1), as read, states that personal data after de-identification is no longer personal data, and Article 2(11) defines de-identification as producing data that cannot identify or help identify a specific person; Article 12(1) states that encrypted personal data remains personal data. The standard is strict and the technical evidence does not support claiming it for automatically redacted free text. The documentation of the open-source redaction tool Presidio states that it offers no guarantee of finding all sensitive information ([Presidio documentation](https://presidio.dataprivacystack.org/)); LLMs inferred personal attributes from real forum text with up to 85% top-1 accuracy, and the authors found text anonymisation ineffective against such inference ([Staab et al., arXiv:2310.07298, preprint, abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.07298)). The defensible position recorded in the notes is that redaction is a risk-reduction measure and the transfer dossier is filed anyway.

The proposed pipeline is a design inference. Deterministic recognisers (regular expressions and checksums for phone numbers, e-mail addresses, identity numbers, bank accounts, URLs and student numbers) run first, followed by a Vietnamese named-entity model for persons, places and organisations, with the seed's own character names allow-listed. Vietnamese resources exist for generic entity recognition ([PhoBERT, Findings of EMNLP 2020, abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2003.00744); [underthesea repository](https://github.com/undertheseanlp/underthesea)), but no published precision or recall for detecting personal identifiers in Vietnamese free text was found. Independently of redaction, requests carry a random per-call identifier and no account identifier. The cost to scoring is expected to be small because answers respond to a fictional scenario whose names and numbers come from the seed: redaction changed classification-type tasks by about 1–5% and question answering by more than 25%, depending on whether task-critical entities were removed ([Pal et al., arXiv:2411.05978, workshop paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.05978)), and replacing names had a modest negative effect across nine tasks ([Lothritz et al., NoDaLiDa 2023](https://aclanthology.org/2023.nodalida-1.2/)). That expectation is not a measurement; no study measures the effect of redaction on ordinal rubric scoring. Stage A closes the gap by reporting precision and recall per identifier type on a Vietnamese and English test set with synthetic identifiers, by scoring every benchmark answer raw and redacted, and by counting false redactions of seed terms.

### Cost per session: a formula now, a figure after instrumentation

The platform has no user revenue, so NFR-11 needs a transparent method. For each call the cost is the sum of uncached input tokens, cache-read tokens times the read multiplier, cache-write tokens times the write multiplier, and output tokens including any billed reasoning tokens, each multiplied by the pinned model's list price on the access date; a session's cost is the sum over narration calls, over the three judges for each scored answer, over follow-up calls and over retries and hedges, reported as a mean, a 95th percentile and a split by component, with the price table and its date stored beside the result. Token counts per session do not exist yet because the prototype has no AI, so no per-session figure can be given. The notes offer two illustrations on assumed token counts: a judge call of 1,500 input and 300 output tokens costs about $0.0003 at $0.10 and $0.50 per million, so three small-tier judges cost roughly $0.001 per scored answer before caching; and a call on a mid-tier model at $2 and $10 with 2,500 input and 400 output tokens costs $0.0090 uncached and $0.0052 with a 2,000-token prefix read from cache, after which output is about 77% of the call's cost.

Three documented levers apply. Cached input costs about 10% of the input price at Anthropic and Google and is discounted by up to 95% at OpenAI, with minimum cacheable prefixes of 512 to 1,024 tokens on current models and lifetimes of 5 to 30 minutes ([Anthropic prompt caching](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-caching); [OpenAI prompt caching](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/prompt-caching); official documentation). The single-call judge design fits this, with the rubric, anchors and scenario in a stable prefix and the answer last, but hit rates depend on traffic and must be measured. Batch processing at 50% applies to Stage A runs and to AI-3's offline checks, not to run time, and should carry only synthetic or redacted data. Output tokens cost several times more than input tokens, so capping the rationale length per competency is the cheapest lever. Cheap-first cascades and routers report large savings on question-answering benchmarks ([Chen et al., FrugalGPT, arXiv:2305.05176, preprint](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.05176); [Ong et al., RouteLLM, arXiv:2406.18665, preprint](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.18665)), but a router that skips or swaps a judge changes the instrument RP-2 validates; the compatible form is deterministic pre-checks before the panel. Semantic caching of scores is inappropriate for an assessment, because two answers close in meaning can differ in the one detail an anchor depends on.

### Waiting time: engineering targets, tail arithmetic and asynchronous scoring

The thresholds in NFR-01 and NFR-02 are engineering targets, not values derived from user research on games. The familiar limits of 0.1, 1 and 10 seconds trace to a table of design constants in a 1991 systems paper that cites earlier books and reports no experiment ([Card, Robertson and Mackinlay, CHI 1991](https://courses.ischool.berkeley.edu/i213/s99/Readings/p181-card.pdf)); the popular wording is a practitioner article ([Nielsen, 1993, practitioner source](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/response-times-3-important-limits/)); the 1968 paper usually cited for them could not be opened. Measured tolerance in a web-search experiment with 196 students showed behavioural intentions flattening at delays of 4 seconds or more and attitudes at 8 seconds or more, with small explained variance ([Galletta et al., Journal of the AIS, 2004, abstract](https://aisel.aisnet.org/jais/vol5/iss1/1/)). In LLM chat on knowledge tasks, behaviour was robust to time-to-first-token of 2, 9 and 20 seconds, and 2-second responses were rated less thoughtful than slower ones ([Tan et al., CHI 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.06183)). None of these concerns an 8-second scoring wait in a game.

Three parallel judges make the panel as slow as its slowest member. Under an independence assumption, if each judge meets a deadline 95% of the time the panel meets it about 85.7% of the time, and for the panel to meet NFR-02 at the 95th percentile each judge must meet it about 98.3% of the time; this is the notes' arithmetic on the fan-out reasoning of a classic systems paper, which also reports that a hedged second request after a short delay cut a 99.9th-percentile latency from 1,800 ms to 74 ms for 2% more requests in a storage benchmark ([Dean and Barroso, Communications of the ACM, 2013](https://www.barroso.org/publications/TheTailAtScale.pdf)). The hedging figure transfers only as a principle. OpenAI's guidance is that output length dominates latency, that halving output tokens roughly halves latency while halving the prompt gains 1–5%, and that streaming is the most effective way to reduce perceived waiting ([OpenAI latency optimisation, official documentation](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/latency-optimization)).

The design responses are interpretations from the notes. AI-4 streams narration into the dialogue box. AI-5 persists the answer and enqueues scoring before any provider call, which gives NFR-04 by construction, and scoring runs while the engine displays the next canon event from the seed; follow-ups that depend on the score are the exception and need the synchronous path. A deadline with degradation returns the result of the judges that answered in time, marked provisional and reconciled later, and must be validated because a two-judge result is a different statistic. Hedging stays within a family, since hedging to another family would break DC-01. The closing measurement is a load test from the deployment region that records each family's latency distribution for a realistic rubric prompt and computes the panel's 95th percentile under the all-three and two-of-three rules.

### Tooling: Inspect AI offline, a thin TypeScript module at run time

No library provides the judge design out of the box, and the citable science lies in the methods, not the tools. For the offline harness the choice is Inspect AI from the UK AI Security Institute (MIT licence; version 0.3.277 dated 6 October 2026 in its changelog). Its documentation, read on 8 October 2026, lists a built-in median reducer, lets a multi-scorer take a reducer, and ships a Krippendorff's alpha metric with an ordinal level, fed by collecting per-judge ratings; the earlier statement that no library computes alpha is out of date for Inspect ([Inspect, scoring metrics](https://inspect.aisi.org.uk/metrics.html); [Inspect, multiple scorers](https://inspect.aisi.org.uk/multiple-scorers.html)). Its built-in model grader extracts a single grade by regular expression with a majority reducer by default, so a multi-competency rubric still needs a custom scorer ([Inspect, model grading](https://inspect.aisi.org.uk/model-graded.html)). Structured output is documented for nine providers including OpenAI, Anthropic, Google and Mistral, with strict enforcement honoured only by Mistral and OpenAI, so the scorer validates output itself ([Inspect, structured output](https://inspect.aisi.org.uk/structured.html)). This changes one item of the proposed tooling: the standalone `krippendorff` package is licensed GPL-3.0-or-later ([PyPI metadata](https://pypi.org/pypi/krippendorff/json)), and Inspect's built-in metric avoids taking that dependency, although whether Inspect's implementation itself depends on the package was not checked. Whether Inspect has a quadratic weighted kappa metric was also not checked; scikit-learn's `cohen_kappa_score` with quadratic weights covers it. Evidence of research use is an experience report on a repository of more than 70 community evaluations built on Inspect ([Abbas et al., arXiv:2507.06893, preprint](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.06893)).

Among the rejected options, OpenAI announced on 3 June 2026 that its hosted Evals platform is deprecated, read-only from 31 October 2026 and shut down on 30 November 2026, and points users to promptfoo ([OpenAI deprecations, official](https://platform.openai.com/docs/deprecations)); promptfoo, DeepEval and Ragas were set aside for score scales normalised to 0–1, a reason that remains unverified by the team. At run time no judge framework is used: a thin TypeScript module calls the providers with zod schemas and reads token usage from each response, as in the integration choice above. For planning and intervals, the R packages `presize`, `kappaSize` and `krippendorffsalpha` are the tools named in the statistics notes.

## Conclusion: the limits of the evidence and the questions that remain

Read together, the notes change the status of the design in one specific way. The architecture is supported: engine control, expert ownership of keys, reference-and-rubric judging and family separation each have a published basis. The parameters are not: the number of calls per judge, the aggregation rule, the flagging rule, the family in each role and the acceptance thresholds are all places where a source measured in another setting points one way and the project's setting is unmeasured. The practical consequence is that Stage A is no longer a demonstration that the machinery runs. It is the experiment that selects the instrument, and the report's sections 5.2.3 and 5.3.6 should present the fixed decisions as the default arm of named ablations, in the manner of the model-selection protocol in the aBridgeAI precedent, with the difference that JobQuest then validates its judge against humans in Stage B.

A second implication concerns what the project can claim. Because no product or prototype examined publishes agreement between model scores and experts, and because the public baselines for prompted scoring sit below the project's target, a carefully reported result that falls short of QWK 0.70 would still be a contribution, provided it is reported with the confusion matrix, the bias index and the expert–expert ceiling. The claim "AI-native" is safest as a checklist against the five tests, with the adopted definition named and its conflict with the stricter definition acknowledged.
