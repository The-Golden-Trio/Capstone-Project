## 1. Character strengths: VIA Classification, VIA Inventory, VIA Youth; CliftonStrengths in brief

### Takeaway
The VIA scales are reliable and free to *take*, but they cannot be an indicator in JobQuest: the shortest youth form has 96 items, the VIA Institute requires permission for any product or abridged assessment built on the 24 strengths, and the six-virtue grouping "never replicated" in factor analyses. Evidence that strengths work helps adolescents exists but concerns multi-week school programmes with wellbeing outcomes, not a label handed out at onboarding and not career exploration. No Vietnamese validation was found.

### Cited Findings
- **Versions and length.** The VIA Institute lists for adults: VIA-IS-P ("VIA Survey", 96 items), VIA-IS-R (192), VIA-IS-M (96), original VIA-IS (240), VIA-120, VIA-72, a 24-item Signature Strengths Survey and GACS-24/GACS-72; for youth: VIA-Youth-1 (98 items, ages 8–12), VIA-Youth-2 (98 items, ages 13–17), VIA-Youth-96 and VIA-Youth-198 (ages 8–17). All score 24 strengths. **[full text]** — [VIA Institute, Assessments for researchers](https://www.viacharacter.org/researchers/assessments)
- **Terms of use.** The survey, classification and reports are copyrighted. Without approval one may link to the survey, cite it, and "duplicate and distribute the VIA Classification for individual use". Permission is required to "create any products using the VIA Classification (e.g., strengths cards, strengths smart-phone apps, etc.)", to "create an assessment – abridged or otherwise – of the VIA character strengths and virtues", to reprint the classification in a publication, or to present an altered form of it. The same page says the VIA measures "are not recommended for use in personnel selection or placement decisions". **[full text]** — [VIA Institute, Permissions and guidelines](https://www.viacharacter.org/faq/permissions-and-guidelines-for-use-and-interpretation)
- **VIA-Youth psychometrics, from the validation article.** Sample 1: 23,850 respondents aged 10–17 (mean 14.35) from 134 countries, 49.1% US, 29.9% Australia, self-selected through two websites; Sample 2: 471 students aged 13–15 in three English schools. The VIA-Youth has 198 items on a five-point scale, 55 reverse-keyed, 7–9 items per scale; alphas for the 24 scales ranged from .70 to .91. The 96-item short form takes the four items with the highest item-total correlation per scale; its scales correlated on average .82 (range .70–.92) with the long-form scales, with the authors' own caveat that both were scored from one administration. McGrath, R. E., & Walker, D. I. (2016). Factor structure of character strengths in youth: Consistency across ages and measures. *Journal of Moral Education, 45*(4), 400–418. doi:10.1080/03057240.2016.1213709. **[full text, pp. 1–7 of 18; results section not read]** — [author copy on the VIA site](https://conversation.viacharacter.org/pdf/Factor%20structure%20of%20character%20strengths%20in%20youth%20(McGrath%20Walker%202016).pdf). The publisher's page for the VIA-Youth-96 quotes mean alpha .86, scale alphas from .69 (Social Intelligence) to .95 (Spirituality), mean correlation with the long form .84 and a correlation of .36 with life satisfaction. **[full text of the publisher page]** — [VIA-Youth-96 page](https://www.viacharacter.org/researchers/assessments/via-youth-96)
- **Six virtues are not recovered.** The same article summarises the adult factor-analytic literature: "the six-virtue model never replicated", and "the resulting factors did not match any intuitive model of virtues"; a three-factor model (Caring, Inquisitiveness, Self-Control) replicated across measures in adults; in youth the data were "best modeled using four factors". McGrath & Walker (2016), abstract and pp. 3–4. **[full text, pages stated]** — [same PDF](https://conversation.viacharacter.org/pdf/Factor%20structure%20of%20character%20strengths%20in%20youth%20(McGrath%20Walker%202016).pdf). The primary adult study: VIA-IS data from 458,998 US adults gave five second-order factors rather than six, and an alternative set of 24 item-level scales of which 20 overlapped the originals. McGrath, R. E. (2014). Scale- and item-level factor analyses of the VIA Inventory of Strengths. *Assessment, 21*(1), 4–14. doi:10.1177/1073191112450612. **[abstract]** — [Europe PMC record](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=TITLE:%22Scale-%20and%20item-level%20factor%20analyses%20of%20the%20VIA%20Inventory%20of%20Strengths%22&resultType=core&format=json)
- **Signature-strengths interventions, all ages.** Meta-analysis of 14 articles, 29 effect sizes, intervention versus control: positive affect or happiness g = 0.32 (9 studies), depression g = 0.21 (7), life satisfaction g = 0.42 (7 samples), strength use g = 0.55 (2 studies only). Not restricted to adolescents; no career outcome. Schutte, N. S., & Malouff, J. M. (2019). The impact of signature character strengths interventions: A meta-analysis. *Journal of Happiness Studies, 20*(4), 1179–1196. doi:10.1007/s10902-018-9990-2. **[abstract]** — [RePEc record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/spr/jhappi/v20y2019i4d10.1007_s10902-018-9990-2.html)
- **Adolescents.** "Strengths Gym": 319 British students aged 12–14 (218 did strengths exercises within the school curriculum, 101 did not); the exercise group reported significantly higher life satisfaction; the authors call this "encouraging preliminary support"; quasi-experimental. Proctor, C., Tsukayama, E., Wood, A. M., Maltby, J., Eades, J. F., & Linley, P. A. (2011). Strengths Gym: The impact of a character strengths-based intervention on the life satisfaction and well-being of adolescents. *Journal of Positive Psychology, 6*(5), 377–388. doi:10.1080/17439760.2011.594079. **[abstract]** — [Leeds Trinity record](https://research.leedstrinity.ac.uk/en/publications/strengths-gym-the-impact-of-a-character-strengths-based-intervent/)
- **Vietnam.** Vietnamese is not among the VIA-Youth-96 translations. **[full text]** — [VIA-Youth-96 page](https://www.viacharacter.org/researchers/assessments/via-youth-96). For the adult survey, search excerpts of two VIA pages show Vietnamese listed, on one of them flagged "in development" (too few completions to check reliability). No peer-reviewed Vietnamese validation turned up. **[secondary]** — [VIA-IS-R page](https://www.viacharacter.org/researchers/assessments/via-is-r)
- **CliftonStrengths (brief).** Commercial Gallup instrument of paired self-descriptors scoring 34 "themes" (the older technical report excerpted describes 177 item pairs; other versions give other counts). The evidence base is mostly Gallup's own technical reports; a 2024 commentary in *Consulting Psychology Journal* (Reid & Short) judged the evidence for its use in higher education insufficient, and Gallup published a rebuttal. **[secondary: search excerpts only; none of these documents was opened]** — [Gallup technical report page](https://www.gallup.com/cliftonstrengths/en/253790/technical-report.aspx); [Gallup rebuttal](https://www.gallup.com/cliftonstrengths/en/652592/real-reliability-validity-evidence-cliftonstrengths-higher-education.aspx)

### Inferences
- VIA fails as a JobQuest indicator on three independent grounds. Length: 96 items against a five-minute budget that is already spent. Licence: an in-product short strengths quiz, or cards showing the 24 strengths, is exactly what the permission page reserves. Construct: strengths such as gratitude, forgiveness and spirituality describe moral character, and the publisher itself advises against placement use, which is what "which job group to try first" amounts to.
- Six virtues would also put a second six-part model next to RIASEC on one profile page, with a weaker structural basis. That is a short, citable answer if the advisor asks why strengths were left out.
- The intervention literature supports *using* a strength over weeks, with wellbeing as the outcome. It does not support the claim that naming strengths at onboarding improves career exploration; no study making that claim was found.
- A free-text prompt that never shows the 24 names and never sorts the answer into them stays outside the VIA licence and outside the validity problem.
- CliftonStrengths is closed, paid and commercially documented; it is not a candidate.

### Gaps
- McGrath & Walker (2016), results pages 8–18, not read: fit indices and factor loadings are not reported here.
- No study linking VIA strengths to occupational choice or career exploration in adolescents was found.
- No adolescent-only meta-analysis of strengths interventions was found (a doctoral review of 13 articles surfaced in search and was not opened).
- CliftonStrengths item count, price and licence were not read from Gallup's own pages.
- The VIA pages read do not say whether a research licence could cover a public Vietnamese web game; unclear cases are directed to info@viacharacter.org.

## 2. Values: Schwartz basic values (PVQ family) and work values (SWVI-R, MIQ, O\*NET)

### Takeaway
Schwartz's measures are the best-documented option and, unusually, have authorised Vietnamese versions and one Vietnamese adult validation; free ultra-short forms exist (10 and 20 items). They still do not earn an indicator slot: nothing maintained links values to occupations (O\*NET froze its Work Values file at release 30.2 and its last listed data update is release 17.0), no adolescent or Vietnamese-adolescent data was found, and the portrait items come in male and female forms.

### Cited Findings
- **Schwartz's own repository.** Schwartz, S. H. (2021). A repository of Schwartz value scales with instructions and an introduction. *Online Readings in Psychology and Culture, 2*(2). doi:10.9707/2307-0919.1173. Licence shown: **CC BY-NC-ND 3.0** (non-commercial, no derivatives). Contains the SVS (57 items), PVQ-40, PVQ-21 (the European Social Survey Human Values Scale) and PVQ-RR, with scoring keys and "authorized language versions". **Vietnamese versions are listed for the SVS, the PVQ-40 (male and female forms) and the PVQ-RR (male and female forms)**; not for the 21-item form. **[full text of the landing page; the introduction PDF returned 403]** — [ScholarWorks@GVSU](https://scholarworks.gvsu.edu/orpc/vol2/iss2/9/)
- **Ultra-short forms.** TIVI (10 items, one per value) and TwIVI (20 items, two per value), adapted from the PVQ-40; derivation sample N = 38,049, evaluation sample N = 29,143; the authors report the short forms capture the pattern and size of the PVQ's correlations. Terms: "ANYONE CAN USE IT FOR ANY PURPOSE. NO NEED TO ASK ME FOR PERMISSION." No Vietnamese version listed. Sandy, C. J., Gosling, S. D., Schwartz, S. H., & Koelkebeck, T. (2016, online). The development and validation of brief and ultrabrief measures of values. *Journal of Personality Assessment*. doi:10.1080/00223891.2016.1231115. **[full text of the authors' page; article not read, so no reliability figure is quoted]** — [Gosling lab](https://gosling.psy.utexas.edu/two-short-measures-of-values-tivi-and-twivi/)
- **Vietnam: the PVQ-40 holds structurally in adults.** Vietnamese PVQ-40 given to adults in Ho Chi Minh City (n = 521), Huế (n = 538) and Hà Nội (n = 533): MDS and CFA supported the theorised circular structure, but adjacent values had to be combined in each sample; partial scalar invariance across the three regions; value hierarchies "differed substantially from the pan-cultural hierarchy". Różycka-Tran, J., Truong, T. K. H., Cieciuch, J., & Schwartz, S. H. (2017). Universals and specifics of the structure and hierarchy of basic human values in Vietnam. *Health Psychology Report, 5*(3), 193–204. **[abstract]** — [journal page](https://czasopisma.bg.ug.edu.pl/index.php/HPR/article/view/9144)
- **Vietnam: World Values Survey.** Wave 5 was fielded in Vietnam from 1 October to 30 November 2006, N = 1,495, population aged 18 and over (data file: 414 variables). **[full text of the catalogue record]** — [IHSN catalogue](https://catalog.ihsn.org/catalog/8953). A Wave 7 Vietnam sample (2020, N = 1,200) also exists. **[secondary]** — [World Database of Happiness entry](https://worlddatabaseofhappiness.eur.nl/studies/europeanworld-values-survey-2021-study-vn-2020-17299). Whether the ten-item Schwartz block was asked in Vietnam is **not confirmed** (see Gaps). Both samples are adults.
- **Interests and values overlap in a readable pattern.** Two studies of career-counselling clients (N = 97 and N = 545): Conventional interests go with conformity, security and tradition values; Enterprising with power and achievement; Social with benevolence; Artistic and Investigative with self-direction and universalism; Realistic interests were unrelated to values. Sagiv, L. (2002). Vocational interests and basic values. *Journal of Career Assessment, 10*(2), 233–257. doi:10.1177/1069072702010002007. **[secondary: abstract seen through a search excerpt]** — [SAGE record](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/1069072702010002007)
- **Value fit and satisfaction.** Round one already read Kristof-Brown et al. (2005): person–organisation fit (largely value congruence) relates to job satisfaction at ρ = .44 (k = 65), and directly *perceived* fit relates far more strongly than objectively computed fit. **[full text of a scanned copy, per round one]** — [scanned PDF](https://leeds-faculty.colorado.edu/dahe7472/kristof%20zimmerman%202005.pdf)
- **O\*NET work values: frozen, not deleted.** From release 30.3 the "Work Values" file is "no longer updated or included in the O\*NET Database"; "developers and researchers may continue to use the latest version of these files from the 30.2 database release". The update history lists Work Values data changes at release 13.0 (812 occupations, together with Interests), 14.0 (96 occupations) and, last, **17.0 (83 occupations)**; nothing later. **[full text]** — [O\*NET 30.3 update appendix](https://www.onetcenter.org/dictionary/30.3/text/appendix_updates.html). The Work Importance Locator was retired on 3 June 2024 (round one). — [O\*NET WIP page](https://www.onetcenter.org/WIP.html)
- **Commercial work-values inventories.** Super's Work Values Inventory–Revised (Kuder, Inc.) scores 12 work values (Achievement, Challenge, Co-Workers, Creativity, Income, Independence, Lifestyle, Prestige, Security, Supervision, Variety, Workplace). The Minnesota Importance Questionnaire scores 20 needs grouped into six values (achievement, altruism, autonomy, comfort, safety, status) and is a paid paper instrument from Vocational Psychology Research, University of Minnesota. **[secondary: search excerpts of Buros listings and encyclopaedia entries; item counts not confirmed]** — [Buros, SWVI-R](https://marketplace.unl.edu/buros/super-s-work-values-inventory-revised.html); [UMN VPR, MIQ](https://vpr.psych.umn.edu/miq)

### Inferences
- The TwIVI is the only instrument found in either round that is short, free for any use, co-authored by Schwartz, and built on a model checked on Vietnamese adults. If the team ever adds a second self-report indicator, this is the candidate. It still fails here: about two more minutes on a full onboarding; the portrait format ("it is important to him/her to…") exists in male and female versions, which pushes toward asking gender, a field the proposal refuses to collect; its content (tradition, religion, obedience, wealth, power) is the "worldview" territory round one advised against scoring for minors; and there is no occupation data to connect it to.
- Because RIASEC scores already line up with values in a patterned way (Sagiv 2002), a values chart would partly re-describe the interest chart. Self-insight gained per item is lower than it looks.
- The O\*NET 30.2 Work Values file can be cited as a frozen historical descriptor, but with a last listed update at release 17.0 it should not be presented as current, and should not feed suggestions.
- "What do you want a job to give you?" is nonetheless a question a 15-year-old can answer in their own words. That fits one optional open prompt whose answer is quoted back, not scored, and stored with K3 under "bạn muốn thành".
- Perceived fit beats computed fit for values too (Kristof-Brown), which again points to asking after play ("điều gì ở vai này bạn thấy đáng làm?") rather than measuring before play.

### Gaps
- PVQ-RR item count and the minimum age Schwartz recommends for the PVQ were not on the landing page; the PDF could not be opened. Do not quote either from memory.
- Whether the Schwartz items were fielded in Vietnam's WVS Wave 5 or Wave 7 could not be confirmed: the catalogue pages do not expose variable labels. The team would need to open the WVS codebook.
- No study of any Schwartz measure in Vietnamese adolescents was opened (a search excerpt mentions a Vietnamese PVQ-40 study of adolescents and their parents, N = 2,226, on value transmission; not located).
- **Do work values differ among IT occupations? No source found.**
- Do work values predict occupational choice? Only search-excerpt evidence: a University of Illinois record of a meta-analysis (66 studies; value scores about .21–.30 and value-fit indices about .38–.53 with work outcomes) and a policy-capturing study of job choice. Neither was opened; not usable as stated.
- SWVI-R and MIQ item counts, prices and licences were not read from the publishers.

## 3. Self-reported strengths and weaknesses, and the "others-report" framing

### Takeaway
The reflected-best-self exercise is a practice with a theory paper behind it and one small adolescent quasi-experiment with mixed results; it is not an evidence-based technique yet. Nothing found supports asking adolescents to name their weaknesses, and nothing found shows it harms; the case against it rests on round one's feedback literature, not on a direct test.

### Cited Findings
- **What the exercise is.** In the Reflected Best Self Exercise (RBSE; Roberts, Dutton, Spreitzer, Heaphy & Quinn, 2005, *Academy of Management Review*) participants collect "short behavioral descriptions of times when they have been at their very best" from "an array of significant people", colleagues but also friends and family, look for patterns, write a "reflected best-self portrait" and an action plan. Described in Spreitzer, G., Stephens, J. P., & Sweetman, D. (2009). The Reflected Best Self field experiment with adolescent leaders: Exploring the psychological resources associated with feedback source and valence. *Journal of Positive Psychology* (volume and pages not verified). **[full text of the authors' manuscript, pp. 1–6 of about 40; the 2005 AMR article itself was not opened]** — [author manuscript](https://webuser.bus.umich.edu/spreitze/pdfs/bestselfrevision.pdf)
- **State of evidence, in the developers' own words (2009).** Reports of the exercise "have generally been positive", but "the exercise has not been subject to empirical testing". Spreitzer et al. (2009), manuscript p. 4. **[full text, page stated]** — [same manuscript](https://webuser.bus.umich.edu/spreitze/pdfs/bestselfrevision.pdf)
- **The adolescent test.** Field quasi-experiment, 108 adolescent leaders, 2×2: feedback valence (strengths-only versus strengths plus improvement-oriented) by feedback source (professional only, such as teachers and coaches, versus professional plus personal, such as friends and family). Feedback from combined professional and personal sources was associated with more positive emotional, agentic and relational resources. "Little support was found for the hypothesis that strengths-based feedback generates more positive emotional, agentic, or relational resources." Spreitzer et al. (2009), abstract. **[full text, manuscript p. 2]** — [same manuscript](https://webuser.bus.umich.edu/spreitze/pdfs/bestselfrevision.pdf)
- **Accuracy of self-rated ability.** Round one: self-assessed ability correlates on average .29 with measured performance, weaker for broad ratings of unfamiliar tasks (Zell & Krizan, 2014). **[abstract, per round one]** — [APS journal page](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/perspectives/1745691613518075/)
- **A neighbouring exercise with a real trial.** "Best possible self" writing, 200 participants aged 11–15, three-arm randomised online trial: higher positive affect after the exercise than in both control groups; no significant reduction in negative affect. This is the exercise behind K3, not the reflected best self. **[secondary: search excerpt of a PMC article, not opened]** — [PMC12018004](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12018004/)

### Inferences
- The proposal's backup question "Bạn bè hay nhờ bạn giúp việc gì nhất?" borrows the *idea* of the RBSE (strengths seen through others' eyes, as concrete episodes) but not its mechanism: in the RBSE other people actually write the feedback, and the one supportive finding is about who the feedback comes from. Asking a player to imagine what friends would say is untested. It is still a reasonable open prompt, because it asks for behaviour that happened, and round one's rules already favour that.
- The adolescent study did not find that strengths-only feedback beats strengths plus improvement points. So the literature gives no licence to claim "we avoid weaknesses because research shows strengths-only is better". The honest reason for not asking about weaknesses is different: a self-named weakness is a broad self-evaluation of low accuracy (.29 on average), it invites a label, and JobQuest has a better source for "what was hard", namely play.
- An AI-written description built from the player's own answer is closer to self-affirmation than to reflected feedback. It should not be presented as "what others see in you".

### Gaps
- Roberts et al. (2005) was not opened; it is known here only through the 2009 manuscript's description.
- **No study was found on asking adolescents to self-report weaknesses** (effects on mood, accuracy, or dropout). Stated plainly: no evidence either way.
- No randomised trial of the reflected-best-self exercise in adolescents was found; no evidence at all for a self-imagined "what would friends say" variant.
- A study reported by Michigan Ross news (business students, interview performance after the RBSE) surfaced only as a press item and was not opened.

## 4. Ikigai: origin, the four-circle diagram, measures, use in career guidance

### Takeaway
Ikigai is a real Japanese wellbeing concept with solid epidemiology behind a one-question measure and a nine-item scale, all on adults and all about health and wellbeing. The four-circle "what you love / are good at / the world needs / can be paid for" diagram is not Japanese and not ikigai: a Western blogger relabelled an existing "purpose" diagram. No controlled evidence for the diagram in career guidance was opened. Citing the concept with its health literature is safe; citing the diagram as a Japanese framework or as an evidence-based career model is not.

### Cited Findings
- **Origin of the concept.** Psychiatrist Mieko Kamiya popularised ikigai in her 1966 book *Ikigai ni tsuite* (On the Meaning of Life). Later Japanese definitions describe it as the sources that make life feel worth living and the feeling itself (Inoue, 2000), or a sense of accomplishment and fulfilment from freely chosen activities (Kumano, 2018, *Applied Research in Quality of Life, 13*(2)). **[tertiary: Wikipedia summary; the Japanese sources were not opened]** — [Wikipedia, "Ikigai"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ikigai)
- **Health evidence.** Ohsaki Study: 43,391 Japanese adults followed for seven years (3,048 deaths), asked one question, "Do you have ikigai in your life?". Those without ikigai had higher all-cause mortality (hazard ratio 1.5, 95% CI 1.3–1.7), cardiovascular mortality (1.6, 1.3–2.0) and external-cause mortality (1.9, 1.1–3.3); cancer mortality was not significantly different (1.3, 1.0–1.6). Sone, T., Nakaya, N., Ohmori, K., Shimazu, T., Higashiguchi, M., Kakizaki, M., Kikuchi, N., Kuriyama, S., & Tsuji, I. (2008). Sense of life worth living (ikigai) and mortality in Japan: Ohsaki Study. *Psychosomatic Medicine, 70*(6), 709–715. doi:10.1097/psy.0b013e31817e7e64. **[abstract]** — [Europe PMC record](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=EXT_ID:18596247&resultType=core&format=json)
- **Validated measure.** Ikigai-9: nine items, five-point scale, three intended dimensions (positive emotions toward life, active attitude toward one's future, acknowledgement of the meaning of one's existence). Original: Imai, T., Osada, H., & Nishimura, Y. (2012), *Japanese Journal of Public Health, 59*(7), 433–439. English validation (Fido, Kotera & Asano; *International Journal of Mental Health and Addiction, 18*, 1352–1359): 349 UK participants, a single factor fitted better than three, high internal reliability, test–retest still needed. German validation (Hajek, Imai, Zwar & König, 2024, *Societies, 14*(3), 39): n = 5,000, alpha .88, three factors supported. **[secondary: search excerpts of repository abstracts; none opened]** — [University of Derby record](https://repository.derby.ac.uk/item/92vxq/english-translation-and-validation-of-the-ikigai-9-in-a-uk-sample); [MDPI article](https://www.mdpi.com/2075-4698/14/3/39)
- **Where the diagram came from.** The four-circle diagram "does not originate in Japan"; it was created by Marc Winn and was "likely inspired by" a "purpose" diagram made in 2011 by Andrés Zuzunaga, with the word "purpose" replaced by "ikigai"; it has been called "catchy but misleading". Wikipedia cites *The Economist*, "What Japan makes of ikigai" (27 October 2022). **[tertiary: Wikipedia; The Economist article not opened]** — [Wikipedia, "Ikigai"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ikigai). Search excerpts add that Winn's post dates from 2014, that he later wrote he had "merged" a purpose Venn diagram with Dan Buettner's talk about ikigai and longevity, that Zuzunaga is described as a Spanish astrologer, and that the purpose diagram first appeared in a 2012 Spanish self-help book. **[secondary: blog and podcast excerpts; Winn's own posts not opened]** — [Ikigai Tribe podcast page](https://ikigaitribe.com/ikigai/podcast05/)
- **Career guidance evidence.** Search surfaced only: a 2024 Thai study of Grade 11 students reporting better career-planning scores after an ikigai-based guidance package than in a control group (sample size and design details not in the excerpt); a Spanish conference paper describing an ikigai-themed platform used by 1,878 students with no comparison group; and a 2025 conceptual paper in *Societies* (doi:10.3390/soc15090264) that itself warns of oversimplification and commercialisation. **[secondary: search excerpts; none opened]** — [Thai journal record](https://so05.tci-thaijo.org/index.php/edjour_stou/article/view/274132); [conceptual paper record](https://www.citedrive.com/en/discovery/ikigai-as-a-framework-for-career-counselling-and-study-choices-conceptual-and-practical-perspectives-in-the-slovenian-context/)

### Inferences
- Two different things share one name. The Japanese construct (life feels worth living; sources can be family, hobbies, small daily things) has adult health evidence and says nothing about choosing an occupation. The diagram is a career-advice graphic with no measurement behind it.
- The diagram's centre claims one spot where love, skill, need and pay coincide. Shown to a player that is "you fit job X" in picture form, the exact message the project forbids, and it treats "what you are good at" as something to self-declare, against the rule that competence comes only from play.
- For the report: the safe sentence is of the form "ikigai is a Japanese concept of a life worth living, studied mainly in relation to health (Sone et al., 2008); the popular four-circle diagram is a later Western graphic and is not used here". An advisor alert to pseudo-science will also notice that the diagram's reported author is an astrologer; that detail is secondary-sourced and should not be asserted in the report without opening a primary source.
- The three questions the diagram bundles are already separated in the proposal by source: what you like (self-told cards), what you can do (play), what the job involves (occupation data). Keeping them apart is the design; a Venn overlap would undo it.

### Gaps
- Kamiya (1966), Imai et al. (2012), Fido et al. and *The Economist* (2022) were not opened; Winn's and Zuzunaga's own pages were not opened. The diagram's history is therefore tertiary and secondary throughout, although every source seen agrees.
- No peer-reviewed study testing the four-circle diagram as an intervention with a credible control was opened; the Thai study needs its full text read before any use.
- No ikigai research on adolescents or on Vietnamese samples was found.

## 5. Ngũ hành and birth-year systems in Vietnam

### Takeaway
A person's "mệnh" comes from the birth year in the sixty-year can-chi cycle, and the element changes only every two years, so a whole school class shares one, exactly like the animal sign. Unlike Western sun signs, these beliefs measurably move real decisions in Vietnam (birth timing, marriage), and the system's core content is who suits whom. No evidence was found on hiring, on naming, or on ngũ hành used as a neutral motif.

### Cited Findings
- **The cycle and its elements.** The sexagenary (can-chi) cycle was part of the traditional calendars of Japan, Korea and Vietnam. Each of the five elements covers two consecutive heavenly stems (Giáp, Ất = Mộc; Bính, Đinh = Hoả; Mậu, Kỷ = Thổ; Canh, Tân = Kim; Nhâm, Quý = Thuỷ), so two consecutive years share a stem element: 2008–2009 Earth, 2010–2011 Metal, 2012–2013 Water. **[tertiary: Wikipedia tables]** — [Wikipedia, "Sexagenary cycle"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sexagenary_cycle); [Wikipedia tiếng Việt, "Địa chi"](https://vi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Địa_chi)
- **Nạp âm, the "mệnh" people quote.** A separate table assigns one of the five elements to each can-chi pair. A Vietnamese academic paper states that old books give two mathematical models for this assignment and proposes a third in two steps (five tones assigned to the pairs, then five elements to the tones), yielding a compact table. Lê Thành Lân (author given as "L. T. Lân") (1996). Một vài mô hình nạp ngũ hành cho các cặp can chi. *Journal of Computer Science and Cybernetics* (Vietnam Academy of Science and Technology), *12*(1), 47–56. doi:10.15625/1813-9663/12/1/8045. **[abstract; author's full name inferred from initials, verify]** — [VJS page](https://vjs.ac.vn/jcc/article/view/8045). Popular tables give thirty named nạp âm, each shared by two consecutive years (Giáp Tý and Ất Sửu = Hải Trung Kim; Bính Dần and Đinh Mão = Lô Trung Hoả). **[secondary: search excerpt of a popular site]** — [Mytour blog](https://mytour.vn/vi/blog/bai-viet/phan-tich-van-menh-qua-ngu-hanh-nap-am-phan-1-hanh-kim.html)
- **Compatibility is the system's main use.** The twelve branches form four trines of mutually compatible signs (Rat–Dragon–Monkey, Ox–Snake–Rooster, Tiger–Horse–Dog, Rabbit–Goat–Pig; "tam hợp") and six opposed pairs (Rat–Horse, Ox–Goat, Tiger–Monkey, Rabbit–Rooster, Dragon–Dog, Snake–Pig), which practitioners consult "for love and marriage"; the Vietnamese zodiac replaces Ox with Water Buffalo and Rabbit with Cat. **[tertiary: Wikipedia; the pairs were read from a table]** — [Wikipedia, "Chinese zodiac"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_zodiac)
- **Birth timing.** In Vietnam birth cohorts in years believed auspicious are about 12% larger; comparing siblings, those born in auspicious years have about two more months of schooling. Because the Vietnamese horoscope is gender-specific, the authors attribute the gap to those children being planned and wanted, not to the horoscope being right. Do, Q.-T., & Phung, T. D. (2010). The importance of being wanted. *American Economic Journal: Applied Economics, 2*(4), 236–253. doi:10.1257/app.2.4.236. **[abstract]** — [AEA page](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/app.2.4.236)
- **Marriage.** A structural model of marriage matching in Vietnam finds that Tử Vi beliefs about marriage fortune carry weight equal to 6.5% of the importance of the whole age-and-education profile; "auspicious" couples receive 11% more transfers from the extended family, 28% under hardship; the authors read the belief as a commitment device between families. Do, Q.-A., Ciscato, E., & Nguyen, K.-T., "Astrology and matrimony: Social reinforcement of religious beliefs on marriage matching in Vietnam". **[abstract of an unpublished paper, from a seminar announcement dated 2 October 2025; data sources not stated]** — [HKUST seminar page](https://calendar.hkust.edu.hk/node/45308)
- **Stereotype without validity still discriminates.** Round one: in China, signs did not predict personality (N = 173,709) or job performance (N = 32,878), yet sign stereotypes produced discrimination in simulated dating and hiring (Lu et al., 2020); in a 2017 online survey Vietnam had the highest share in nine Asia-Pacific markets taking horoscopes seriously (75%, n = 1,003). **[abstract and full text respectively, per round one]** — [Lu et al. 2020](https://gosling.psy.utexas.edu/wp-content/uploads/2020/03/Lu-et-al.-2020-Disentangling-stereotypes-from-social-reality-Astrological-stereotypes-and-discrimination-in-China-JPSP.pdf); [YouGov 2017](https://yougov.com/articles/19426-horoscope-and-behaviour-change)

### Inferences
- **Cohort problem, confirmed and wider.** Animal sign: one per birth year. Stem element and nạp âm: one per two birth years. A Grade 10 class would be almost entirely one mệnh. As a personal attribute it carries no information about the individual and makes every classmate "the same type".
- **Higher risk than the constellation layer.** The Western zodiac layer in round one could be made harmless by showing astronomy and myth. Ngũ hành has no such neutral content: its everyday meaning is tương sinh / tương khắc, who supports and who clashes with whom, and Vietnamese families demonstrably act on birth-year beliefs when planning children and marriages. A game that shows "mệnh Hoả" next to job groups will be read as "nghề hợp mệnh", whatever the disclaimer says. Assigning mệnh by birth year is also a type label derived from personal data, which breaks two hard constraints at once.
- **As a pure motif.** Five colours or five shapes named Kim, Mộc, Thuỷ, Hoả, Thổ could decorate the interface if nothing ties them to a person. The tempting use, one element per job group (the proposal has exactly five groups), is the dangerous one, because it recreates "element ↔ career" in one step. Without that mapping the motif adds little that five ordinary theme colours would not, so the benefit is small and the misreading risk is real. This is a judgement; no study of such a motif was found.
- **If a Vietnamese note is wanted,** round one's option stands: the animal of the *current* year as a shared seasonal theme at Tết, identical for everyone.
- For the advisor: the defensible statement is that these systems are culturally important and studied by economists as beliefs that change behaviour, which is the reason JobQuest does not attach them to a person.

### Gaps
- The Vietnamese term "tứ hành xung" (three groups of four clashing branches) was not found in any source opened; only the six opposed pairs were read. Verify before writing it into the report.
- The nạp âm table itself was not read from a scholarly source; only its existence and mathematical structure (abstract) and two example pairs (popular site). The remark that Lê Quý Đôn's *Vân đài loại ngữ* records a method of calculation comes from a search excerpt and is not confirmed.
- No Viện Nghiên cứu Hán Nôm or encyclopaedia source on ngũ hành in daily life was opened; a Vietnam Academy of Social Sciences document "Âm dương – Ngũ hành" surfaced but was not read.
- **No research found on these beliefs in hiring or in naming in Vietnam.** The hiring evidence is Chinese and simulated (round one).
- The marriage paper is unpublished as far as could be seen; the Do & Phung full text was not read (the list of auspicious years and the gender rule are not in the abstract).
- No evidence on Vietnamese adolescents' own belief in or use of mệnh.
