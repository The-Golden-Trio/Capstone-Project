6The problemCorrected

The decision is made blind

People choose an occupation without knowing what the work is.

1The obstacle is informational

In Gati, Krausz and Osipow’s validated taxonomy, insufficient occupational information is a principal category of career decision difficulty, distinct from indecisiveness and from poor self-knowledge (Gati, Krausz & Osipow, 1996). Its structure was recently confirmed across 13 countries (Levin et al., 2023).

2What guidance offers instead

Interest and personality inventories elicit what a person believes about themselves. They do not supply information about the work, so they cannot address that category of difficulty.

The evidence on MBTI, both sides
Weeks

Early retest studies: many respondents re-typed within weeks (Pittenger, 2005)

0.81–0.86

Publisher data: scale reliability (Myers & Briggs Foundation, 2023)

The real limit is what is measured: a stable preference is still a preference, not information about the work.

Two implications: the deficit that most needs fixing is informational, and the dominant instruments are structurally unable to fix it.

Changed since 18 Sep: MBTI evidence presented on both sides; Gati now paired with the 2023 cross-country confirmation.
The framing has not changed, but I state it precisely because everything downstream depends on it. Gati's taxonomy separates insufficient information about occupations from indecisiveness and from poor self-knowledge, and that distinction is the basis of the project. The taxonomy dates from 1996, but its structure was confirmed across 13 countries in 2023 (Levin et al.), so it is not an outdated source. On MBTI: last time we showed a single figure, 39 to 76 percent re-typed after five weeks, as if it were settled. That figure comes from retest studies of 1962 to 1979 and is disputed; the publisher reports scale reliability of 0.81 to 0.86. We now show both sides. More importantly, the argument no longer depends on MBTI being unreliable. Even a perfectly stable instrument measures a preference, and a preference is not information about the work, so it cannot address the informational difficulty Gati identifies.
7The alternativeCorrected

Honest previews of a job improve fit

Established, not speculative: four decades of meta-analytic evidence.

1Phillips, 1998: the effect

Meta-analysis of 40 studies. Realistic job previews reduce inflated expectations, increase self-selection, improve performance and lower voluntary turnover. Richer audiovisual previews outperform written ones (Phillips, 1998).

2Earnest et al., 2011: the mechanism

Meta-analytic path analysis over 52 studies and about 17,000 participants identifies perceived honesty as the mediating mechanism. The effect depends on candour, not on information volume (Earnest, Allen & Landis, 2011).

No meta-analysis has since superseded it. The most recent umbrella review identifies it as the current synthesis (Graef, 2020).

The constraint was never evidence, it was delivery. Previews required workplace exposure, or content authored one occupation at a time.
Changed since 18 Sep: shown that the 2011 meta-analysis is still the current synthesis; its age reflects the field, not an outdated choice.
The realistic job preview literature is forty years old and consistent across four meta-analyses. Two findings matter for our design. Phillips found richer audiovisual previews more effective than written ones, which bears on why we deliver this as an interactive scene rather than a description. And Earnest and colleagues found the effect is mediated by perceived honesty: it depends on the preview's candour, not merely its informational content. That is why business rule BR-07 mandates that occupational representation include the unfavourable aspects of the work. On your comment about old references: the 2011 meta-analysis is still the current synthesis. The most recent umbrella review, Graef 2020, identifies it as such, and no newer meta-analysis has replaced it. Its age reflects the state of the field, not an outdated choice by us. The reason this approach has not been applied to career exploration is delivery cost, and that is the constraint generative models change.

11Foundation 2ANew

Choices as trade-offs

Not right or wrong: situational judgment tests, the established method behind our choice tasks

1What they are

Work situations with response options that differ in effectiveness: a “low-fidelity simulation” (Motowidlo, Dunnette & Carter, 1990).

2The evidence

102 validity coefficients, 10,640 people: validly related to job performance, ρ = .34 (McDaniel et al., 2001).

Items measure distinct constructs: interpersonal skill, teamwork, leadership (Christian et al., 2010).

3How they are built

Experts supply critical incidents (Flanagan, 1954) → options are written from real behaviour → a different group of experts rates each option’s effectiveness to form the key (Whetzel et al., 2020).

Design consequence: each option carries an expert-rated effectiveness profile across competencies, so a choice can trade one skill against another.
This is new, and it answers a gap in how we described our choice tasks. Situational judgment tests are the established method for presenting work situations whose options differ in effectiveness rather than in right or wrong. Motowidlo called them low-fidelity simulations; McDaniel's meta-analysis of 102 coefficients shows they validly predict job performance; Christian showed items can target distinct constructs. The construction method is what we adopt: critical incidents from experts, options written from real behaviour, and a separate group of experts rating each option's effectiveness to form the key. One design choice to mention: the instruction matters. "What would you do?" relates more to personality; "what is most effective?" relates more to cognitive ability (McDaniel et al., 2007). Our narrative framing uses "what do you do?", which suits exploration.
12Foundation 2BCorrected

Career theory: fit is not a fixed verdict

Four traditions, and two of them say something most career systems ignore.

1Holland

Occupational choice as person–environment congruence. Underlies the interest inventories in widespread use, including the public-domain instrument distributed with O*NET (Holland, 1997).

2Social cognitive career theory

Self-efficacy, built principally through mastery experience, determines interest formation and choice: accessible to a system where users perform tasks, inaccessible to one that only elicits self-description (Lent et al., 1994). → How self-efficacy forms, and what we can build: next slide.

3Super

Vocational self-concept develops over time, so fit is changing rather than fixed (Super, 1980).

4Krumboltz

Unplanned events are a normal and potentially productive part of career development, not disruption (Krumboltz, 2009).

Consequence. Super and Krumboltz jointly justify two features: probabilistic life events (FR-14), and treating work–life balance as an attribute of an occupation rather than an externality (the five-attribute rating, FR-11).
Changed since 18 Sep: the social cognitive mechanism is now analysed in full (slide 13).
Four traditions inform this field, and I want to draw out the two that most systems ignore. Holland and social cognitive career theory are the familiar ones, and SCCT matters to us particularly, because mastery experience is a mechanism a performance-based system can reach and a questionnaire cannot. You asked whether there is a social cognitive aspect; the next slide analyses it in full. Super and Krumboltz jointly support a position career systems rarely represent: that a career decision sits inside a whole life. That is the theoretical justification for two features: our roguelike life events exist because Krumboltz says unplanned events are normal, and our five-attribute rating includes work–life balance because Super says fit changes over a life.

Your question was whether there is a social cognitive aspect. There is, and this is it. Bandura identifies four sources of self-efficacy. JobQuest supplies the strongest one, mastery experience, directly: the user performs the task. It supplies verbal persuasion through feedback and guidance. It supplies little vicarious experience, because there are no peers to observe, and it does not target affective state. The evidence that mastery is the strongest source comes from a meta-analysis, it has been extended to career exploration specifically, and a three-wave study of 1,512 students confirmed it in 2025. The limit matters as much: self-efficacy is task-specific, and our interaction is text-based, so the confidence we build is for written analysis, prioritisation and decisions, not for speaking or interviewing. Slide 20 makes that limit explicit.
14Foundation 3Corrected

Generation and judging

LLMs can judge open responses, but judging must be validated in four ways.

1Feasibility

Agreement with human preferences above 80%, comparable to inter-human agreement, while the same work documents position, verbosity and self-preference bias (Zheng et al., 2023).

2Method

Structured chain-of-thought against an explicit rubric improves alignment, with a residual preference for model-generated text (Liu et al., 2023).

3Validation

Raw agreement overstates chance-corrected performance: a ~41-point gap between exact-match agreement and kappa on one benchmark (“Reliability without validity”, 2026). On difficult pairs with objective labels, the strongest evaluator reached ~64% (Tan et al., 2025).

The four requirements

✓ Report chance-corrected agreement: quadratic weighted kappa (slide 43) → evaluation design

✓ Draw the evaluator from a different model family than the generator → DC-01

✓ Control option ordering and response length → assessment pipeline

✓ Report performance stratified by item difficulty → reference-set strata

Changed since 18 Sep: the generator/assessor separation is now design constraint DC-01; the agreement metric is QWK.
This is where our methodological contribution sits, so I connect it forward. The literature on language models as judges has gone through three stages, and the third is the one most systems skip. Raw agreement systematically overstates chance-corrected performance, with a 41-point gap on one benchmark, and high test–retest consistency can coexist with severe position bias, so consistency is not evidence of validity. Four requirements follow, and each appears somewhere concrete in our specification. Chance-corrected reporting is in the evaluation design, and the metric is now quadratic weighted kappa, explained on slide 43. Cross-family evaluation was a business rule on 18 September; it is now design constraint DC-01, because it is a constraint on the implementation rather than a domain policy. Ordering and length control are in the assessment pipeline, and difficulty stratification is why the reference set has strata.

16The systemCorrected

What JobQuest is, and who it serves

Occupations as performable tasks; competence assessed from the performance.

Three presentation conventions, each chosen against evidence

RPG progression. Levels earned by demonstrated competence. Role-play shows the strongest genre-level association with skill acquisition: overall effect ≈ 0.82, largest on skill acquisition (“Effectiveness of the role-play method”, 2025).

Visual-novel branching scenes. Narrative is among the strongest moderators in the gamification meta-analyses.

Roguelike career and life events. Narrative variety only: explicitly not coupled to any reward schedule or monetisation, per the harm evidence on randomised rewards (“Randomized reward mechanisms”, 2023).

Four user groups

Explorer. The end user; everything else serves them.

Domain expert. Senior practitioner on one or more occupations: co-authors content, play-tests, validates scenarios.

Content administrator (business administrator). One per occupation: co-authors with its experts; publishes and retires it.

System administrator (IT administrator). Creates occupations, assigns staff; models, coverage, cost.

Many-to-many: an occupation can have several experts; an expert can serve several occupations.

Changed since 18 Sep: responsibility per occupation made explicit: exactly one content administrator and one or more domain experts, who co-author its content.
Three conventions, each selected against evidence rather than by preference. Progression is competence-based because role-play carries the strongest genre-level evidence for skill acquisition. Scenes are branching narrative because narrative is the strongest moderator in the meta-analyses. Events are probabilistic, but that randomisation governs narrative variety only; it is not attached to any reward schedule, given the evidence linking randomised reward mechanisms to problem gambling. The correction is on the right. There are four user groups: the explorer, the domain expert, the content administrator, who is the business administrator, and the system administrator. What changed since 18 September is that responsibility for each occupation is now explicit. Every occupation has exactly one content administrator and one or more domain experts, and the relationship with experts is many-to-many: an occupation can draw on several experts, and an expert can support several occupations. The experts and the content administrator co-author the occupation's content inside the system. The next slide explains who the experts are and how they contribute.

This answers your question of 23 September: what does "task" include? A task is one scenario within an occupation level, with a role, a team and a context, asking for one response. Why generation and assessment are reported separately

Three distinct failure modes: biased judgment, unstable judgment, and judgment failing selectively on hard items. Each is diagnosable only when reported separately; a single accuracy figure hides which one occurred.

This answers your question of whether the evaluation is feasible, and the answer is a design choice: two stages, because experts arrive in stages. What counts as success

Established thresholds, not ones we chose

Why quadratic weighted kappa

Chance correction is why we use kappa at all: raw agreement overstates performance. Rubric scores are ordinal: plain Cohen’s κ treats a 1-point and a 3-point disagreement alike; QWK weights larger disagreements more.

This answers your question: why Cohen's kappa, and what value is acceptable? We use kappa at all because of chance correction; raw agreement overstates performance, by 41 points on one benchmark. But our rubric scores are ordinal, and plain Cohen's kappa treats a one-point and a three-point disagreement as equally wrong. Quadratic weighted kappa weights larger disagreements more, so it is the right refinement. For acceptance we did not choose our own numbers. For RP-2 we adopt the ETS framework for automated scoring: QWK of at least 0.70 between automated and human scores, a standardised mean difference of at most 0.15, and a degradation of at most 0.10 from human–human to automated–human agreement, which is exactly what RP-2 already asks. For RP-1 we use content validity indices with Polit's thresholds, McNemar's test for seeded against unseeded, and Krippendorff's alpha of at least .800 for expert agreement. Fact preservation at 95 percent is our proposed target. Everything is reported per role and per difficulty stratum.
