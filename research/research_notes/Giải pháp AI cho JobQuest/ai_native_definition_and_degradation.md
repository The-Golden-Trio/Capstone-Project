## 1. Which reputable sources define "AI-native" (or "AI-first"), and what criteria do they share?

### Takeaway
There is no single authoritative definition: every serious source opened says the term is unsettled, and no ITU-R, 3GPP or ETSI normative text defining it was found. The usable anchors are Ericsson's white-paper definition (vendor, but the one other bodies cite), the ITU-T FG-AINN description ("not merely an add-on ... deeply embedded in the core architecture"), a 2025 software-engineering grey-literature review (preprint), and a 2026 survey of AI-native games (preprint) whose counterfactual test is the most directly testable. They converge on four ideas: AI is intrinsic to the core function, it is present across the whole lifecycle, the system is built around data/knowledge flows, and AI needs its own management and trust machinery.

### Cited Findings

**Ericsson (vendor white paper; the definition most often cited by others)**
- Title "Defining AI native: A key enabler for advanced intelligent telecom networks"; contributors Massimo Iovene, Leif Jonsson, Dinand Roeland, Mirko D'Angelo, Göran Hall, Melike Erol-Kantarci, Jitendra Manocha. **[vendor]**, not peer-reviewed. The web page shows no publication date. — [Ericsson white paper](https://www.ericsson.com/en/reports-and-papers/white-papers/ai-native)
- Definition (returned by the fetch tool as five consecutive segments, joined here): "AI native is the concept of having intrinsic trustworthy AI capabilities, where AI is a natural part of the functionality, in terms of design, deployment, operation, and maintenance. An AI native implementation leverages a data-driven and knowledge-based ecosystem, where data/knowledge is consumed and produced to realize new AI-based functionality or augment and replace static, rule-based mechanisms with learning and adaptive AI when needed." — [Ericsson white paper](https://www.ericsson.com/en/reports-and-papers/white-papers/ai-native)
- Explicit negative criterion (two consecutive segments): "Simply using AI to replace existing or add new functionalities in one, multiple, or all components, does not make an implementation AI native." It also says "An AI native implementation is managed by AI-aware control components". — [Ericsson white paper](https://www.ericsson.com/en/reports-and-papers/white-papers/ai-native)
- The paper itself says the concept lacks a clear definition and that "AI native" is typically used as a prefix to an entity (system, function, implementation). — [Ericsson white paper](https://www.ericsson.com/en/reports-and-papers/white-papers/ai-native)
- Four key architectural aspects: "1) intelligence everywhere; 2) a distributed data infrastructure; 3) zero- touch; 4) AIaaS." On zero-touch, humans "would still be in control by expressing requirements to the system." — [Ericsson white paper](https://www.ericsson.com/en/reports-and-papers/white-papers/ai-native)
- The paper does not discuss what happens when the AI fails and describes no fallback to rule-based logic; it only says AI replaces rule-based mechanisms "when needed", that legacy products can evolve to AI native "where this makes business sense", and that the system should implement "AI safety and AI control mechanisms". — [Ericsson white paper](https://www.ericsson.com/en/reports-and-papers/white-papers/ai-native)
- Dating evidence: the white paper cites ITU-R Report M.2516-0 (11/2022), and it is itself cited as reference [42] of the O-RAN nGRG report released 2023.09, so it was published between November 2022 and September 2023. — [Ericsson white paper](https://www.ericsson.com/en/reports-and-papers/white-papers/ai-native); [O-RAN nGRG RR-2023-03](https://mediastorage.o-ran.org/ngrg-rr/nGRG-RR-2023-03-Research-Report-on-Native-and-Cross-domain-AI-v1_1.pdf)

**Software-engineering literature on AI-native applications**
- Cao, Li, Fan, Li (Nanjing University) and Zhong (Nanjing Univ. of Science and Technology), arXiv 2509.13144; v1 (16 Sep 2025) titled "Towards the Next Generation of Software: Insights from Grey Literature on AI-Native Applications", v2 (21 Jul 2026) titled "A Grey Literature Review of AI-Native Applications". **[preprint]**; the header names the journal Information and Software Technology but publication there was not confirmed (no Crossref record found). — [arXiv 2509.13144](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.13144)
- Abstract: AI-native applications "still lack a unified engineering definition and architectural blueprint". Result: "AI-native applications are distinguished by two core pillars: the central role of AI as the system's intelligence paradigm and their inherently probabilistic, non-deterministic nature." Critical quality attributes "include reliability, usability, performance efficiency, and AI-specific observability"; these systems "emphasize response quality, cost-effectiveness, and outcome predictability". — [arXiv 2509.13144](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.13144)
- Body (v1 HTML): AI-native applications are "software systems fundamentally reimagined and architected around AI" (§2.2); "AI serves as the core pillar of its architecture and value proposition" (§4.1). Method: grey literature from Google, Bing and GitHub, 1 Dec 2022 to 1 May 2025; 106 sources in the abstract (the fetch noted that §3.3 says 112, an internal inconsistency). — [arXiv 2509.13144v1 HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.13144v1)
- The same review quotes Sapphire Ventures (AI-native applications "typically involve some degree of proprietary AI technology, such as model fine-tuning or orchestration") and paraphrases Ericsson. — [arXiv 2509.13144v1 HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.13144v1)

**AI-native games (closest domain to JobQuest's visual-novel format)**
- Xu, Meng, Xu, Verbrugge, Lucas, Zhao, "AI Native Games: A Survey and Roadmap", arXiv 2607.00527 (v1 1 Jul 2026, v2 3 Jul 2026). **[preprint]**, venue not stated. — [arXiv 2607.00527](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.00527)
- Definition (abstract, read verbatim via the arXiv API): "This paper defines AI-native games by whether runtime generative AI is constitutive of the core loop: if the AI component were removed or trivially replaced, the central form of play would collapse or become fundamentally different. This counterfactual criterion separates AI-native games from AI-augmented games and adjacent boundary artifacts." — [arXiv 2607.00527](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.00527)
- Also from the abstract: "generation alone does not make a game AI-native, nor does it guarantee playability"; "AI-native design depends on mechanical invariants: goals, rules, state, feedback, pacing, and player agency that make open-ended AI outputs interpretable and consequential." The survey analysed 53 public AI-native games and prototypes. — [arXiv 2607.00527](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.00527)

**A stricter, conflicting definition (systems research)**
- Cheng Tan, "Defining AI-Native Systems: Autonomy as Revision Authority", arXiv 2607.21659 (22 Jul 2026). **[preprint]**. Abstract: "'AI-native' remains a marketing term with no precise technical definition." The paper defines "a system as AI-native when an AI autonomously rewrites the system's own implementations", and "The definition further requires an escalation detector, a verification procedure, and a verified fallback, while leaving purpose and correctness human-owned." — [arXiv 2607.21659](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.21659)

**A different sense of the word: AI-native software engineering (the process, not the product)**
- Hassan, Oliva, Lin, Chen, Jiang, "Towards AI-Native Software Engineering (SE 3.0): A Vision and a Challenge Roadmap", arXiv 2410.06107 (v1 8 Oct 2024, v2 9 Jan 2026). **[preprint]**; journal venue not checked. It uses "AI-native" for a development process ("an AI-native approach characterized by intent-centric, conversation-oriented development between human developers and AI teammates") and contrasts it with "AI-assisted software engineering (SE 2.0)". — [arXiv 2410.06107](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.06107)

### Inferences
- **Shared criteria across the opened sources** (each is traceable to at least two of them):
  1. *Intrinsic, not add-on.* AI is part of the core function and architecture (Ericsson "natural part of the functionality"; FG-AINN "not merely an add-on feature"; Cao et al. "core pillar of its architecture and value proposition"; O-RAN "embedding ... into functionalities"; Xu et al. "constitutive of the core loop").
  2. *Counterfactual dependence.* Remove or trivially replace the AI and the central function collapses or changes fundamentally (stated outright only by Xu et al.; implied by Ericsson's negative criterion and by Cao et al.'s contrast with AI-assisted applications).
  3. *Whole lifecycle.* AI is present in design, deployment, operation and maintenance (Ericsson; FG-AINN characteristic (ii), unverified).
  4. *Data and knowledge loop.* Data/knowledge is "consumed and produced" by the system (Ericsson); a distributed data infrastructure with drift detection and monitoring (Ericsson; O-RAN §1.2 lifecycle management).
  5. *AI-aware management and trust.* Model lifecycle management, monitoring of model behaviour over time, "intrinsic trustworthy AI capabilities" (Ericsson); reliability and AI-specific observability as quality attributes (Cao et al.); escalation detector, verification procedure and verified fallback (Tan).
  6. *Deterministic scaffolding around a probabilistic core.* Cao et al.'s second pillar (non-determinism) and Xu et al.'s "mechanical invariants" both say an AI-native product still needs fixed rules, state and feedback around the model.
- **Testable criteria the report can derive for JobQuest** (inference, to be stated as the team's operationalisation, not as a published standard):
  - *T1, ablation test (criterion 2).* With all model calls disabled, enumerate which use cases still complete. The claim "AI-native" holds only if the core loop (free-text answer → fitted follow-up → per-competency score → unlock) cannot be delivered, or is fundamentally different. The current no-AI prototype (hand-written scenarios, keyword matching) is the natural ablation baseline.
  - *T2, lifecycle coverage (criterion 3).* Show an AI component in each lifecycle stage: content design (AI-1, AI-2), verification before release (AI-3), operation (AI-4, AI-5), maintenance (re-scoring benchmark, drift checks). A table of stage × component is the evidence.
  - *T3, data loop (criterion 4).* Show that the system produces data it later consumes: seeds, expert keys, flagged disagreements and expert adjudications feed the benchmark and prompt revisions.
  - *T4, AI-aware control (criterion 5).* Ericsson's minimum bar (maturity level 1) is reported by the page as a defined architecture for AI facilities, mechanisms to deploy and manage models, ingestion of the needed data, and the ability to monitor model behaviour over time. DC-02 (replaceable provider), versioned prompts/models and judge-agreement monitoring map onto this.
  - *T5, mechanical invariants (criterion 6).* Engine-controlled canon order, verbatim canon text and deterministic run-time checks are exactly the "goals, rules, state, feedback, pacing" that Xu et al. say AI-native design depends on. This lets the report argue that tight engine control is consistent with, not contrary to, being AI-native.
- **The definitions conflict, and the report should say so.** Under Tan's definition (AI rewrites the system's own implementation) JobQuest is *not* AI-native. Under the telecom definitions the object is a network, so they transfer only by analogy. The report should name which definition it adopts (the counterfactual/core-loop criterion plus Ericsson's lifecycle clause is the defensible combination) and note that both anchors are a vendor white paper and preprints, not a standard.
- "AI-native software engineering" (Hassan et al.) is about how software is built, not what the product does; citing it to justify a product claim would be a category error.

### Gaps
- **No formal definition from ITU-R, 3GPP or ETSI was found.** ITU-R M.2160-0 uses "AI-native" without defining it. No 3GPP TS/TR defining the term was opened or found by search. ETSI GR ENI 004 (ENI terminology; a V4.1.1 dated 2026-06 appears in search results) was not opened, so whether it defines the term is unknown.
- The FG-AINN "three delimiting characteristics" and its study FG-AINN-O-08 could not be opened (connection reset); the quoted wording is from a search snippet only.
- Ericsson's maturity-model matrix is an image on the page; the per-level, per-dimension text could not be read. Only the structure (levels 0–5, independent rows; dimensions including architecture, collaboration, data ingestion/storage/processing, model LCM, security, self-*) and the level-1 minimum were recovered.
- The Ericsson publication date is not shown on the page; only the Nov 2022–Sep 2023 bracket can be supported.
- No peer-reviewed software-engineering paper (ICSE, FSE, IEEE Software, TSE, TOSEM) defining an "AI-native application" was found. The closest sources are preprints. "AI-first" and "LLM-native" produced no reputable definition at all.
- Gartner's article "What 'AI-Native' Means for Enterprise Organizations" returned HTTP 403 and was not read.

## 2. Is there a recognised contrast between "AI-native", "AI-enabled / AI-augmented" and "AI-assisted", or a published maturity model?

### Takeaway
Contrasts exist but they are local to each source and do not agree on vocabulary or ordering; no peer-reviewed taxonomy covering all three terms was found. The only published maturity model actually opened is Ericsson's (vendor), and the cleanest two-way contrast is AI-native versus AI-augmented in the games survey and AI-native versus AI-assisted in the grey-literature review.

### Cited Findings
- Ericsson maturity model: "The model consists of a matrix of five levels, with an additional level of zero, signifying not being AI native"; "The rows in the model are independent"; to count as AI native an implementation "should reach level 1 on at least the architecture, data ingestion, storage and processing, model lifecycle management (LCM) and security, and self-* dimensions." The page describes levels as a measuring tool rather than a goal (level 5 is not needed everywhere). **[vendor]** — [Ericsson white paper](https://www.ericsson.com/en/reports-and-papers/white-papers/ai-native)
- Ericsson distinguishes approaches that are *not* by themselves AI native: replacing or augmenting existing functions with AI, adding a new AI-based component, and "adding an AI-based component that acts as a control for legacy component(s)". — [Ericsson white paper](https://www.ericsson.com/en/reports-and-papers/white-papers/ai-native)
- Cao et al. contrast AI-native with AI-assisted: the AI-assisted paradigm "incorporates AI technologies as auxiliary features into existing applications" (Introduction); "AI-assisted applications simply add AI features to a deterministic workflow" (§5.1.1). The paper does not define AI-enabled, AI-augmented or AI-powered. **[preprint]** — [arXiv 2509.13144v1 HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.13144v1)
- Xu et al. contrast AI-native with AI-augmented games using the counterfactual criterion ("separates AI-native games from AI-augmented games and adjacent boundary artifacts"). **[preprint]** — [arXiv 2607.00527](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.00527)
- O-RAN nGRG uses a two-step vocabulary of "AI-augmented" then "AI-infused" under the heading "Native AI". **[SDO report]** — [O-RAN nGRG RR-2023-03](https://mediastorage.o-ran.org/ngrg-rr/nGRG-RR-2023-03-Research-Report-on-Native-and-Cross-domain-AI-v1_1.pdf)
- Tan orders systems by "revision authority" on a ladder "self-tuning, self-rewriting, self-architecting"; this is a maturity ladder for autonomy, not for the three marketing terms. **[preprint]** — [arXiv 2607.21659](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.21659)
- Hassan et al. label "AI-assisted software engineering" as SE 2.0 and "AI-native" as SE 3.0, again for the development process. **[preprint]** — [arXiv 2410.06107](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.06107)
- Weber, "Large Language Models as Software Components: A Taxonomy for LLM-Integrated Applications", arXiv 2406.10300 (13 Jun 2024), proposes thirteen dimensions for characterising each "LLM component" of an application. Only the abstract was read; it does not use the AI-native/AI-assisted contrast. **[preprint]** — [arXiv 2406.10300](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.10300)

### Inferences
- A defensible three-level framing for the report, assembled from the sources above and to be presented as the team's synthesis: **AI-assisted/AI-augmented** = AI is an auxiliary feature on a deterministic workflow, and removing it leaves the core function intact (Cao et al.; Xu et al.); **AI-native** = AI is constitutive of the core function and present across the lifecycle (Xu et al.; Ericsson). "AI-enabled" has no stable meaning in the opened sources and is best avoided or defined locally.
- Weber's per-component view supports analysing JobQuest component by component (AI-1 to AI-5) instead of labelling the whole system once; some components are constitutive at run time (AI-4, AI-5) and some are authoring aids (AI-1, AI-2, AI-3).
- Ericsson's rule that nativeness needs level 1 on several *independent* dimensions is a useful precedent for a checklist-style claim ("meets the criterion on dimensions X, Y, Z") in place of a yes/no label.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed taxonomy or maturity model that orders AI-assisted, AI-enabled/augmented and AI-native for software products was found.
- UNVERIFIED: a Gartner enterprise scorecard is reported in search snippets to use stages such as "Stage 0 (Not AI-enabled)" and "Stage 1 (AI-assisted)" with "AI-augmented" later; the page returned HTTP 403. It concerns organisations, not products.
- Blog and consultancy maturity models (several appeared in search) were not opened and are not reputable enough to cite.

## 4. Published research prototypes using LLMs for career exploration or job simulation

### Takeaway

## 5. Graceful degradation and resilience patterns for applications that depend on external LLM APIs; empirical availability data

### Takeaway
Official architecture guidance from AWS, Google Cloud and Microsoft agrees on the pattern set (abstraction layer or gateway, timeouts, retries with backoff, circuit breakers, redundancy and failover, fallbacks such as simpler models and cached data, queueing of deferrable work) and AWS adds a caution that fallback paths must be simpler, tested, and generally avoided when rarely exercised. The one peer-reviewed measurement study shows provider incidents are routine (mean time between reported incidents about 5 days for both the OpenAI and Anthropic APIs, most over within hours) and uncorrelated across providers, which is the empirical basis for multi-provider redundancy.

### Inferences
- **Resolving the advisor's "AI-native, yet still runs when AI is down" question.** The two demands are compatible if "runs" is defined the way AWS REL05-BP01 defines graceful degradation: the system keeps a *predictable and recoverable state* and its most critical function, not full functionality. Under the counterfactual criterion (Section 1) a system whose core loop works unchanged without AI is by definition *not* AI-native. So the honest claim is: without the models JobQuest cannot deliver its core value (that is what makes it AI-native), but it fails safely, loses no committed progress (NFR-04), keeps authored content available, and completes deferred work when the provider returns. Tan's definition and the ETSI ZSM "ML Fallback Management" service show that a designed fallback is treated as part of AI-native system design, not as evidence against it.
- **A per-component design is justified because the five components have different dependency types** (AWS: "transform applicable hard dependencies into soft dependencies"; "as much a business discussion as a technical one"). Suggested classification, for the report to argue:
  - *AI-1, AI-2, AI-3 (authoring and pre-release testing, offline, staff-facing).* Soft dependency for players: an outage delays content production but touches no live session. Pattern: persist partial work, retry with backoff, resume later. No fallback model needed.
  - *AI-4 (run-time narration).* Partly soft: canon events are already shown verbatim from the seed and the engine controls order, so the session can continue with authored canon text and seed-provided hints while connecting narration and fitted follow-ups are unavailable. This matches Google's "fallbacks like simpler models and cached data" and AWS's "alternate data". Pre-generated or cached connectors are an option; note the staleness and fit risk (Azure) and that a cached follow-up is not fitted to the player's answer, so the degraded experience is closer to the non-AI prototype.
  - *AI-5 (scoring).* Hard dependency for the product's value, but it is *deferrable*: accept and durably store the answer, queue scoring, and complete asynchronously (AWS dead-letter queue and buffered writes; Azure queue-based load leveling; Google message queues). The three-judge panel from three model families is already redundancy across uncorrelated providers (Chu et al.), so a 2-of-3 quorum rule is a natural degraded mode. Whether a two-judge median meets NFR-14 is an empirical question the Stage A benchmark can answer by scoring with each pair.
- **Do not fall back to keyword matching silently.** AWS's caution (fallback paths must be simpler, tested, and are "generally" to be avoided) plus RP-2 argue against substituting an unvalidated scorer during an outage: a score produced by a different, unvalidated method would enter the competency record. Deferring the score is the safer degraded mode than replacing the scorer.
- **Sizing the risk.** With a mean time between reported incidents of about 5 days per provider API and a median recovery of roughly 1 hour (data to August 2024; "incident" includes partial degradation, not only full outage), a 10–15 minute session will sometimes overlap an incident; with three independent judge providers, the chance that at least one is degraded at any time is higher than for a single provider, which is why a quorum rule matters more than it first appears. This is reasoning from the published means, not a computed probability.
- **Failures peak 8:00–16:00** (time zone as reported in the paper; check before quoting), which may or may not coincide with Vietnamese evening usage. Worth one sentence, not a design driver.
- **DC-02 and the abstraction layer.** All three cloud frameworks recommend an abstraction layer or gateway between application and model; DC-02 (replaceable provider) is the same idea. Azure's warning applies: the abstraction can itself become a single point of failure and sees raw user text, which interacts with DC-03 (de-identification before the call, and any failover to another provider or region is a different cross-border transfer that must be covered in advance).
- **Timeout budgets.** NFR-01 (5 s p95 generation) and NFR-02 (8 s p95 scoring) need explicit per-call timeouts shorter than those limits for the fallback to engage in time; with three parallel judges the p95 of the *slowest* judge governs unless the quorum rule returns after two. That is a design consequence, to be measured in the prototype.

### Gaps
- **No peer-reviewed measurement of LLM API latency variance or request-level error rates was found.** Chu et al. measure incidents from status pages, not latency or per-request errors. Search returned only vendor blogs and a conference poster (not opened). ## 6. How do users react when an AI feature is unavailable or delayed, and to disclosure of degraded mode?

### Takeaway
No study was found that measures user reaction to an AI feature being unavailable or to an explicit "degraded mode" notice. The nearest evidence concerns response delay in chatbots (explaining a delay can raise perceived transparency and trust, but not in every condition; faster is not always rated better) and design guidance (peer-reviewed guidelines and Google's guidebook) that says to state limits, explain failures specifically and give users a way forward.

### Cited Findings
- Zhang, Tsiakas, Schneegass, "Explaining the Wait: How Justifying Chatbot Response Delays Impact User Trust", CUI '24 (6th ACM Conference on Conversational User Interfaces, Luxembourg, 8–10 July 2024), Article 27, DOI 10.1145/3640794.3665550. **[peer-reviewed]**. Pilot N = 10; main between-subjects online study N = 194. Textual justifications of the delay increased perceived transparency and trust in the instant-response condition and had no effect in the dynamic-delay condition. The authors attribute mixed prior findings to "the absence of contextual cues that allow for interpretation of the delay." (Publication page read, not the full paper; no effect sizes available.) — [TU Delft research portal](https://research.tudelft.nl/en/publications/explaining-the-wait-how-justifying-chatbot-response-delays-impact/)
- Gnewuch, Morana, Adam, Maedche, "Faster Is Not Always Better: Understanding the Effect of Dynamic Response Delays in Human-Chatbot Interaction", 26th European Conference on Information Systems (ECIS 2018), Portsmouth. **[peer-reviewed]**. Online experiment in a customer-service setting: dynamically delayed responses (scaled to message complexity) increased perceived humanness, social presence and satisfaction compared with near-instant responses; the authors argue response time "represents a social cue that triggers social responses." Sample size is not given on the repository page. — [KITopen record](https://publikationen.bibliothek.kit.edu/1000089970)
- Amershi et al., "Guidelines for Human-AI Interaction", CHI 2019, DOI 10.1145/3290605.3300233 (CHI 2019 Honorable Mention). **[peer-reviewed]**. Eighteen guidelines; those relevant here, as titled in Microsoft's HAX library: G1 "Make clear what the system can do", G2 "Make clear how well the system can do what it can do", G9 "Support efficient correction", G10 "Scope services when in doubt", G11 "Make clear why the system did what it did", G18 "Notify users about changes". The library describes G10 as using disambiguation or gracefully degrading services when uncertain about the user's goals. — [Microsoft Research publication page](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/guidelines-for-human-ai-interaction/); [HAX design library](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/haxtoolkit/library/)
- Google PAIR, People + AI Guidebook, chapter "Errors + Graceful Failure" (no date shown). **[vendor design guidance]**. "What the user considers an error is deeply connected to their expectations of the AI system."; "Focusing on what your users can do after the system fails empowers them while maintaining the usefulness of your product."; "Explain why a certain result couldn’t be given and provide alternative paths forward."; "When an AI system fails, often the easiest path forward is to let the user take over."; "Messages about user-perceived errors should inform the user as specifically as possible about the system’s limitations." — [PAIR Guidebook, Errors + Graceful Failure](https://pair.withgoogle.com/chapter/errors-failing/)

### Inferences
- The evidence supports *telling the player what is happening and what happens next* over failing silently: explanations help when users otherwise lack cues to interpret system behaviour (Zhang et al.), and both guideline sets call for specific statements of limits and a path forward. It does not establish how much trust is gained or lost; the effect in Zhang et al. depended on the condition.
- Applied to JobQuest's degraded modes (design suggestion, not a tested result): when scoring is deferred, show that the answer is saved and that feedback will arrive later (PAIR "alternative paths forward"; HAX G1/G2); when narration falls back to authored text, the player can simply continue ("let the user take over"); when the panel returns a quorum score, record that fact for expert review even if it is not shown to the player.
- Delay studies caution against assuming that faster is always better, but they concern delays of seconds in chat, with social-presence framing. They say nothing about an 8-second scoring wait in a game or a scoring result that arrives minutes later. NFR-01 and NFR-02 thresholds are therefore engineering targets, not values derived from user research.
- HAX G10's "gracefully degrade" refers to uncertainty about the user's goal, not to provider outage. It should not be cited as evidence about outage handling.

### Gaps
- **No study of user reaction to AI feature unavailability, and none of disclosing degraded mode, was found.** News coverage of chatbot outages exists but is anecdotal and was not opened.
- Leads not opened (search snippets only), about a different event type: studies using Italy's April 2023 ChatGPT ban as a natural experiment on developer output (Kreitmeir and Raschky, arXiv 2403.01964; a synthetic difference-in-differences study, arXiv 2406.11046), with mixed results. These concern productivity after loss of access, not user experience of a degraded product.
- UNVERIFIED lead: a CHI 2026 study reported by a secondary site as finding that very fast responses were rated less thoughtful than slower ones; not located on the ACM site.
- No evidence for Vietnamese users, for students, or for game-like learning contexts. The measurement that would close the gap: in the Stage B pilot, instrument the deferred-scoring path and compare completion and satisfaction between sessions with immediate and deferred feedback, with the degraded state disclosed.
- The full texts of Zhang et al. and Gnewuch et al. were not read (publication-record pages only), so designs and statistics should be checked in the papers before citing numbers.
