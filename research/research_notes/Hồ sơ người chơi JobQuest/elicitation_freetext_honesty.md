## 1. Socially desirable responding and faking when nothing is at stake

### Takeaway
The measured faking problem is a high-stakes problem: meta-analytic inflation is defined as the gap between applicants (or instructed fakers) and a low-stakes baseline, and forced-choice shrinks that gap to almost nothing. For a low-stakes, private profile the deliberate-faking argument for forced-choice is weak; what remains is unconscious self-enhancement and limited self-knowledge, which no format in the sources reviewed removes.

### Cited Findings
- Forced-choice (FC) personality scales show an overall score inflation of d = 0.06 between high-stakes and low-stakes administrations (95% CI 0.02 to 0.09), which the authors call smaller than a small effect and much smaller than earlier estimates for single-statement scales. — [Cao, M., & Drasgow, F. (2019). Does forcing reduce faking? A meta-analytic review of forced-choice personality measures in high-stakes situations. *Journal of Applied Psychology, 104*(11), 1347–1368](https://doi.org/10.1037/apl0000414) **[F]** (abstract and results sections of the article PDF)
- Within FC, design matters: PICK format (choose the one statement most like me) d = 0.05; MOLE format (most and least like me) d = 0.37; normative scoring d = 0.00, ipsative d = 0.31, partially ipsative d = 0.72; blocks balanced on social desirability gave non-significant inflation (d = 0.02). Induced-faking lab designs gave larger inflation than real applicant-versus-incumbent designs. — [Cao & Drasgow 2019](https://doi.org/10.1037/apl0000414) **[F]**
- The single-statement (Likert) benchmark: across 33 studies, job applicants scored higher than non-applicants on conscientiousness (d = .45), emotional stability (d = .44), openness (d = .13) and extraversion (d = .11); distortion concentrated on job-relevant traits. — [Birkeland, S. A., Manson, T. M., Kisamore, J. L., Brannick, M. T., & Smith, M. A. (2006). A meta-analytic investigation of job applicant faking on personality measures. *International Journal of Selection and Assessment, 14*(4), 317–335](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1468-2389.2006.00354.x) **[A]**
- Putting a questionnaire on a screen without a proctor does not by itself make people more candid: three meta-analyses comparing computerised with paper administration (social desirability scales k = 30; Big Five k = 66; psychopathology k = 96) found a near-zero overall effect (Δ = 0.01), with no difference between proctored and unproctored settings. — [Gnambs, T., & Kaspar, K. (2017). Socially desirable responding in web-based questionnaires: A meta-analytic review of the candor hypothesis. *Assessment, 24*(6), 746–762](https://doi.org/10.1177/1073191115624547) **[A]**
- Socially desirable responding has two components, self-deception and impression management; the 1984 paper compared desirability scales under anonymous and public conditions. — [Paulhus, D. L. (1984). Two-component models of socially desirable responding. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 46*(3), 598–609](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.46.3.598) **[S]** (search summary of a library record; publisher page did not load, so the direction and size of the anonymous-versus-public difference were not read)
- Experimenter demand is weaker than often assumed in online surveys: telling over 12,000 participants the researcher's hypothesis, or even paying them to confirm it, did not consistently change treatment effects in five replicated survey experiments. — [Mummolo, J., & Peterson, E. (2019). Demand effects in survey experiments: An empirical assessment. *American Political Science Review, 113*(2), 517–529](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0003055418000837) **[A]**

### Inferences
- All inflation figures above are high-stakes minus low-stakes. They say how much stakes add, not how much distortion remains at the low-stakes baseline. JobQuest sits at the baseline (private, no employer, no score), so deliberate impression management toward an audience should be small; this is an inference, not a measured result.
- The teammate draft's two-sided items are a PICK-style forced choice with a "very / somewhat like me" strength. Its documented benefit (faking resistance) is mostly not needed here. Its remaining benefits are speed on a phone and a fallback when text is thin. Fourteen items are not justified by the faking literature.
- If two-sided items are kept, the one design rule with evidence behind it is to balance the two sides on desirability (already an aim in the draft).
- Situational or behavioural questions and indirect questions were named in the brief; no source comparing them with direct trait questions under low stakes was opened in this session (see Gaps).

### Gaps
- No source read gives the absolute size of self-enhancement in anonymous, low-stakes self-description. Paulhus (1984) could only be confirmed at secondary level.
- No evidence found for Vietnamese respondents or for ages 13–17 on any point in this section. The faking meta-analyses are adult employment samples.
- No source opened on situational-judgment or indirect questioning as low-stakes honesty aids.

## 2. Open-ended questions on web and mobile: quality, non-response, drop-out, typing burden

### Takeaway
Open-ended questions cost more non-response than closed ones, answers get shorter on phones and later in a questionnaire, and motivation matters more than layout. A short framing sentence that says why the answer matters and how much to write is the best-supported cheap intervention; a larger box helps mainly the less motivated.

### Cited Findings
- In a split-ballot web experiment, the open-ended version of a question produced more missing data and more inadequate answers than closed versions, though a far wider range of answers; the authors advise wording open questions more explicitly in self-administered web surveys. — [Reja, U., Manfreda, K. L., Hlebec, V., & Vehovar, V. (2003). Open-ended vs. close-ended questions in web questionnaires. *Developments in Applied Statistics (Metodološki zvezki), 19*, 159–177](https://repozitorij.uni-lj.si/IzpisGradiva.php?id=6266) **[S]** (search summary of the repository abstract)
- Three web surveys of undergraduates: a larger answer box had little effect on early responders but substantially improved response quality among late responders; any introduction that made quality and length salient (clarifying or motivating) improved responses for both groups. Quality was measured by amount and type of information, response time and item non-response. — [Smyth, J. D., Dillman, D. A., Christian, L. M., & McBride, M. (2009). Open-ended questions in web surveys: Can increasing the size of answer boxes and providing extra verbal instructions improve response quality? *Public Opinion Quarterly, 73*(2), 325–337](https://doi.org/10.1093/poq/nfp029) **[A]** (full-text PDF returned HTTP 403)
- Topic interest drives whether people answer an open question and how well. An interactive follow-up probe improved quality mainly for those already very interested; item non-response was high for the initial question and higher still for the probe. The authors advise probing only a few key questions. — [Holland, J. L., & Christian, L. M. (2009). The influence of topic interest and interactive probing on responses to open-ended questions in web surveys. *Social Science Computer Review, 27*(2), 196–212](https://doi.org/10.1177/0894439308327481) **[A]**
- A static open-ended probe has a measurable price: in a German salary-site survey (N = 22,306), each meaningful comment gained cost about 0.1 break-offs and about 3.7 item-missings on the closed question when the probe was embedded, and 0.2 break-offs when it was on a following page. Smartphone users were more likely to give a meaningful (though shorter) comment, and smartphone use amplified the probe's negative effect on break-off and item non-response. — [Luebker, M. (2021). How much is a box? The hidden cost of adding an open-ended probe to an online survey. *Methods, Data, Analyses, 15*(1), 36 ff.](https://doi.org/10.12758/mda.2020.09) **[S]** (findings from a search summary of the GESIS/SSOAR abstract, since the repository blocked direct access; title and DOI confirmed in OpenAlex)
- Mobile web versus PC in a Russian online panel experiment: mobile gave a lower completion rate and shorter open answers, with similar levels of socially undesirable and non-substantive responses. — [Mavletova, A. (2013). Data quality in PC and mobile web surveys. *Social Science Computer Review, 31*(6), 725–743](https://doi.org/10.1177/0894439313485201) **[A]**
- A randomised crossover in a probability panel (n = 1,390) found smartphone respondents at least as conscientious and as willing to disclose sensitive information as on PC, provided the question formats were easy on a small touchscreen; sliders and date wheels were the problem formats. — [Antoun, C., Couper, M. P., & Conrad, F. G. (2017). Effects of mobile versus PC web on survey response quality: A crossover experiment in a probability web panel. *Public Opinion Quarterly, 81*(S1), 280–306](https://doi.org/10.1093/poq/nfw088) **[A]**
- Position matters: answers to questions placed later in a web questionnaire were faster, shorter (for open-ended items) and more uniform; the longer the announced length (10, 20, 30 minutes), the fewer people started and finished. — [Galesic, M., & Bosnjak, M. (2009). Effects of questionnaire length on participation and indicators of response quality in a web survey. *Public Opinion Quarterly, 73*(2), 349–360](https://doi.org/10.1093/poq/nfp031) **[A]**
- Typing speed on phones: 37,370 volunteers averaged 36.2 words per minute in a transcription task with 2.3% uncorrected errors; ages 10–19 were the fastest group; over 74% typed with two thumbs, which was faster; autocorrect use correlated with faster entry, word-prediction use with slower entry. — [Palin, K., Feit, A. M., Kim, S., Kristensson, P. O., & Oulasvirta, A. (2019). How do people type on mobile devices? Observations from a study with 37,000 volunteers. *MobileHCI '19*](https://userinterfaces.aalto.fi/typing37k) **[S]** (authors' project page, not the paper)
- Composing is much slower than transcribing: in an AI-led chat interview, typed answers came at a mean of 21.1 words per minute (median 18.0), against 52.5 for voice. — [Wuttke, A., Lang, M. M., Klamm, C., Würschinger, Q., & Kreuter, F. (2026). AI Conversational Interviewing: Scaling up semi-structured and in-depth interviews. arXiv:2606.20064](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.20064) **[F, partial]** **preprint** (adult German online-panel sample; first 100k characters of the HTML read)
- Early adolescents can answer open-ended self-reflective questions on a computer: among 261 US fifth graders, computer-administered responses were longer and lexically richer than paper ones. — [Love, A. M. A., Butz, A. R., Usher, E. L., & Waiters, B. L. (2018). Open-ended responses from early adolescents: Method matters. *Journal of Adolescence, 67*, 31–34](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.adolescence.2018.05.007) **[S]** (findings from a search summary of the university record; citation confirmed in OpenAlex)
- For children and adolescents as survey respondents, data quality (item non-response, internal consistency) improves with years of education, and poor reading skill lowers it. — [Borgers, N., de Leeuw, E., & Hox, J. (2000). Children as respondents in survey research: Cognitive development and response quality. *Bulletin de Méthodologie Sociologique, 66*, 60–75](https://utrecht-main-test.atmire.com/items/2ab0afb2-0329-4928-b37e-5dcdfa1345ad) **[S]** (search summary; says nothing specific about open-ended items)

### Inferences
- Time budget (own arithmetic, not a sourced figure): at roughly 20 composed words per minute, three typed answers of about 25 words each take close to four minutes. Add reading time and the draft's 14 two-sided items and the total exceeds five minutes. A five-minute phone flow has room for about three short typed answers plus a handful of taps, not 14 + 3.
- Because later answers are shorter and motivation dominates, the typed question that matters most should come early (after one easy tap), and the framing line should say what the text is used for.
- The "larger box" result suggests the input should look like it wants one to three sentences: a multi-line box and a soft "1–3 câu là đủ" hint do more than a 400-character counter.
- Smartphone users do write, just less. Short answers are the expected case, so the reading step has to work with one or two sentences per answer or abstain.

### Gaps
- No evidence found on open-ended self-description by 13–17-year-olds on phones, on Vietnamese typing with diacritics (Telex/VNI), or on drop-out caused by a required typed answer in a game onboarding. The draft's rule that the three narrative answers are mandatory once started has no support or refutation in what was read.
- The mobile-typing speed by age group was seen only on a project page and in news coverage, not in the paper.
- Voice input as a lower-burden alternative has one preprint behind it (Wuttke et al. 2026: about twice the words, but drop-out concentrated in the forced-voice arm and more technical problems); not explored further.

## 3. Prompting cues: what helps people start, and what plants the answer

### Takeaway
Cues work by directing attention: examples make people consider the items shown and under-report what is not shown, and unusual examples steer hardest. A cue should therefore name the dimensions to talk about (what, with whom, what kept you there) and not offer sample content; if examples are shown, they should be optional, paired and contrasting.

### Cited Findings
- Examples in a question change the answer: frequent-item examples raised reported consumption compared with infrequent-item examples; atypical examples had more impact than typical ones (people think of typical cases anyway); in open-ended versions people omitted items, except the items they had been given as examples. The authors conclude examples determine which and how many category members respondents consider. — [Tourangeau, R., Conrad, F. G., Couper, M. P., & Ye, C. (2014). The effects of providing examples in survey questions. *Public Opinion Quarterly, 78*(1), 100–125](https://doi.org/10.1093/poq/nft083) **[A]** (abstract and author list from the OpenAlex record; the study is about factual recall of food consumption)
- Clarifying and motivating introductions in the question stem improved open-ended response quality for all respondents. — [Smyth et al. 2009](https://doi.org/10.1093/poq/nfp029) **[A]**
- "Why" follow-ups late in a 20-minute chatbot survey drew more relevant, specific and clear answers than most "what" questions asked mid-survey (authors' observation, not a controlled comparison). — [Xiao, Z., Zhou, M. X., Liao, Q. V., Mark, G., Chi, C., Chen, W., & Yang, H. (2020). Tell me about yourself: Using an AI-powered chatbot to conduct conversational surveys with open-ended questions. *ACM TOCHI, 27*(3), Article 15](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.10700) **[F]** (arXiv version of the TOCHI article, DOI 10.1145/3381804)
- Respondents show limited ability to bend answers toward what a researcher wants even when told the hypothesis. — [Mummolo & Peterson 2019](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0003055418000837) **[A]**

### Inferences
- The Tourangeau result is about factual recall of food items, not self-description. Transferring it: an example answer such as "I coded alone until 2 a.m." makes solitary deep work the considered category. The risk is highest for vivid, unusual examples, which is exactly what copywriters reach for.
- Practical phrasing rules that follow from the above (design inferences, not tested rules): (a) sub-questions ask about dimensions and name both poles symmetrically ("một mình hay với ai"); (b) no evaluative adjectives in the cue; (c) ask for one recent concrete episode, not a self-label; (d) example answers hidden behind a "Cần gợi ý?" tap, always shown as a contrasting pair in random order; (e) log whether the hint was opened; (f) when reading the text, reject evidence quotes that only repeat the wording of the cue or the example.
- Sentence stems and "choose a card, then say why" are plausible low-burden starters, but no experimental source on either was opened.

### Gaps
- No experimental evidence read on sentence stems or sentence completion as web or mobile survey devices, nor on "choose then explain" formats. The psychology sentence-completion tests (Rotter; Loevinger) were not opened.
- A creativity-research finding that examples cause conformity to the examples' features (Smith, Ward & Schumacher, 1993, *Memory & Cognition*, DOI 10.3758/BF03202751) is a relevant lead, but neither abstract nor text could be opened; do not cite it without reading.
- Classic leading-question and priming work was not opened in this session.

## 4. AI conversational interviewers and follow-up probes

### Takeaway
AI follow-up probes reliably produce longer and more specific answers, and the cost is real: roughly double the time, small rises in early drop-out, and a worse experience on phones. The peer-reviewed experiment closest to JobQuest's situation capped probing at one per question and recommends using probes sparingly, especially on mobile.

### Cited Findings
- Field study with adult US gamers (282 chatbot, 300 Qualtrics completes): chatbot answers were more informative (283.33 vs 203.53 bits), more relevant, and longer (response length 90.11 vs 63.98 words, F(1,576) = 57.92), with fewer gibberish surveys (2.48% vs 9.00%). Participants spent 24.38 vs 17.90 minutes. The panel's completion rate was 54% vs 24.2%, while the response rate was lower for the chatbot (84.6% vs 91.2%). — [Xiao et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.10700) **[F]**
- Dose in that study: over about 19 open-ended questions, each participant was prompted for more information 2.9 times on average and complied 71% of the time; 61% received at least one follow-up question and answered it 89% of the time. 33.7% of chatbot participants were on mobile, with no significant association with quality or engagement. — [Xiao et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.10700) **[F]**
- Failure modes reported there: a 30% abandon rate in the first days when the chat screen appeared unannounced (fixed by telling people in advance they would chat with an AI); users noticing the bot repeating a question; gibberish detection misfiring on legitimate proper nouns; a likely novelty effect; effects not attributable to any single chatbot behaviour. — [Xiao et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.10700) **[F]**
- Web experiment with 1,803 completes (65% on smartphones), LLM chatbot, at most one probe per question: elaboration probes raised specificity and explanation on two of the questions and raised word counts; they also caused small significant drops in ease and satisfaction and more frustration (under 0.05 on a 0–1 scale), about 2–3 percentage points more drop-out at the first probed question (later probes added none), and doubled chat length (about 6 vs 3 minutes). Mobile respondents reported lower ease and higher frustration. Confirmation probes ("did I code that right?") left ratings unchanged but had about twice the drop-outs of control (88 vs 43). Recommendation: probe sparingly, especially for mobile users, and only where the analysis needs it. — [Barari, S., Angbazo, J., Wang, N., Christian, L., Dean, E., Slowinski, Z., & Sepulvado, B. (2026). AI-assisted conversational interviewing: Effects on data quality and respondent experience. *Survey Research Methods, 20*(2)](https://doi.org/10.18148/srm/2026.v20i2.8624) **[F]**
- An earlier NORC wave (n = 1,200, May 2024) reported that probing gains appeared mainly among desktop respondents, with no quality effect and a worse experience on mobile, and that heavy early probing increased drop-out; it advises placing probes mid-survey or later. — [Barari et al. (2024). Generative AI can enhance survey interviews. NORC brief](https://www.norc.org/research/library/generative-ai-can-enhance-survey-interviews.html) **[F]** (institutional brief, not peer reviewed; no effect sizes given)
- Qualtrics experiment (n = 1,200) with a GPT-3 chatbot generating follow-ups on a final open question: higher-quality, more detailed answers and a stated preference for follow-ups, but respondents disliked repetitive probes and not knowing how many exchanges were required. — [Callegaro, M., & Petronin, P. (2023). Combining closed-based and chatbot-based questions in surveys. Google Research](https://research.google/pubs/combining-closed-based-and-chatbot-based-questions-in-surveys/) **[A]** (publication page; venue not stated)
- AI-led qualitative interviews are reported to give thematically richer answers than other at-scale methods, with a decomposition attributing most of the gain to dynamic follow-up questions; responses predicted behaviour six months later. — [Chopra, F., & Haaland, I. (2023, revised 2026). Conducting qualitative interviews with AI. CESifo Working Paper No. 10666](https://www.ifo.de/en/cesifo/publications/2023/working-paper/conducting-qualitative-interviews-ai) **[A]** **working paper, not peer reviewed**
- Fatigue point in typed AI interviews: answer length in the chat condition declined progressively after about the tenth turn (exploratory); most respondents still preferred a standard survey for the next round, most strongly among chat completers; interviews averaged about 10 minutes. — [Wuttke et al. 2026, arXiv:2606.20064](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.20064) **[F, partial]** **preprint**
- Even static probes help mainly the already-interested and add non-response. — [Holland & Christian 2009](https://doi.org/10.1177/0894439308327481) **[A]**

### Inferences
- No study gives a fatigue threshold for a five-minute onboarding. The usable anchors are: one probe per question as a tested guardrail (Barari), a visible decline around ten typed turns in a ten-minute interview (Wuttke, exploratory), and respondents' dislike of an unknown number of exchanges (Callegaro). For three questions on a phone, at most one follow-up per question and at most two in the whole flow, announced up front and skippable, is the conservative reading.
- The first probe is where drop-out appears, so the first typed question should not be probed; a probe belongs on the second or third answer and only when the answer carries no usable evidence.
- Every probe needs a model round trip. On a slow connection the wait is itself burden; none of the studies measured latency (Barari lists it as future work). A timeout that silently skips the probe is a design safeguard without direct evidence.
- Xiao's engagement gains came from paid adult panellists in a 20-minute survey in 2018–2019, with a novelty effect the authors acknowledge. They should not be read as "a chat format makes onboarding sticky".

### Gaps
- No AI-probing study with adolescents, in Vietnamese, or in a game onboarding was found.
- Under-probing by LLM interviewers (the project's earlier note cites Wuttke et al., LaTeCH-CLfL 2025, arXiv:2410.01824) was not re-verified in this session.
- Chopra and Haaland's probe counts, interview length and sample details were not read (abstract only; sample size differs between versions according to search summaries).

## 5. Self-disclosure to machines

### Takeaway
People disclose more to a system that discloses first and that they believe no human is watching, and chat formats raise disclosure of personal information as a side effect. For JobQuest this cuts both ways: the same levers that produce honest text also pull out identifying details that are about to be sent to a foreign model.

### Cited Findings
- Reciprocity and sequence affect how likely people are to reveal intimate information to a computer; such exchanges also change later behaviour. — [Moon, Y. (2000). Intimate exchanges: Using computers to elicit self-disclosure from consumers. *Journal of Consumer Research, 26*(4), 323–339](https://doi.org/10.1086/209566) **[A]** (the abstract names the two factors but not the manipulation details)
- A chatbot that self-disclosed during small talk drew deeper participant self-disclosure than chat styles without self-disclosure, sustained over three weeks (47 participants, three groups), and raised perceived intimacy and enjoyment. — [Lee, Y.-C., Yamashita, N., Huang, Y., & Fu, W. (2020). "I hear you, I feel you": Encouraging deep self-disclosure through a chatbot. *CHI '20*](https://doi.org/10.1145/3313831.3376175) **[A]**
- Participants interviewed by a virtual human disclosed more when told it was fully automated than when told a human was operating it. — [Lucas, G. M., Gratch, J., King, A., & Morency, L.-P. (2014). It's only a computer: Virtual humans increase willingness to disclose. *Computers in Human Behavior, 37*, 94–100](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2014.04.043) **[S]** (search summary of press and institutional coverage; publisher page returned 403; a clinical-intake setting with 239 adults per that coverage)
- In the chatbot survey, 32.62% of participants disclosed personal information in their self-introduction against 15.67% in the form survey, and covered more types of information (5.16 vs 3.57). — [Xiao et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.10700) **[F]**
- Computer administration alone does not reduce socially desirable responding (Δ = 0.01). — [Gnambs & Kaspar 2017](https://doi.org/10.1177/1073191115624547) **[A]**

### Inferences
- The lever is not "it is a computer" but what the person believes about the audience and whether the system gives something first. A one-line statement that no person reads the answers, and what happens to the text, is supported in direction by Lucas et al.; a short self-disclosing opener by the guide character is supported by Moon and by Lee et al.
- A reciprocal opener is itself an example answer, so it collides with section 3. If used, it should disclose something off-dimension (for instance, the guide admitting it gets things wrong sometimes), not a work-style preference.
- "Only an AI reads this" must be true and must include the fact that text goes to an external model provider. Stating the opposite of what happens would trade honesty of users for dishonesty of the product.
- Higher disclosure means more names, schools and places in the text. A visible "đừng ghi tên người, tên trường" line plus pattern stripping before the model call is the proportionate response.

### Gaps
- Lucas et al. could only be confirmed at secondary level. No chatbot-disclosure study with minors or Vietnamese users was found.
- No source read tests anonymity wording or tone (formal vs playful) on disclosure in a low-stakes game.

## 6. Reading personality, interests or work style from short free text with LLMs

### Takeaway
Zero-shot LLM trait inference from text agrees with self-report at about r = .2 to .3 on ordinary text and up to about r = .44 when the conversation is designed to elicit trait-relevant content; repeat runs agree with each other far better than they agree with the person. A profile indicator read from three short answers is a weak, biased signal that must be presented as "what you told us", backed by quotes, and allowed to be empty.

### Cited Findings
- GPT-3.5 and GPT-4, zero-shot, on the 200 most recent Facebook status updates of 1,000 users (mean 17.1 words per update): average correlation with self-reported Big Five r = .29 (range .22 to .33); the authors note a substantial share was captured with as few as 20 updates; three runs were averaged and run-to-run agreement was r = .73 to .96 by trait; inferred scores and residuals differed by gender and age. Supervised baseline cited: Park et al. average r = .37. — [Peters, H., & Matz, S. C. (2024). Large language models can infer psychological dispositions of social media users. *PNAS Nexus, 3*(6), pgae231](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11211928/) **[F]**
- When a GPT-4 chatbot was instructed to elicit personality-relevant information, inferred Big Five correlated with self-report at mean r = .443 (range .245 to .640); with a naturalistic-interaction emphasis mean r = .218; as a default helpful assistant mean r = .117. The first two conditions were rated equally natural and engaging. — [Peters, H., Cerf, M., & Matz, S. C. (2024). Large language models can infer personality from free-form user interactions. arXiv:2405.13052](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.13052) **[A]** **preprint**
- On 555 semi-structured interviews with BFI-10 self-reports, three LLMs showed high test-retest reliability but limited validity: maximum r = .27, Cohen's κ below 0.10, predictions skewed toward moderate or high trait levels; chain-of-thought and longer context did not improve trait-level accuracy. — [Zhu, J., Jin, R., & Coifman, K. G. (2025). Can LLMs infer personality from real world conversations? arXiv:2507.14355](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.14355) **[A]** **preprint**. A companion benchmark reports all correlations below .26, including fine-tuned models, and minimal gain from chain-of-thought. — [Zhu, J., Maharjan, J., Li, X., Coifman, K. G., & Jin, R. (2025). arXiv:2509.13244](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.13244) **[A]** **preprint**
- Seven commercial LLMs scoring Big Five from streams of thought and daily video diaries converged with self-report at levels the authors call comparable to or above established benchmarks; averaging across models agreed best with self-report. — [Wright, A. G. C., Ringwald, W. R., Vize, C. E., Eichstaedt, J. C., Angstadt, M., Taxali, A., & Sripada, C. (2026). Assessing personality using zero-shot generative AI scoring of brief open-ended text. *Nature Human Behaviour, 10*(3), 541–555](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-025-02389-x) **[A]** (via a university repository record; article paywalled; text length per person and coefficients not read)
- Non-English evidence: ChatGPT inferring Big Five from short Czech texts by 155 participants performed competitively with human raters and showed a positivity bias on all dimensions. — [Derner, E., Kučera, D., Oliver, N., & Zahálka, J. (2024). Can ChatGPT read who you are? *Computers in Human Behavior: Artificial Humans, 2*(2), 100088](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chbah.2024.100088) **[S]** (findings from a search summary of an indexed abstract; citation confirmed in OpenAlex; coefficients not read)
- Classic baselines: word-count linguistic style relates to personality with modest effect sizes. — [Pennebaker, J. W., & King, L. A. (1999). Linguistic styles: Language use as an individual difference. *JPSP, 77*(6), 1296–1312](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.77.6.1296) **[A]**. A supervised open-vocabulary model trained on 66,732 Facebook users and tested on 4,824 gave valid, stable language-based trait scores. — [Park, G., et al. (2015). Automatic personality assessment through social media language. *JPSP, 108*(6), 934–952](https://doi.org/10.1037/pspp0000020) **[A]** (its coefficients were read only as reported in Peters & Matz)
- Evidence-span extraction is a documented practice in this area: the PsyTEx protocol has LLMs isolate psychologically informative text segments before judging traits; the authors report the extracted segments align with psychological expectations while trait interpretation by five LLMs stays significantly limited. The abstract reports no accuracy gain from extraction. — [Bhandarkar, A., Wilson, R., Swarup, A., Webster, G., & Woodard, D. (2025). PsyTEx: A knowledge-guided approach to refining text for psychological analysis. *NLP4DH 2025*, 151–178](https://aclanthology.org/2025.nlp4dh-1.14/) **[A]**
- Asking an LLM to cite its support does not guarantee the support exists: on the ALCE benchmark, even the best models lacked complete citation support about half the time on one dataset, which is why citation quality is checked automatically. — [Gao, T., Yen, H., Yu, J., & Chen, D. (2023). Enabling large language models to generate text with citations. *EMNLP 2023*](https://doi.org/10.18653/v1/2023.emnlp-main.398) **[A]**

### Inferences
- The draft's rule (every signal carries a verbatim quote; exact-match check; unmatched quotes dropped) is in line with documented practice and removes one failure class with certainty: invented quotes. It does not address the larger failure class: a real quote with a wrong reading. No source read measures how much quote-grounding reduces wrong inferences in personality or work-style reading.
- Amount of text: the positive results use hundreds to thousands of words per person or a purpose-built conversation. Three answers of 20–400 characters are an order of magnitude less than the smallest quantity any source reports on (20 status updates of about 17 words). Expect many dimensions with no evidence; "no evidence" must be a normal output, not an error.
- The Peters, Cerf and Matz pattern (.44 vs .12) says elicitation design matters more than the model: questions written to surface a specific dimension are what make text readable. That supports mapping each typed question to a few target dimensions, as the draft does.
- Run-to-run stability is high in the sources, so a single low-temperature call is defensible on cost; stability is not validity.
- Known biases to guard against in prompts and monitoring: skew toward moderate or high and positive readings (Zhu et al.; Derner et al.) and demographic differences in error (Peters & Matz). A reader that never outputs negative values on a dimension is showing this bias.
- Every result above concerns Big Five traits. The draft reads eight work-style preferences, a construct no source validates for LLM reading. Reading explicit stated preferences ("I hate being interrupted") is closer to information extraction than to trait inference and is plausibly easier, but that is an assumption.

### Gaps
- No study found on LLM inference of vocational interests (RIASEC), values or work-style preferences from short free text.
- No study found on Vietnamese text, and none on adolescent writers.
- No study found that tests whether evidence-span grounding improves accuracy or reduces fabricated trait inferences; the practice is documented, its benefit for this task is not.
- Wright et al. (2026) is the strongest peer-reviewed positive result and could only be read at abstract level.

## 7. Character creation versus honest self-description

### Takeaway
When people build a game character they describe someone better than they rate themselves, and a self-representation can feed back into behaviour. A questionnaire framed as "build your character" can therefore be expected to drift toward the ideal self; the sources support separating a "me now" layer from a "who I want to become" layer, though none tested that separation directly.

### Cited Findings
- World of Warcraft players rated their main character as having more favourable attributes than they rated themselves, and the gap was larger for players with lower psychological well-being. — [Bessière, K., Seay, A. F., & Kiesler, S. (2007). The ideal elf: Identity exploration in World of Warcraft. *CyberPsychology & Behavior, 10*(4), 530–535](https://doi.org/10.1089/cpb.2007.9994) **[A]**
- People conform to their digital self-representation independent of how others see them: participants given more attractive avatars disclosed more and stood closer; those given taller avatars negotiated more confidently. — [Yee, N., & Bailenson, J. (2007). The Proteus effect: The effect of transformed self-representation on behavior. *Human Communication Research, 33*(3), 271–290](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1468-2958.2007.00299.x) **[A]**

### Inferences
- Bessière et al. measured ratings of an existing avatar, not answers to an onboarding questionnaire, so the drift toward the ideal under a "build your character" framing is an extrapolation.
- Design consequence: put present-tense, recent-episode questions under a neutral "bạn bây giờ" label, and put the aspirational question (the draft's T3, five years ahead) under "bạn muốn thành", stored and displayed separately and not scored into the current indicators.
- The Proteus effect suggests the profile itself may nudge later in-game choices (a player shown as "thiên về hệ thống" may choose accordingly). Since the game updates the profile from choices, a self-reported profile could partly confirm itself. Showing self-report and observed-play signals as separate sources guards against that.
- Players with lower well-being idealised more, so the aspirational layer should be worded as a wish, never compared against the current layer as a "gap".

### Gaps
- No study found that manipulates questionnaire framing ("describe yourself" versus "create your character") and compares answers.
- Back et al. (2010), "Facebook profiles reflect actual personality, not self-idealization" (*Psychological Science*, DOI 10.1177/0956797609360756), is a relevant counterpoint for profile-style self-presentation, but only its title was confirmed; do not cite without reading.
- No adolescent or Vietnamese evidence.

## 8. Satisficing and realistic length for five minutes on a phone

### Takeaway
Satisficing rises with task difficulty and falls with ability and motivation, and it applies in both directions here: tapping through fourteen similar two-sided items and typing a throwaway line are the same behaviour. Five minutes on a phone supports about three short typed answers and a few taps.

### Cited Findings
- When answering well takes substantial cognitive effort, some respondents give a merely satisfactory answer: picking the first reasonable option, agreeing, not differentiating among items, saying "don't know", or choosing at random. — [Krosnick, J. A. (1991). Response strategies for coping with the cognitive demands of attitude measures in surveys. *Applied Cognitive Psychology, 5*(3), 213–236](https://doi.org/10.1002/acp.2350050305) **[A]**
- Later questions get faster, shorter and more uniform answers, and longer announced length lowers start and completion rates. — [Galesic & Bosnjak 2009](https://doi.org/10.1093/poq/nfp031) **[A]**
- Typed composition in a chat interview ran at about 21 words per minute (median 18). — [Wuttke et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.20064) **[F, partial]** **preprint**
- Gibberish or fully irrelevant responses appeared in 9.00% of form surveys and 2.48% of chatbot surveys among paid adult panellists. — [Xiao et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.10700) **[F]**

### Inferences
- Length estimate (own arithmetic from the figures above): 3 typed answers × about 25 words ≈ 75 words ≈ 3.5 to 4 minutes at 20 words per minute; that leaves about one minute, or 4 to 6 taps. Every follow-up answered adds roughly 30 to 60 seconds. The draft's 1 + 14 + 3 exceeds five minutes under these assumptions.
- A minimum length forces text but not thought; a 20-character floor will be met with filler by a satisficing respondent. Detecting low-information answers at reading time and abstaining is safer than forcing length at input time.
- Announcing the true length up front ("3 câu hỏi ngắn, khoảng 4 phút") is supported in direction by the announced-length result.

### Gaps
- No direct measurement of how many typed answers adolescents will give in a game onboarding before quitting. The length estimate should be checked in the team's own pilot by logging time per question, characters typed, and exit point.
- Peytchev (2009, *Public Opinion Quarterly*) on page-level predictors of break-off was sought and could not be opened; one search summary of a different study suggested open-ended questions were associated with lower, not higher, break-off risk, which is unverified and conflicts in direction with Luebker (2021).

## Đề xuất cho JobQuest

Các đề xuất dưới đây suy ra từ các nguồn ở trên. Chưa có nguồn nào đo trên người Việt, trên học sinh 13–17 tuổi, hay trên chỉ số "cách làm việc"; mọi con số về thời lượng là ước tính, cần kiểm bằng log của chính nhóm.

**1. Cơ cấu câu hỏi (khoảng 4–5 phút trên điện thoại)**
- 1 câu chọn giai đoạn (một chạm, không chấm).
- 3 câu gõ chữ là phần chính.
- Tối đa 4–6 câu hai vế, và chỉ hỏi cho những chiều mà chữ chưa có bằng chứng. Bỏ bộ 14 câu cố định: lý do chính của dạng hai vế là chống khai đẹp khi có lợi ích, mà JobQuest không có lợi ích nào để khai đẹp (mục 1). Nếu giữ, hai vế phải nghe "đẹp" ngang nhau.
- Báo trước độ dài thật: "3 câu hỏi ngắn, khoảng 4 phút".
- Thứ tự: câu giai đoạn, rồi câu gõ dễ nhất (kể một lần cụ thể), rồi hai câu còn lại. Câu quan trọng nhất đặt sớm vì câu về sau được trả lời ngắn hơn.

**2. Giới hạn cho câu gõ chữ**
- Gợi ý "1–3 câu là đủ", ô nhập nhiều dòng, tối đa 300 ký tự (bản nháp: 400).
- Không ép độ dài tối thiểu quá khoảng 15 ký tự; trả lời ngắn mà rỗng thì xử lý ở bước đọc, không ép ở bước nhập.
- Mỗi câu có nút "Bỏ qua câu này". Cân nhắc lại luật "đã làm thì ba câu kể là bắt buộc": chưa có bằng chứng ủng hộ hay bác bỏ.
- Một dòng dẫn ngắn ngay trên ô nhập: chữ này dùng để làm gì và ai đọc ("Chỉ AI đọc để dựng hồ sơ cho riêng bạn. Đừng ghi tên người hay tên trường.").

**3. Bốn kiểu câu mồi**
Nguyên tắc chung: câu mồi chỉ ra *khía cạnh nên kể* (làm gì, với ai, điều gì giữ bạn lại), không đưa *nội dung trả lời*; nêu cả hai cực ngang nhau; không dùng tính từ khen chê; hỏi một lần cụ thể gần đây thay vì hỏi "bạn là người thế nào".
- **Câu hỏi phụ nêu hai cực:** "Kể một lần gần đây bạn làm gì đó mà quên cả giờ. Lúc đó bạn làm một mình hay cùng người khác? Điều gì khiến bạn chưa muốn dừng?"
- **Câu bỏ lửng:** "Mình nhanh hết pin nhất khi phải…"
- **Chọn rồi kể:** "Nhóm vừa nhận việc mới. Bạn nhận phần nào trước: *đi hỏi xem người ta cần gì* · *gỡ phần khó nhất* · *soát lại cho chắc*? Vì sao là phần đó?" Lượt chọn là tín hiệu đóng, câu "vì sao" là chữ để đọc.
- **Ví dụ theo cặp trái chiều, chỉ hiện khi bấm "Cần gợi ý?":** "Có người viết: *ngồi vẽ sơ đồ một mình tới khuya*. Có người viết: *cả nhóm cãi nhau một hồi mới ra cách làm*. Còn bạn?" Đảo thứ tự hai ví dụ ngẫu nhiên, và ghi log việc người chơi đã mở gợi ý.

**4. AI hỏi tiếp**
- Tối đa **1 câu hỏi tiếp cho mỗi câu, tối đa 2 câu trong cả bài**. Không hỏi tiếp ở câu gõ đầu tiên, vì lượt hỏi tiếp đầu là chỗ người ta bỏ.
- Chỉ hỏi tiếp khi câu trả lời không cho bằng chứng nào dùng được. Câu trả lời đã đủ thì không hỏi.
- Câu hỏi tiếp chỉ xin ví dụ hoặc hỏi "vì sao", không gợi tính cách hay chiều nào.
- Báo rõ số lượt và cho bỏ qua: "Hỏi thêm 1 câu thôi, bỏ qua cũng được".
- Mạng chậm: quá vài giây chưa có câu hỏi tiếp thì đi tiếp, không bắt chờ.
- Nói trước ở màn mở đầu rằng người chơi sẽ trò chuyện với AI.

**5. Từ chữ sang chỉ số, kiểm lại được**
- Giữ luật của bản nháp: mỗi giá trị kèm trích nguyên văn, máy chủ so khớp chính xác, không khớp thì bỏ. Luật này chặn được trích dẫn bịa, **không** chặn được việc đọc sai một câu trích thật.
- Thêm bốn luật ở máy chủ: (a) câu trích dài ít nhất khoảng 3 từ; (b) bỏ câu trích chỉ lặp lại chữ của câu mồi hoặc của ví dụ; (c) "không có bằng chứng" là đầu ra hợp lệ cho mọi chiều; (d) một chiều chỉ hiện giá trị khi có ít nhất hai bằng chứng độc lập (hai câu trả lời khác nhau, hoặc một câu trích cộng một câu chọn).
- Hiện câu trích cho người chơi ngay cạnh chỉ số, kèm hai nút "Đúng với mình" và "Chưa đúng"; bấm "Chưa đúng" thì gỡ tín hiệu đó. Đây là lớp kiểm thứ hai do chính người chơi làm.
- Lưu kèm: mã câu hỏi, câu trích, giá trị, tên model, phiên bản prompt, người chơi có mở gợi ý không.
- Một lần gọi model cho cả bài, nhiệt độ thấp. Lọc mẫu số điện thoại, email, đường dẫn trước khi gửi.
- Trước khi phát hành, đọc tay 30–50 bài để đếm lỗi "trích đúng, hiểu sai", và theo dõi phân bố giá trị: các nghiên cứu cho thấy model hay lệch về phía trung bình và phía tích cực, nên prompt phải yêu cầu tìm bằng chứng cho cả hai cực.
- Nhãn hiển thị: "Bạn tự kể · AI đọc · có thể chưa đúng". Không gọi là tính cách; độ khớp giữa AI đọc chữ và bảng hỏi tính cách trong các nghiên cứu chỉ khoảng r = 0,2–0,45, và đó là với lượng chữ nhiều hơn hẳn.

**6. Khi chữ cho quá ít bằng chứng**
- Chiều đó hiện "chưa đủ dữ liệu", không hiện điểm giữa.
- Mời 4–6 câu hai vế chỉ cho các chiều còn trống; có thể bỏ qua.
- Phần còn lại để lựa chọn khi chơi lấp dần (đúng tinh thần D-03).
- Không suy ra gì từ độ dài, chính tả hay giọng văn.

**7. Tách "bạn bây giờ" và "bạn muốn thành"**
- Hai câu gõ đầu hỏi chuyện đã xảy ra gần đây, đặt dưới nhãn "bạn bây giờ" và được chấm vào chỉ số.
- Câu về năm năm tới (T3) đặt dưới nhãn "bạn muốn thành", lưu và hiện riêng, **không cộng vào chỉ số hiện tại**. Không so hai lớp thành "khoảng cách".
- Trên hồ sơ, tách nguồn "tự kể" và nguồn "khi chơi" để hồ sơ tự khai không tự xác nhận chính nó.

**Ba điều chưa trả lời được, cần nhóm tự đo:** tỉ lệ bỏ giữa chừng ở từng câu gõ trên điện thoại; tỉ lệ bài mà AI đọc ra dưới hai chiều có bằng chứng; tỉ lệ "trích đúng, hiểu sai" khi đọc tay.
