## 1. Career counselling and psychology methods: what each elicits, evidence, fit for adolescents

### Takeaway
The best-documented fit for JobQuest is Savickas's Career Construction Interview in its self-guided form, the *My Career Story* workbook: four indirect questions (admired figures, favourite media, favourite story, favourite saying) that surface self-concept, preferred settings and a "script" without asking "what are you like?", already used with early adolescents and written to be answered alone. Its evidence is promising but thin (small, non-randomised studies), and none of the methods reviewed has been validated as an input to automated scoring, so the team must present all of them as conversation starters, not measurements.

### Cited Findings

**Career Construction Interview (CCI) and My Career Story (MCS)**
- The CCI has one opening question plus five story questions. Published purposes: opening question (how the counsellor can be useful) elicits counselling goals; admired figures while growing up portray the self or reputation; favourite magazines or TV shows indicate manifest interests and preferred work settings; current favourite book or movie links self to setting (a "script"); favourite saying or motto gives self-advice for the next episode; earliest recollections (three stories from about ages 3–6) state the central preoccupation or problem. — [Hartung & Santilli, *Journal of Career Assessment*, advance online 2017, Table 1](https://www.marksavickas.com/files/1_Savickas_bio/Career%20Construction%20Theory/Publications/Articles/MCS_Validity.pdf), DOI [10.1177/1069072717692980](https://doi.org/10.1177/1069072717692980) **[full, pp. 1–8 of 14]**
- The MCS workbook keeps only **four** of the five CCI story questions: it drops early recollections. It opens instead with two prompts: write a few sentences about your current career situation and the decision you face, and list the occupations you are now considering (the workbook explicitly allows "none at all"). — [Savickas & Hartung, *My Career Story* workbook, 2012/2021, pp. 4–9](https://www.marksavickas.com/files/1_Savickas_bio/Career%20Construction%20Theory/Publications/Books/My_Career_Story-2.pdf) **[full, pp. 1–10]**; confirmed in [Hartung & Santilli, p. 4](https://www.marksavickas.com/files/1_Savickas_bio/Career%20Construction%20Theory/Publications/Articles/MCS_Validity.pdf) **[full]**
- How the MCS phrases its questions (useful as a model for "câu mồi"): the role-model question (the only item quoted in these notes: "Who did you admire when you were growing up?") asks for three people **other than mother and father**, admired at about age six to eight, allows real or fictional figures, lists neutral categories of who it could be (neighbour, teacher, athlete, scientist, cartoon character…), and asks for only four or five describing words per figure. The media question asks for three shows, websites or magazines and what the person likes about each. The story question asks what happens and how it ends. The saying question allows the person to invent their own if none comes to mind. — [MCS workbook, pp. 6–9](https://www.marksavickas.com/files/1_Savickas_bio/Career%20Construction%20Theory/Publications/Books/My_Career_Story-2.pdf) **[full]**
- The MCS is described by its authors as a self-directed, counsellor-free workbook that individuals, groups and educators may use, including as the syllabus of a high-school or college career course. Stated aims: narratability, intentionality, career adaptability. — [Hartung & Santilli, pp. 3, 5](https://www.marksavickas.com/files/1_Savickas_bio/Career%20Construction%20Theory/Publications/Articles/MCS_Validity.pdf) **[full]**
- **Terms of use.** The workbook carries the notice "Copyright © 2012, 2021 by Mark L. Savickas and Paul J. Hartung"; I found no licence or permission statement on pages 1–10. — [MCS workbook, title page](https://www.marksavickas.com/files/1_Savickas_bio/Career%20Construction%20Theory/Publications/Books/My_Career_Story-2.pdf) **[full, pp. 1–10]**. The authors' journal article says the MCS is available free of charge at vocopher.com in English and Portuguese versions. — [Hartung & Santilli, p. 3](https://www.marksavickas.com/files/1_Savickas_bio/Career%20Construction%20Theory/Publications/Articles/MCS_Validity.pdf) **[full]**
- **Validity evidence is preliminary.** Usability: 22 counselling graduate students rated the workbook's clarity and ease of use, mean item rating 4.5 on a 5-point scale (SD .43). Validity: latent semantic analysis found a mean agreement of .81 between life portraits written by 10 participants and portraits written for them by expert counsellors; the authors themselves call this initial and call for more research. — [Hartung & Santilli, abstract and p. 5](https://www.marksavickas.com/files/1_Savickas_bio/Career%20Construction%20Theory/Publications/Articles/MCS_Validity.pdf) **[full]**
- **Early adolescents.** A group intervention built on the MCS was compared with a traditional career intervention in 108 early adolescents (27 girls and 27 boys per group), assigned by convenience, not randomly. The MCS group, but not the control group, increased on career adaptability (concern and control dimensions) and future orientation; neither group changed on hope, optimism or resilience; participants rated the intervention useful and satisfying. — [Santilli, Nota & Hartung (2019), *Journal of Vocational Behavior*, 111, 49–58, repository record](https://neomed.omeka.net/items/show/6391), DOI [10.1016/j.jvb.2018.06.007](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jvb.2018.06.007) **[abstract]**
- A quasi-experimental life-design group study with 236 Portuguese students in Grades 9 and 12 reportedly found effects on career certainty and career decision self-efficacy but not on career adaptability. — [Cardoso, Janeiro & Duarte (2017), *Journal of Career Development*](https://doi.org/10.1177/0894845316687668) **[secondary: search-result summary of the abstract; not opened]**
- A pre/post study with urban youth of colour reportedly found gains in vocational identity and in the control and confidence dimensions of adaptability. — [Cadaret & Hartung, *British Journal of Guidance & Counselling*, repository record](https://neomed.omeka.net/items/show/11217) **[secondary: search-result summary]**

**McAdams's Life Story Interview (key scenes)**
- A condensed version published by Northwestern in 2005 has six steps: life chapters (two to seven, each named); eight critical events (peak experience, low point, turning point, earliest memory, a significant childhood scene, teenage scene, adult scene, one other scene), each described as a specific moment with feelings, people present and what it says about the person; greatest life challenge; most positive and most negative influences; a hoped-for and a feared future; and a life theme. — [Northwestern Magazine, Winter 2005, sidebar to the feature on McAdams](https://www.northwestern.edu/magazine/winter2005/feature/redemption/sidebar1.html) **[full; condensed version, not the research protocol]**
- A 2023 research adaptation that cites "McAdams DP (2008) The Life Story Interview" keeps: chapters; one high point and one low point per chapter with their lasting impact; a pride question; a what-would-you-change question; a motto or lesson for young people; the next chapter (plans and dreams); and a closing reflection on how the interview felt. — [Supplementary interview scheme, *Ageing & Society* article S0144686X23000715](https://static.cambridge.org/content/id/urn:cambridge.org:id:article:S0144686X23000715/resource/name/S0144686X23000715sup001.pdf) **[full; adapted version]**
- The ability to tell a coherent life story develops during adolescence: a review distinguishes four kinds of global coherence (temporal, biographical, causal, thematic) and concludes that the cognitive tools and social motives for building a life story emerge in adolescence. — [Habermas & Bluck (2000), *Psychological Bulletin*, 126(5), 748–769](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.126.5.748) **[secondary: abstract as summarised in search results]**

**Motivational interviewing (MI): open questions, affirmations, reflections, summaries**
- MI is described by its trainers' network as a guiding style between following and directing, meant to strengthen a person's own reasons for change; it is a way of communicating, not an intervention in itself. Core skills (OARS): open questions explore the person's experience; affirmations name strengths and past successes; reflections restate or deepen what was said to show understanding; summaries confirm shared understanding. The "spirit" is partnership, acceptance, compassion and evocation (people already hold the resources; the helper draws out their values). — [MINT, "Understanding Motivational Interviewing", citing Miller & Rollnick, 3rd ed., 2013](https://motivationalinterviewing.org/understanding-motivational-interviewing) **[full; institutional summary, not the book]**
- With adolescents: a meta-analysis of 21 studies of MI for adolescent substance use reportedly found a small but significant overall effect (d = .173). An independent appraisal cautions that quality was not assessed and most studies were a single session. — [Jensen et al. (2011), *Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology*, 79(4), 433–440, as summarised by Drug and Alcohol Findings](https://www.drugsandalcohol.ie/17795/) **[secondary: figures from search-result summaries, not read in the paper]**

**Laddering (Reynolds & Gutman 1988)**
- Reynolds & Gutman's article is described as the most detailed account of the laddering interview and as extending it to quantitative analysis through an "implication matrix"; the technique comes from personal construct psychology and climbs from concrete product attributes to higher-order personal constructs. — [Bell, "Marketing", *Internet Encyclopaedia of Personal Construct Psychology*, 2004](https://www.pcp-net.org/encyclopaedia/marketing.html) **[full for the encyclopaedia entry; Reynolds & Gutman itself is secondary]**. Citation as given there: Reynolds, T. J., & Gutman, J. (1988). Laddering theory, method, analysis, and interpretation. *Journal of Advertising Research*, 28, 11–31.

**Best Possible Self (King 2001)**
- The exercise asks people to write about their best possible future self after everything has gone as well as it possibly could. A meta-analysis of 29 studies (26 articles, 2,909 participants) found pooled effects versus control of d+ = .325 for wellbeing, .334 for optimism, .511 for positive affect, .192 for negative affect and .115 for depressive symptoms (the last from three studies only). — [Carrillo et al. (2019), *PLoS ONE*, 14(9), e0222386](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0222386) **[full, first ~100,000 characters; King 2001 itself is secondary]**
- Moderators (wellbeing): shorter total practice time and older age were each associated with larger effects at marginal significance (p = .078 and p = .079); online versus face-to-face delivery did not moderate. — [Carrillo et al. (2019)](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0222386) **[full]**
- Samples were mainly university students and young adults; study mean ages ran from 17.83 to 35.62; no study met all nine quality criteria and only three had follow-ups beyond three months. — [Carrillo et al. (2019)](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0222386) **[full]**

**Twenty Statements Test (TST)**
- The TST asks for up to twenty completions of "I am…" in answer to "Who am I?"; it was introduced by Kuhn & McPartland (1954, *American Sociological Review*, 19, 68–76). — [SAGE encyclopaedia entry and related summaries via search](https://sk.sagepub.com/ency/edvol/embed/socialscience/chpt/twenty-statements-test) **[secondary]**
- With adolescents: 822 pupils aged 13–18 in three English schools completed the stem up to 20 times in five minutes or less, instructed not to think too much. They produced 6,558 self-descriptions, about eight per person; most were traits and most were positive (happy, funny, kind) alongside roles (son or daughter, student, friend). Design was cross-sectional and monocultural. — [BPS Research Digest, 28 May 2019, reporting Hards et al., *Memory*, 2019](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/teenagers-define-themselves-mostly-terms-their-positive-traits) **[secondary: digest read in full; journal article not opened]**

**Values card sorts**
- The Personal Values Card Sort (Miller, C'de Baca, Matthews & Wilbourne, University of New Mexico, 2001) is listed by the Motivational Interviewing Network of Trainers as public domain; hosted copies state it may be copied or adapted without further permission. People sort value cards into importance piles and then pick their top few. — [MINT resource listing](https://motivationalinterviewing.org/node/4481) **[secondary: search-result summary; the CASAA PDF I fetched could not be text-extracted]**; [CASAA copy](https://casaa.unm.edu/assets/inst/personal-values-card-sort-2.pdf)
- The Organizational Culture Profile (O'Reilly, Chatman & Caldwell, 1991) uses the same sorting logic with 54 value statements to compare a person's profile with an organisation's. — see section 4.

**Holland's "party" exercise**
- Counsellor-education material describes a "career interest party": six groups of people standing for the six RIASEC types; the person picks the group they would join first, second and third, giving a three-letter code. It is attributed to Bolles and used with adolescents as a discussion starter. — [NCDA Career Convergence article](https://www.ncda.org/aws/NCDA/pt/sd/news_article/554248/_self/CC_layout_details/false) **[secondary: search-result summary]**
- An undergraduate honours thesis (473 undergraduates) compared the party exercise with the Self-Directed Search and concluded the results do not support it as a valid estimate of a Holland code. — [University of Central Florida honours thesis](https://stars.library.ucf.edu/honorstheses1990-2015/1888) **[secondary: search-result summary; unpublished thesis, weak evidence either way]**

**Johari window**
- A four-quadrant model (open, blind, hidden, unknown) of what is known to self and to others, published by Luft and Ingham in 1955 in the proceedings of a UCLA training laboratory. — [Wikipedia, "Johari window"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johari_window) **[secondary; original paper not located]**

### Inferences
- **Indirect beats direct.** The CCI/MCS logic is to ask about admired figures, media and stories and let themes emerge, instead of asking for a self-description. That matches the team lead's principle and gives an LLM concrete text to quote back.
- **The MCS already shows what a good "câu mồi" looks like:** it widens the set of acceptable answers (real or fictional people; "none at all" is allowed; invent your own saying), shrinks the size of the answer (four or five words), and uses neutral category lists rather than model answers. The one risky device is its worked example answers (it shows what one might write about specific famous figures), which could anchor a teenager; JobQuest cues should copy the first three devices and avoid the fourth.
- **Drop what the authors dropped.** The self-guided MCS omits early recollections, the CCI question aimed at a person's central preoccupation or pain. A game with no counsellor present should also omit it, and should omit McAdams's low point, life challenge and negative-influence prompts for the same reason (see section 3).
- **From McAdams, borrow the scene rule, not the interview.** Ask for one specific moment (what happened, who was there, what you felt), not a period or a whole-life overview. Given Habermas & Bluck, younger teens can narrate one scene more reliably than they can connect scenes into a theme; the thematic linking is the LLM's job, and must stay visibly tentative.
- **MI maps onto the AI's reply, not the question list.** Open question → the bank; affirmation → the profile summary should name a strength the user actually showed in their text; reflection → the AI's optional follow-up should restate before asking; summary → the self-description shown at the end. This also fits D-02 (no "you fit job X"): MI evokes, it does not prescribe.
- **Laddering should be one rung, not three.** One "và điều đó quan trọng với bạn vì…?" follow-up reaches a value; repeated why-questions feel like interrogation and add typing on a phone. This is my design judgement; I did not find a study of ladder depth with adolescents.
- **Best Possible Self is a good frame for T3 but is an "ideal self" layer.** Its evidence is for mood and optimism in adults, not for career decisions or for under-17s; and shorter practice worked at least as well, which suits a single short answer. Do not read T3-type answers as evidence of present traits.
- **Sentence stems are fast for teenagers.** Thirteen-to-eighteen-year-olds produced about eight "I am…" completions each within five minutes, so three stems inside a minute is plausible. Expect mostly positive trait words; those are weak evidence on their own and need a behavioural question beside them.
- **Party exercise and card sorts belong to the "chọn rồi kể" format** in the plan: pick first, then write one line about why. The pick alone should not be scored as an interest code (no good evidence that the party exercise recovers a Holland code).

### Gaps
- I could not open Savickas's own CCI manual or the vocopher.com download page; the CCI purposes above come from the authors' journal article. **No explicit licence** for translating or reproducing the MCS was found. If the team wants to use MCS wording verbatim or publish a Vietnamese translation, write to the authors (the article lists Paul Hartung as corresponding author). Adapted, paraphrased questions with credit are what this note provides.
- The original Foley Center page for the Life Story Interview returned an error (HTTP 410); I could not read the 2008 protocol or any permission statement. The two versions cited are a magazine condensation and a third-party adaptation.

- No randomised trial of the MCS or CCI with adolescents was found. No Vietnamese validation of any of these methods was found.
- No study was found on using CCI-type answers as input to automated (LLM) scoring.

## 2. Career guidance practice in Vietnam ("hướng nghiệp")

### Takeaway
Official Vietnamese guidance defines what students should be able to say about themselves (strengths, weaker points, interests, fit with a chosen occupation group), and ILO and VVOB produced teacher and student materials, but I could not obtain any published Vietnamese question set in full text. The team's questions will therefore be self-written and must be labelled as unvalidated.

### Cited Findings
- The 2018 general-education curriculum makes "Hoạt động trải nghiệm, hướng nghiệp" compulsory in lower and upper secondary school (Circular 32/2018/TT-BGDĐT). Requirements include, at lower-secondary level, pointing out some stronger and weaker points in one's own qualities and competences related to a favoured occupation and expressing one's interests positively; at upper-secondary level, assessing one's strengths, weaknesses and capacity to change, and judging whether one's qualities and competences fit a chosen occupation group. — [Reproduction of the curriculum text, hoatieu.vn](https://hoatieu.vn/chuong-trinh-giao-duc-pho-thong-mon-hoat-dong-trai-nghiem-256395) **[secondary: third-party reproduction read; the official gazette text was not opened; grade-by-grade wording not verified]**
- The ILO developed a career guidance package for Viet Nam from November 2013 with Vietnamese education experts; the Ministry of Education and Training appraised it in August 2014. It contains workbooks and guidebooks for students and teachers, a career dictionary and a toolkit, intended to help students understand careers, job opportunities and their own strengths and weaknesses. It was piloted in the first half of 2014 with about 2,000 secondary and high-school students and 300 out-of-school youths in Phú Thọ, Quảng Nam and Thừa Thiên-Huế. — [ILO news, 6 December 2014](https://www.ilo.org/resource/news/innovative-career-guidance-connect-dots-better-jobs-young-people) **[full]**
- In late 2019 the ILO and the Ministry were finalising an updated, customised "Career Guidance Toolbox", introduced to teachers and employment-service staff in several provinces. The article does not describe the toolbox's contents and gives no download link. — [ILO article, 20 December 2019](https://www.ilo.org/resource/article/update-ilos-career-guidance-toolbox-viet-nam) **[full]**
- A 2026 ILO procurement notice seeks a consultant to review and update the ILO career guidance package. — [UNGM notice 301831](https://www.ungm.org/Public/Notice/301831) **[secondary: search-result summary]**
- VVOB Vietnam commissioned (2014) the completion of training material on individual counselling skills for career exploration, choice and development for secondary students, and retained intellectual-property rights over it. — [VVOB terms of reference, March 2014](https://ngocentre.org.vn/pipermail/opportunities/attachments/20140319/dea2705c/attachment-0002.pdf) **[secondary: search-result summary]**
- A "career tree" model circulates in Vietnamese guidance: the roots are four things a student should understand about themselves (interests, abilities, personality, occupational values) and the fruits are career outcomes. — [Hanoi University of Industry career hub page](https://careerhub.haui.edu.vn/vn/ly-thuyet-huong-nghiep/ly-thuyet-cay-nghe-nghiep-trong-huong-nghiep/63311) **[secondary: search-result summary; the page returned 404 when fetched]**
- A Vietnamese IT-recruitment article (ITviec, 11 December 2025) lists typical culture-fit questions; see section 4. — [ITviec story hub](https://itviec.com/story-hub/chia-se-ve-cultural-fit-screening-va-cach-tra-loi-cau-hoi-tinh-huong-nhat-quan-chan-that-3240) **[full]**

### Inferences
- The official curriculum asks students for exactly the self-knowledge JobQuest's profile targets (interests, strong and weaker points, fit with an occupation group), so a short self-reflection is consistent with what schools are already required to do. This is a usable argument for the report, with the caveat that the curriculum text was read in a reproduction.
- The four "roots" of the career-tree model (sở thích, khả năng, cá tính, giá trị nghề nghiệp) line up with the plan's indicator families (interests, strengths, work style, values). Using these four Vietnamese words as section labels would make the flow feel familiar to students and teachers. Treat this as a naming convenience, not as evidence.
- Vietnamese materials frame self-knowledge as "điểm mạnh, điểm yếu". For a product read by minors alone, the bank below asks about strengths directly and gets at weaker points only through neutral preferences ("việc nào làm bạn nhanh hết năng lượng"), never "điểm yếu của bạn là gì".

### Gaps
- **No Vietnamese published question set was read in full.** The ILO package, the 2019 toolbox, the VVOB counselling material and the Ministry's teacher-training documents were not available as open full text in this session. Ask a school career teacher or VVOB/ILO Hanoi directly if the team wants them.
- The official text of Circular 32/2018 and the share of curriculum time for career-orientation content were not verified at source.
- No material from a Vietnamese university counselling centre with a published question list was found.

## 3. Self-reflection prompts for adolescents: appropriate topics, sensitive areas, safeguarding

### Takeaway
Keep prompts on everyday, chosen experiences (media, hobbies, school and group work, things made, hoped-for futures) and leave out the prompt types that established interviews use to reach pain (earliest memories, low points, greatest challenge, negative influences, loneliness). Free text from minors can still contain disclosures, so the product needs an honest statement of who reads the text, a skip on every question, and a fixed supportive response with the national child helpline; it should not let the model improvise counselling.

### Cited Findings
- The self-guided MCS omits the CCI's early-recollections question, which in the counsellor-led interview is the one aimed at the client's central preoccupation or problem. — [Hartung & Santilli, Table 1 and p. 4](https://www.marksavickas.com/files/1_Savickas_bio/Career%20Construction%20Theory/Publications/Articles/MCS_Validity.pdf) **[full]**
- The MCS role-model question explicitly excludes the respondent's mother and father. — [MCS workbook, p. 6](https://www.marksavickas.com/files/1_Savickas_bio/Career%20Construction%20Theory/Publications/Books/My_Career_Story-2.pdf) **[full]**
- McAdams-type interviews include prompts for a low point of despair, guilt or pain, a greatest life challenge, and the people who influenced the story most negatively. — [Northwestern Magazine, 2005](https://www.northwestern.edu/magazine/winter2005/feature/redemption/sidebar1.html) **[full]**
- Aron's closeness procedure escalates from light questions to family, worst memories and crying; a reader comment on a university practice page notes that some of these questions may distress people with trauma, a point the page itself does not address. — [Greater Good in Action, UC Berkeley](https://ggia.berkeley.edu/practice/36_questions_for_increasing_closeness) **[full for the practice page; the caution is a user comment, weak evidence]**
- Guidance for adults responding to a child's disclosure: listen and let the child set the pace; reflect back using their words; reassure them they did the right thing and that it is not their fault; **never promise to keep what they say secret**; record their exact words; report through the organisation's safeguarding procedure, and call emergency services if the child is in immediate danger. The page also says not to wait for a disclosure before acting on concerns. — [NSPCC Learning, "Recognising and responding to child abuse and neglect", updated 6 November 2025](https://learning.nspcc.org.uk/child-abuse-and-neglect/recognising-and-responding-to-abuse) **[full]**
- UNICEF notes that many chatbots are not built to detect or respond to users in distress, and that people seeking help may get error messages or irrelevant answers that increase isolation or guilt; it publishes an implementation guide with safeguarding blueprints for chatbots that may reach at-risk users, especially girls, women and children in vulnerable situations. — [UNICEF, Safer Chatbots Implementation Guide, landing page](https://www.unicef.org/documents/safer-chatbots-implementation-guide) **[full for the landing page; the guide PDF was not opened]**
- A UNICEF East Asia and Pacific learning brief on chatbots that answer young people's private questions sets out seven safeguarding steps and warns that even a seemingly harmless inadequate response may deter a young person from seeking help later. — [Brown & Binder, UNICEF learning brief](https://www.unicef.org/eap/media/5376/file) **[secondary: search-result summary; PDF not retrievable]**
- Viet Nam's national child-protection helpline is 111: free of charge, operating around the clock, run under the government's children's department, offering reporting and psychological counselling for children and carers; also reachable through a Zalo account. — [Pháp Luật TP.HCM](https://plo.vn/tong-dai-quoc-gia-111-ket-noi-de-bao-ve-tre-em-post663452.html); [VietTimes](https://viettimes.vn/tong-dai-mien-phi-bao-ve-tre-em-111-hoat-dong-247-post65407.html) **[secondary: press reports via search summary; no official page opened]**
- Among 13–18-year-olds completing "I am…" stems, most self-descriptions were positive traits and social roles; some (more often girls) wrote "tired", "shy" or comments on appearance. — [BPS Research Digest on Hards et al., 2019](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/teenagers-define-themselves-mostly-terms-their-positive-traits) **[secondary]**

### Inferences
- **Age-appropriate topics** (all present in the MCS, which has been run with early adolescents): things you watch, read or play; people or characters you admire; stories you like; a saying you like; things you have made or fixed; roles you take in a class or club; what you would do with a free day; a hoped-for future day. All can be answered with no work history.
- **Leave out by design:** earliest memories; worst or lowest moments; biggest failure or regret; family circumstances, income or parents' jobs; health, body and appearance; mental-health labels; romantic relationships; religion and politics; "what would you change about yourself"; "what are your weaknesses". Even the mild "drain" question (T2) and open stems can surface distress (teenagers do write "tired", "shy"), so wording should stay about tasks and settings, not about the self.
- **Role-model question:** follow the MCS and steer away from parents, but do it by widening rather than excluding ("người thật, nhân vật truyện, phim, game đều được"), so the question never touches family circumstances.
- **Translating the NSPCC rule to a product:** "never promise secrecy" becomes "say truthfully who and what reads the answer". JobQuest's rule that results are private (BR-06) means no human will see a disclosure; so the product must not imply that someone is listening, and must hand the user to people who can (helpline 111, a trusted adult). Proposed handling, to be decided by the team: (1) a one-line notice before the first free-text box: an AI reads the answers to build the profile, nobody else sees them, do not write real names, school or address; (2) a visible "Bỏ qua" on every question; (3) if a text signals self-harm, abuse or acute distress, the profile pipeline stops scoring that answer, shows a fixed, pre-written supportive message with 111, and does not echo the content in the profile summary; (4) the model is instructed not to diagnose, not to counsel, not to ask follow-up questions on that content.
- Detecting distress with keywords or a model will produce both misses and false alarms; a fixed, gentle message is cheap when wrong, which is why it is preferable to a model-written reply.

### Gaps
- I did not find guidance from a recognised body written specifically for **career or education products with no human moderator** receiving disclosures from minors; the NSPCC page addresses adults in organisations. The UNICEF chatbot documents are the closest, and their detailed steps were not read.
- No official Vietnamese government page for helpline 111 was opened; confirm the number and wording before putting it in the UI.
- Vietnamese legal duties (child-protection law, personal-data rules for minors, mandatory reporting) were **not researched here** and need a separate check before the handling above is adopted.
- No empirical source was found on which self-reflection topics adolescents find intrusive in a Vietnamese context.

## 4. Recruiting and selection: structured interviews, culture fit, adapting questions for people with no job history

### Takeaway
Structured interviews are among the most valid selection methods and both past-behaviour and situational questions work; what makes them work is structure (same questions, defined criteria), not clever wording. Culture-fit questioning has real predictive links to satisfaction and retention but is also a documented channel for similarity bias, which supports JobQuest's rule of describing work preferences without issuing a fit verdict.

### Cited Findings
- Campion, Palmer & Campion (1997, *Personnel Psychology*, 50, 655–702) identify fifteen components of interview structure, seven about content (job analysis, same questions for everyone, limited prompting, better question types, longer interviews, controlling ancillary information, no questions from the applicant until the end) and eight about evaluation (rating each answer, anchored scales, notes, multiple and consistent interviewers, training…), and conclude that interviews are easily improved by adding structure. — [ProQuest record](https://www.proquest.com/docview/220138496) **[abstract, via search-result summary]**
- A 2022 re-analysis of selection meta-analyses (Sackett, Zhang, Berry & Lievens, *Journal of Applied Psychology*, 107, 2040–2068, DOI 10.1037/apl0000994) corrected earlier over-corrections for range restriction; as reported by a practitioner summary, structured interviews then had the highest mean operational validity at .42, ahead of job-knowledge tests (.40), empirically keyed biodata (.38), work samples (.33) and cognitive ability (.31); interests were .24. The authors note that structured-interview validity varies widely across settings. — [O'Shea & Luscombe, SIOP *TIP* article summarising Sackett et al.](https://www.siop.org/tip-article/is-cognitive-ability-the-best-predictor-of-job-performance) **[secondary: summary read in full; the journal article was not opened]**
- The US Office of Personnel Management defines a structured interview as one that applies rules for eliciting, observing and evaluating responses; behavioural questions ask for a past behaviour in a relevant situation (premise: behavioural consistency), situational questions ask what the person would do (premise: people can project their own behaviour). Both are described as effective; behavioural questions show higher validity for highly complex jobs; faking is described as less frequent than on written inventories measuring the same competencies. — [OPM, "Structured Interviews"](https://www.opm.gov/policy-data-oversight/assessment-and-selection/other-assessment-methods/structured-interviews/) **[full]**
- Person–organisation fit: the Organizational Culture Profile (O'Reilly, Chatman & Caldwell, 1991, *Academy of Management Journal*, 34(3), 487–516) uses 54 value statements in a card-sort format; a NIOSH catalogue entry lists an overall coefficient alpha of .88. — [NIOSH instrument entry](https://archive.cdc.gov/www_cdc_gov/niosh/topics/workorg/detail059.html); [ProQuest record](https://proquest.com/scholarly-journals/people-organizational-culture-profile-comparison/docview/199849384/se-2) **[secondary: search-result summaries; article not opened]**
- Culture fit as bias: from 120 interviews with employers at elite professional-service firms and observation of a hiring committee, Rivera argues hiring is cultural matching as well as skills sorting; evaluators favoured candidates similar to themselves in leisure pursuits, experiences and self-presentation, and these concerns often weighed as heavily as productivity. — [Rivera (2012), *American Sociological Review*, 999–1022, Kellogg research page](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/academics-research/research/detail/2012/hiring-as-cultural-matching-the-case-of-elite-professional/) **[abstract]**
- "Culture fit versus culture add": an LSE review piece notes that fit is linked in meta-analysis (Kristof-Brown, Zimmerman & Johnson, 2005) to job satisfaction, citizenship behaviour and lower turnover, but that interview-based fit judgements can mistake culturally specific behaviour for values and carry similarity bias; it proposes "culture add" (hire for the values the organisation lacks) and psychometric rather than impressionistic assessment of values. The "culture add" term itself is sourced there to a media article, not to research. — [Will, "Should you hire for culture fit?", LSE Business Review, 5 May 2022](https://blogs.lse.ac.uk/businessreview/?p=41330) **[full; secondary commentary]**
- Amazon publishes 16 Leadership Principles (among them Customer Obsession, Ownership, Learn and Be Curious, Dive Deep, Bias for Action, Have Backbone; Disagree and Commit, Deliver Results). The principles page does not state how they are used in interviews. — [Amazon Jobs, Leadership Principles](https://www.amazon.jobs/content/en/our-workplace/leadership-principles) **[full]**. Sample interview questions circulating online come from third-party preparation sites, not from Amazon. — [search results, e.g. igotanoffer](https://igotanoffer.com/blogs/tech/amazon-behavioral-interview) **[secondary]**
- Netflix's culture memo is organised under "The Dream Team", "People Over Process", "Uncomfortably Exciting" and "Great and Always Better", lists eight valued behaviours (selflessness, judgment, candor, creativity, courage, inclusion, curiosity, resilience) and describes the "keeper test". It does not use the phrase "culture add". — [Netflix Jobs, culture memo](https://jobs.netflix.com/culture) **[full]**
- Vietnamese IT practice (one practitioner article, not company policy): typical culture-fit questions include a time you disagreed with a team's technical decision; how you react to a tight deadline; a hard piece of feedback and what you did; whether you prefer clear requirements or exploring on your own; comfort with direct feedback; how far you take responsibility for a task; handling conflict; whether you can say no; what makes work meaningful; what tells you that you are improving; speed versus accuracy. Advice: prepare three to five true stories in a situation–action–result shape; answer as you really work, because a wrong match tires the candidate; if you have never met the situation, say so and then describe what you would do. — [Khanh Ha, ITviec Story Hub, 11 December 2025](https://itviec.com/story-hub/chia-se-ve-cultural-fit-screening-va-cach-tra-loi-cau-hoi-tinh-huong-nhat-quan-chan-that-3240) **[full]**

### Inferences
- **What transfers from structured interviewing to JobQuest is the discipline, not the hiring purpose:** every user gets the same questions; each question has a declared target (the indicator codes in the bank); the reader (LLM) scores against written criteria and must quote the text it relied on. That is Campion's content and evaluation structure applied to a self-profile.
- **Company principles are useful as a vocabulary of work-style dimensions** that IT employers actually name: curiosity and self-teaching, ownership, attention to detail, bias for action versus thoroughness, candour and feedback, coping with ambiguity. Several map directly onto the team's eight dimensions (MOHO, TIMI, APLUC, NGUOI). They should inspire question topics; they should not be presented as criteria the user is judged against.
- **Adapting "tell me about a time…" for people with no job history:** change the *domain list*, keep the structure. Each behavioural question in the bank names several neutral life domains in the cue (bài tập nhóm, câu lạc bộ, đội thể thao, kênh riêng, dự án cá nhân, việc nhà, game) so a 15-year-old can find an instance. Where no instance exists, fall back to a situational version of the same question, as the ITviec article itself advises candidates to do.
- **Family responsibilities** are a legitimate source of behavioural evidence but touch family circumstances; the bank mentions "việc nhà" only as one optional domain among several and never asks about the family itself.
- **The culture-fit evidence backs D-02.** Fit judgements are where similarity bias enters hiring, and fit is two-sided and context-specific. A product for teenagers should therefore reflect preferences back ("bạn có vẻ thích…", with the quoted evidence) and let play test them, not rank occupations by "fit".

### Gaps
- Taylor & Small's (2002) meta-analytic comparison of situational and past-behaviour questions (*Journal of Occupational and Organizational Psychology*, 75, 277–294, DOI [10.1348/096317902320369712](https://doi.org/10.1348/096317902320369712)) and Culbertson, Weyhrauch & Huffcutt's (2017) direct comparison (*Human Resource Management Review*, 27, 167–177) were **located but not readable** (paywall; abstracts withheld); I do not report their numbers. The only verified statement on relative validity is OPM's.
- Campion et al. (1997), Sackett et al. (2022), O'Reilly et al. (1991) and Kristof-Brown et al. (2005) were not opened in full.
- No official, company-published interview question lists were found for Amazon or Netflix, and no Vietnamese IT employer's published culture-fit question set was found; the ITviec piece is one practitioner's account.
- No research was found on behavioural interviewing of school-age respondents.

## 5. Warm, disclosure-building question sets: what makes a question easy and pleasant to answer

### Takeaway
The repeatable features are: start light and escalate gradually, ask about something concrete and specific, keep the first answer small, make every answer acceptable, and give something back (reciprocity or acknowledgement). People also tend to underestimate how well deeper questions go, so the flow need not stay shallow, but depth should come late and stay optional.

### Cited Findings
- Aron et al. (1997, *Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin*, 23(4), 363–377, DOI [10.1177/0146167297234003](https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167297234003)): pairs of strangers spent 45 minutes on self-disclosure tasks that gradually escalate in intensity; this produced more closeness than matched small-talk tasks. The effect did not depend on pairs sharing attitudes, expecting to like each other, or being told that closeness was the goal. — [abstract as indexed](https://stafforini.com/works/aron-1997-experimental-generation-interpersonal/) **[abstract, via search-result summary]**
- Structure of the procedure as published for practice: three sets of twelve questions, about fifteen minutes each, each set more personal than the last; both people answer every question and take turns going first; set I is light (an imagined dinner guest, a "perfect" day), set III is the most personal. Later replications are mostly with university students; the page says more research is needed in other groups and cultures. — [Greater Good in Action, UC Berkeley](https://ggia.berkeley.edu/practice/36_questions_for_increasing_closeness) **[full; secondary to the paper]**
- Across ten experiments, people expected deep conversations with strangers to be more awkward and less enjoyable than they turned out to be, because they underestimated how much the other person would care; telling people about this bias made them choose deeper questions. — [Kardas, Kumar & Epley, *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, online 2021, as reported by PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/new-psychology-research-finds-deep-conversations-with-strangers-tend-to-go-better-than-people-expect) **[secondary: press summary]**
- The Proust Questionnaire descends from the Victorian "confession album" parlour game, in which friends wrote short answers to a fixed set of questions about tastes and values; Proust answered two such albums as a teenager and young man; *Vanity Fair* has printed celebrities' answers on its back page since 1993. — [Wikipedia, "Proust Questionnaire"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proust_Questionnaire) **[secondary]**
- Hinge requires three prompt answers to activate a profile, says it reviews its prompts and keeps those most likely to lead to a date, and requires users to like one specific photo or prompt because its research found that this produces better matches and more dates than liking a whole profile. — [Hinge, mission page](https://hinge.co/mission/) **[full; company self-report, no data published]**
- MCS phrasing devices: permission to give few or no answers; real or fictional figures both allowed; a small required answer (four or five words); the option to invent a saying. — [MCS workbook, pp. 5–9](https://www.marksavickas.com/files/1_Savickas_bio/Career%20Construction%20Theory/Publications/Books/My_Career_Story-2.pdf) **[full]**
- In the adolescent "I am…" study, the instruction was to answer quickly without thinking too much, within five minutes. — [BPS Research Digest on Hards et al., 2019](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/teenagers-define-themselves-mostly-terms-their-positive-traits) **[secondary]**

### Inferences
Phrasing features worth adopting (each is tied to at least one source above; the list as a whole is my synthesis):
1. **Light first, reflective last.** Order by intimacy, as in Aron's three sets; never open with a self-evaluation.
2. **Ask about a thing outside the self before asking about the self** (a show, a character, a story). The answer is easy and still diagnostic; this is the CCI's core move.
3. **One specific scene, not a general habit.** "Lần gần nhất…", "Kể một lần…" instead of "Bạn thường…"; borrowed from McAdams's scene rule and behavioural interviewing.
4. **Small first answer.** Three or four words, one or two sentences, three stems. A short box that can grow reads as less work than a large empty one.
5. **Every answer is acceptable, and the question says so.** "Chưa nghĩ tới cái nào cũng là một câu trả lời"; "việc nhỏ cũng được"; real or fictional.
6. **A cue that widens, not narrows.** Good cues are neutral sub-questions (where, when, with whom, what first) or balanced lists of *domains*; bad cues are sample answers or lists of traits, which hand the user the answer. This is the practical test for "mồi mà không gài".
7. **Specific prompts beat generic ones** ("Bạn bè hay nhờ bạn việc gì?" versus "Điểm mạnh của bạn là gì?"); consistent with Hinge's claim about specific prompts and with the trait-heavy, generic answers teenagers give to bare "I am…" stems.
8. **Give something back.** A human partner reciprocates in Aron's procedure; a product cannot share a life, but it can acknowledge with a one-line reflection of what the user wrote (MI's reflection and affirmation) before the next question. An AI "sharing" invented experiences of its own would be false and should be avoided.
9. **Light, playful frames are legitimate.** Confession albums and dating prompts show people enjoy answering short questions about taste; one or two such items make a good warm-up, but they give weak evidence and should carry little weight.

### Gaps
- Aron et al. and Kardas et al. were not read in full; neither involved adolescents, a single-user (non-reciprocal) setting, or text typed to software.
- No published, checkable data were found on which dating-app prompts work; Hinge's statements are self-reported.
- Evidence on how text-box size, wording of instructions and mobile typing affect open answers (Smyth et al. 2009 in the plan) was outside this scope and not checked here.
- I found no direct study of what makes a self-reflection question pleasant for Vietnamese teenagers; the feature list above is an inference to be tested in the team's own pilot.

## 6. Past-behaviour versus hypothetical versus preference questions

### Takeaway
For validity, anchor on past behaviour where the person has any instance and fall back to a concrete "what would you do" scenario where they do not; both forms are supported in structured interviewing, and pure preference questions ("do you like…?") are the weakest because they ask for the same self-theory a questionnaire would. For teenagers, the easiest questions to answer are small past scenes from school, hobbies and friendships, and quick stems; whole-life or abstract-preference questions are the hardest.

### Cited Findings
- Behavioural (past) questions rest on behavioural consistency; situational (hypothetical) questions rest on people's ability to project their behaviour; both are described as effective and both are built from critical incidents; behavioural questions show higher validity for highly complex jobs. — [OPM, "Structured Interviews"](https://www.opm.gov/policy-data-oversight/assessment-and-selection/other-assessment-methods/structured-interviews/) **[full]**
- Structured interviews, which use these two question types, had the highest reported mean operational validity (.42) among common selection methods in the 2022 re-analysis, with wide variation between settings. — [SIOP *TIP* summary of Sackett et al. (2022)](https://www.siop.org/tip-article/is-cognitive-ability-the-best-predictor-of-job-performance) **[secondary]**
- An earlier matched-question study reportedly found little correspondence between situational and behaviour-description questions written for the same characteristic, and weaker prediction from situational questions for higher-level jobs. — [Huffcutt et al. (2001), course outline copy](https://web.pdx.edu/~mccunee/quant_621/Outlines/Huffcutt%20et%20al%202001(2).doc) **[secondary: search-result summary]**
- Practitioner advice to candidates who have never met the situation: say so, then describe what you would do. — [ITviec Story Hub, 2025](https://itviec.com/story-hub/chia-se-ve-cultural-fit-screening-va-cach-tra-loi-cau-hoi-tinh-huong-nhat-quan-chan-that-3240) **[full]**
- The capacity to link events into a coherent life story (causal and thematic coherence) develops across adolescence. — [Habermas & Bluck (2000)](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.126.5.748) **[secondary: abstract summary]**
- Early adolescents completed MCS-based narrative questions in groups and rated the experience useful and satisfying. — [Santilli, Nota & Hartung (2019)](https://neomed.omeka.net/items/show/6391) **[abstract]**
- Teenagers aged 13–18 produced on average eight short self-descriptions in five minutes, mostly positive traits. — [BPS Research Digest on Hards et al., 2019](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/teenagers-define-themselves-mostly-terms-their-positive-traits) **[secondary]**
- Future-self writing has measurable effects on affect and optimism in adults and older students; no included study sampled minors. — [Carrillo et al. (2019)](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0222386) **[full]**

### Inferences
| Type | Information value | Ease for a 15-year-old | Use in JobQuest |
|---|---|---|---|
| Past behaviour, one small scene ("Kể một lần…") | Highest: concrete, quotable, harder to answer "for show" | Easy if the domain is school, hobby, friends; hard if it presumes a job | Main type; always list several neutral domains in the cue |
| Hypothetical with a concrete scene ("Lớp bạn tổ chức… bạn nhận phần nào?") | Good; reflects intentions and knowledge of self | Easy; needs no history | Fallback when no past instance; also the natural bridge to the game's own scenarios |
| Indirect or projective (admired figure, favourite story, media) | Moderate; needs interpretation, so the reader must stay tentative | Very easy and pleasant | Warm-up and self-concept; low weight per item |
| Sentence stems ("Mình là người…") | Low to moderate; mostly positive traits | Very easy and fast | One short block; pair with a behavioural item |
| Bare preference ("Bạn thích làm một mình hay nhóm?") | Lowest: a self-theory, same weakness the report criticises in self-report tests | Easy to answer, easy to answer for show | Only with a "kể một lần cho thấy điều đó" tail |
| Future ideal ("vài năm nữa, một ngày của bạn…") | A separate layer (the self one wants), not present traits | Moderate; younger teens may write vague answers | One item at the end; label as "bạn muốn thành" |

- The table is a synthesis: the validity ordering between past and hypothetical questions rests on OPM's statement and the structured-interview literature in hiring; **its extension to teenagers' self-reflection is my inference**, not a tested result.
- The game itself is the stronger "situational" instrument: every play choice is a response to a concrete scenario (the 97 events already emit signals on the eight dimensions). The onboarding should therefore lean on past-behaviour and indirect questions, which play cannot supply, and leave most "what would you do" probing to the scenarios.
- A cue that asks for the most recent instance ("lần gần nhất") reduces cherry-picking of a flattering instance and is easier to recall.

### Gaps
- No study was found that compares question types for validity or ease **in adolescents**, or in self-reflection outside selection.
- The two key meta-analytic and direct comparisons of situational versus past-behaviour questions (Taylor & Small 2002; Culbertson et al. 2017) could not be read; relative effect sizes are not reported here.
- No evidence was found on how well an LLM extracts work-style indicators from each question type; this needs the team's own check (for example, two readers versus the model on a small pilot set).

### Inferences
- **Coverage.** Every indicator family has at least three questions; each of the eight work-style dimensions has at least one question that targets it directly (NGAT: C1, C2; TAPTRUNG: B1, C2, C12; MOHO: B4, C3; TIMI: C4, C8; NGUOI: B1, C9, C10; KETQUA: B3, C5, D2; LAP: C6, C12; APLUC: C7, C8, C10).
- **A five-minute flow cannot cover all eight work-style dimensions by free text.** Narrative questions give strong evidence on two or three dimensions each and none on the rest. The dimensions left without evidence should be filled by play (the 97 events already emit signals) or, if the team wants them at onboarding, by a few quick two-option items, as the plan's question 8 anticipates.
- **Gender caution for N–V.** The plan flags a large gender difference on the people–things axis (Su, Rounds & Armstrong, not checked here). Questions B2, B3 and B5 list domains in mixed order and avoid gendered examples on purpose; the cue lists should be reviewed by the team for hidden stereotypes.
- **Cue format.** All cues are of the "widening" kind (neutral sub-questions or domain lists). Two stems (A6, C12, F5's cue) use the "câu bỏ lửng" form and one item (D3) uses "chọn rồi kể", so the team can compare the three formats named in the plan on real users.
- **Follow-up.** Only D1 scripts an AI follow-up (one laddering rung). If the team allows a follow-up elsewhere, it should be a reflection plus one neutral probe ("Bạn kể thêm một chút về lúc đó được không?"), never a why-chain.

### Gaps
- None of the 35 questions has been piloted. Wording was checked only by me for naturalness; a native-speaker read-through by two or three students aged 14–16 is the cheapest next step.
- Mapping from answers to indicators is proposed, not tested. Whether an LLM reads these answers reliably is unknown.
- I did not check the existing 14 forced-choice items on `feat/gtkm`, so overlaps with them are possible.

## 8. Deliverable: shortlist for a five-minute flow, and verdict on T1–T3

### Takeaway
Use nine questions, ordered from light to reflective: A1, A6, B1, B2, B5, C3, C11, D1, F1, with F3 as an optional tenth. Keep T1 almost as written, reword T2 to be gentler and anchored in one concrete day, and reword T3 on the Best Possible Self pattern while labelling it as the "bạn muốn thành" layer.

### Cited Findings
- Teenagers aged 13–18 produced about eight short self-statements each within five minutes. — [BPS Research Digest on Hards et al., 2019](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/teenagers-define-themselves-mostly-terms-their-positive-traits) **[secondary]**
- Shorter Best Possible Self practice was associated with effects at least as large as longer practice (marginal moderator). — [Carrillo et al. (2019)](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0222386) **[full]**
- The self-guided MCS consists of two opening prompts and four story questions. — [MCS workbook](https://www.marksavickas.com/files/1_Savickas_bio/Career%20Construction%20Theory/Publications/Books/My_Career_Story-2.pdf) **[full]**
- Disclosure procedures escalate gradually from light to personal. — [GGIA page on Aron et al.](https://ggia.berkeley.edu/practice/36_questions_for_increasing_closeness) **[full]**

#### Verdict on the teammate's T1–T3

| Draft | Verdict | What to change and why |
|---|---|---|
| **T1** · a time you lost track of time, alone or with others | **Keep, light rewording** (= B1) | Sound on every count: a single past scene, easy for someone with no job, quotable. Move "alone or with others" out of the question into the cue alongside "what" and "where", so the question does not pre-structure the answer around one dimension. |
| **T2** · what kind of work or environment drains you most and why | **Reword** (= C11) | "Công việc, môi trường" presumes work history, and "kiệt sức" invites distress disclosures from teenagers. | **T3** · five years from now, an ideal working day | **Reword** (= F1) | Follow the Best Possible Self instruction (everything has gone as well as it could) but ask for an ordinary day, which gives concrete detail on people, things, data and ideas. ### Inferences
- **Timing is an estimate, not a measurement.** Nine questions in five minutes allows about thirty seconds each, which fits one or two typed sentences per question plus the three stems in A6. If the pilot shows people writing more, cut to seven (drop B5 and C3) rather than hurrying the flow. The adolescent stem data make A6's timing plausible; the rest is untested.
- **Order follows the escalation principle:** outside-the-self first (A1), quick self-labels (A6), pleasant past scene (B1), light hypothetical (B2), strengths through others' eyes (B5), then the two questions that ask for a less comfortable scene (C3, C11), then values (D1) and the future (F1).
- **What the shortlist deliberately does not include:** the role-model, story and motto questions (A2–A4). They are the heart of the CCI and pleasant to answer, but each needs interpretation that is hard to verify by quotation; they fit better as an optional "kể thêm về bạn" second round than as inputs to indicators.
- **Skip must be free.** Because the flow is optional (D-29) and the questions are free text, each should be skippable without penalty, and the profile should say which indicators have no evidence yet rather than guessing.

### Gaps
- No pilot data: completion rate, time per question and answer length on a phone are unknown.
- Whether nine free-text questions give enough evidence to fill the chosen indicators is untested; a small trial (ten users, two human readers and the model) would answer it.
- The shortlist assumes the eight work-style dimensions and the two interest axes are the target set; if the team settles on RIASEC instead (plan section 7, question 1), B-group questions need re-mapping and A1, B2 and B3 carry most of the load.
